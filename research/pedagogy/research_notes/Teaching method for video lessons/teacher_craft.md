# Teacher craft: delivery moves that drive a concept in (board, voice, questioning, structure)

Scope note: this file catalogues *delivery moves* for a narrated lesson with a synced whiteboard. Each move has a
**Say** pattern, a **Board** action at that instant, and **Why** (with the evidence strength). Example lines use
forex/CAIIB topics; any fact inside an example line (e.g., value dates, FEMA s.2(n) wording) must still be checked
against the lesson's fact sheet before use. Coaching providers are described by style only; no material copied.

Evidence-strength key used below: **[R]** = research-backed (controlled studies / reviews), **[O]** = observational
research on effective teachers, **[P]** = practitioner craft / press description only.

---

## 1. Board / chalk-talk craft (planned layout, live construction, signalling)

### Takeaway
The strongest support is for *drawing and writing in sync with the spoken word* and *signalling where to look*
(Mayer's multimedia principles as applied by Khan Academy), plus the Japanese *bansho* idea that the board is planned
left-to-right and stays up as the record of the lesson. Celebrated lecturers (Lewin) look spontaneous but rehearse
heavily; evidence on specific layouts is mostly practitioner opinion, not controlled study.

### Cited Findings
- Khan Academy designs videos on Mayer's principles: **conversational narration** (personalization: "people learn
  better when narration is given in a conversational style, rather than a formal style"), **segmenting** (videos
  average 6–9 minutes, then practice), **text–narration matching** (on-screen text kept minimal; words written as they
  are spoken), and **signalling** (a moving circle or cross that follows the narration shows where to look) —
  [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-videos-are-made-to-help-you-learn/)
- Khan began by drawing in SmoothDraw on a Wacom tablet, recorded with screen capture; the drawing-while-talking
  format grew out of informal tutoring of his cousins — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-videos-are-made-to-help-you-learn/); [Labnol](https://labnol.org/internet/khan-academy-style-videos/19875)
- Japanese *bansho* (board writing): teachers write extensively, working **left to right**, on boards far larger
  than usual; students watch the reasoning built live and the record stays up for later reference (the board at the
  end is the lesson's record) — summarised in search results from [Nottingham primary education blog](https://blogs.nottingham.ac.uk/primaryeducationnetwork/?p=7486) (page itself not fetched; redirect)
- Carleton linguistics study of maths "chalk talk": "it is crucial to write on the board and say everything out
  loud", because learning maths is like learning a new language (spoken explanation paired with writing) —
  [Carleton TLS](https://carleton.ca/tls/2010/when-you-say-math-we-say-chalk-talk/)
- Mathematicians' view: board lectures are slower than slides, which gives the audience an "expanded short-term
  memory" (earlier lines stay visible) — [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/716362); Harvard's Knill on blackboards as the best tool to develop a thought — [Harvard Knill pedagogy page](https://abel.math.harvard.edu/~knill/pedagogy/blackboards/index.html)
- Practical layout advice (Poonen, conference talks): start in the upper-left; with sliding boards keep the previous
  board visible; erase completely so stray marks are not misread as notation — [Poonen, Berkeley](https://math.berkeley.edu/~poonen/papers/speaking.pdf)
- Walter Lewin: lectures looked informal but came from "weeks of exacting preparation and rehearsal"; dress rehearsal
  from 5:30 a.m. on lecture day — [CS Monitor](https://csmonitor.com/World/Making-a-difference/2011/0727/Walter-Lewin-is-hands-on-helping-students-grasp-physics); dry runs two weeks ahead and again on the morning — [The Tech](https://thetech.com/2011/06/03/lewin-v131-n27); demonstrations choreographed "down to the minute and millimeter" — [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2011/walter-lewin-lecture-book-0518). The often-quoted "rehearsed three times" figure is only from a forum post — unverified.
- Feynman: built from basics; conversational voice was deliberate (an editor's rewrite into textbook-speak was
  reverted); Feynman himself was "pessimistic" about how well the lectures reached average freshmen —
  [Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/lecture-notes-1.14290); [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/richard-p-feynman-teacher)
- Expert-teacher research: effective teachers **think aloud and model** steps, and give worked-out examples; they
  "anticipate students' errors and warn them" — [Rosenshine 2012, American Educator](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)

### Inferences (move catalogue: board)

**B1. Planned board, left-to-right story.** Before scripting, sketch the *final* board; then reveal it in the order
of the story (left = situation, middle = rule, right = numbers/traps).
- Say: "Let me lay out today's board. On the left, the customer's problem. In the middle, the rule. On the right,
  the numbers."
- Board: three faint column headings appear, empty.
- Why: bansho/Poonen layout; the final board becomes the recap (B7). [P/O]
- Forex: value dates board: left "Deal today", middle a timeline T, T+1, T+2, beyond; right "which rate?".

**B2. Write-as-you-say (text–narration match).** Only the key word goes on the board, written exactly as spoken, at
the moment it is spoken. Never a paragraph on screen while the voice says something else.
- Say: "Spot means delivery on the **second working day**." Board: "SPOT = T+2 (working days)" writes in sync with
  "second working day".
- Why: Mayer text–narration match / redundancy avoidance as used by Khan. [R]

**B3. Signal: circle, underline, arrow on the named thing.** Every time the voice names an object already on the
board, a mark lands on it (circle, underline, pointer glow).
- Say: "Look at this gap between today and the spot date." Board: bracket appears over T to T+2.
- Why: Mayer signalling principle (Khan Academy). [R]

**B4. Box the definition; colour-code by role.** A boxed item = "learn word for word". Keep one colour per role
across the course (e.g., rule = blue, trap = red, number = green, example = grey). Consistent colour is practitioner
craft, not tested here. [P]
- Forex: FEMA "foreign exchange" definition boxed, with its three "pockets" drawn as three trays inside the box.

**B5. Arrows carry causation.** Draw the arrow while saying "so" / "therefore".
- Say: "Importers rush to buy dollars, **so** dollar demand rises, **so** the rupee weakens." Board: arrow by arrow,
  see-saw tilts on the last "so".
- Why: makes the causal chain visible; Feynman/Lewin "why before what". [P]

**B6. Think-aloud on the board (model the expert's hidden steps).** Solve a numerical while narrating every decision,
including the check.
- Say: "First I ask: which date? Today is a Friday deal, so T+2 skips Saturday and Sunday… Tuesday. Let me check:
  is Tuesday a holiday in either centre?" Board: calendar, X over weekend, circle on Tuesday.
- Why: Rosenshine (think aloud, model, worked examples). [O/R]

**B7. The board at the end is the summary.** Never wipe the core board; the last 30 seconds zoom out to the whole
board/mind map and walk it once.
- Why: bansho "record stays up"; also serves as the retrieval scaffold for the close (Q-section 7). [P/O]

**B8. One rehearsed "demonstration" per lesson (Lewin move).** A single vivid, prepared visual experiment: e.g., the
see-saw that actually tips when exporters' dollar supply is added.
- Why: Lewin's demonstrations; must be choreographed exactly. [P]

### Gaps
- No controlled study found comparing specific board layouts (columns vs free-form) or colour schemes for learning.
- Bansho source page not fetched (redirect); description relies on search summary.
- No primary source found for *why* Khan videos show no face.

## 2. Questioning and engagement (Lemov, Rosenshine, Rowe, Hattie)

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced moves are: **ask many questions including process questions**, **check every learner's
response**, **wait time** (~3 s), **high success rate (~80%)**, and **choral / all-respond formats**. Lemov's
techniques are observational (from watching effective teachers), not individually tested. In a recorded video,
"cold call" and "wait time" become *scripted pauses with on-screen prompts* — the learner answers privately.

### Cited Findings
- Rosenshine's 10 principles include: daily review; new material in small steps with practice after each step;
  **ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all students**; provide models; guide practice;
  check understanding; obtain a high success rate; scaffolds; independent practice; weekly and monthly review —
  [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- In one maths study the most effective teachers spent ~23 minutes of a 40-minute period on lecture, demonstration,
  questioning and worked examples vs ~11 minutes for the least effective; the least effective asked **only nine
  questions** in 40 minutes and almost no process questions — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- Ways to check all students: tell the answer to a neighbour; summarise the main idea in one or two sentences;
  **choral responses** for new vocabulary or lists; think aloud — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- Optimal success rate about **80 percent** — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- Wait time (Rowe): pausing ~3 s after a question and again after the first answer; when average wait time passed
  ~2.7 s, response length rose 300–700% across studies (secondary briefing; verify against Rowe 1986, J. Teacher
  Education 37(1):43–50) — [Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/wait-time-three-seconds); [Wikipedia: Mary Budd Rowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Budd_Rowe)
- TLAC 3.0: Wait Time (33), Cold Call (34: teacher names who answers so everyone prepares), Call and Response (35),
  Means of Participation (36), Everybody Writes (38), Show Call (13), Exit Ticket (26), Format Matters (18), Right Is
  Right (16); techniques are observational, not individually tested; criticism that they can produce compliance
  rather than thinking (Cushing 2021; Golann 2015) — [Structural Learning TLAC guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques)
- No Opt Out is Technique 1 in the original TLAC; often paired with Cold Call (a student who can't answer must
  still end by giving the right answer) — search-result summaries of [TES](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/10-new-teaching-techniques-doug-lemov-wants-you-know) and [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/education/becoming-a-champion-teacher/2011/02)
- Hattie: teacher clarity d = 0.75; feedback reported 0.70–0.75 depending on edition; a later re-analysis of
  feedback found d = 0.48; Hattie's CLE values were miscalculated (d values stand) — [Hattie appendix (Corwin)](https://resources.corwin.com/sites/default/files/appendix_4.pdf); [VCU teacher clarity review](https://vtss-ric.vcu.edu/media/vtss-ric/documents/tier-1-forum/academic-tfi/2022/TeacherClarity_APotentYetMisunderstoodTeachingStrategy.pdf); [Wikipedia: Visible Learning](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Visible_Learning); [PMC feedback meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)
- Choral responding (all respond in unison) and response cards: increased active student response is functionally
  related to achievement; response-card sessions beat hand-raising on next-day quizzes (Narayan/Heward 1990;
  Gardner, Heward & Grossi 1994: all 22 students scored higher). Mostly small single-class studies — [PMC (Heward lab)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297777); [Kozloff, choral responding](https://people.uncw.edu/kozloffm/choral.pdf)

### Inferences (move catalogue: questioning, adapted to recorded video)

**Q1. Video Cold Call ("you, yes you").** Ask a specific question, name a persona or say "you, answer before I do",
then a visible countdown.
- Say: "Quick one for you. A deal on Monday, value Wednesday. Cash, tom or spot? Say it out loud." [pause]
- Board: question card + 3-dot timer; options greyed; after pause, "SPOT" lights.
- Why: Cold Call principle (everyone prepares an answer); Rowe wait time. [O/R]

**Q2. Scripted wait time (3 s after question, short beat after answer).** Write `[HOLD 3s]` into the script. The
voice never answers instantly.
- Why: Rowe ~2.7–3 s threshold. In video the learner's pause button is the "longer wait". [R]

**Q3. Call and Response / "say it with me".** For a list or a key term, prompt a spoken repetition.
- Say: "Cash — today. Tom — tomorrow. Spot — day two. Say it with me: cash, tom, spot." [HOLD 2s]
- Board: three tiles flash in rhythm with the words.
- Why: choral responding evidence (Heward) and Rosenshine (choral for lists/vocabulary). Best for short lists, not
  for reasoning. [R, small studies]

**Q4. No Opt Out, video form ("come back to it").** After revealing a commonly-missed answer, re-ask the same
question in a new form 1–2 minutes later so every viewer ends right.
- Say (later): "Earlier, Monday deal, Wednesday value was spot. Now: Thursday deal, spot date?" Board: calendar
  reappears; weekend greyed; "Monday" circles.
- Why: No Opt Out principle; also spaced retrieval. [O]

**Q5. Everybody Writes (pause-and-jot).** "Pause the video and write the three pockets of 'foreign exchange' before I
show them."
- Board: three empty trays with a pencil icon; then trays fill.
- Why: Everybody Writes; retrieval/generation; in a phone setting, "say it aloud" is a lighter alternative. [O]

**Q6. Process question, not only fact question.** "*Why* does the spot date skip Saturday?" "How did you get T+2?"
- Why: Rosenshine — less effective teachers ask almost no process questions. [O]

**Q7. Check for Understanding with a hinge question.** One multiple-choice item whose wrong options each map to a
known misconception; reveal the reason behind each wrong option.
- Forex: "A draft issued by a bank in London, payable in rupees — is it foreign exchange under FEMA? (a) no, it's in
  rupees (b) yes (c) only if over USD 10,000." Board: each wrong option gets a red tag naming the trap.
- Why: CFU / Culture of Error; Hattie feedback. Hinge-question naming is a practitioner term (not sourced here). [O/P]

**Q8. Show Call, video form (show a "student's" answer).** Show a plausible worked answer with one error; ask the
viewer to find it before the teacher circles it.
- Why: Show Call (TLAC 13). Use an invented learner, never a real person. [O]

**Q9. Right Is Right / Format Matters.** When the near-right answer is "two days", insist on "second **working** day"
— voice repeats the full phrase, board underlines "working".
- Why: TLAC 16/18; exam options often hinge on exactly this word. [O/P]

**Q10. Clarity statement up front (Hattie clarity).** "By the end you'll be able to name the value date of any deal
and say which rate applies." Board: learning-goal strip at top, ticked at the end.
- Why: teacher clarity d = 0.75 (Hattie; the construct includes clear goals and explanations). [R, meta-analytic, with caveats]

**Q11. Aim for ~80% success in in-video questions.** Calibrate check-ins so most viewers get most right; save
hard ones for the practice set.
- Why: Rosenshine ~80% success rate. [O]

### Gaps
- Full Lemov definitions for No Opt Out, Call and Response, Format Matters were not fetched from a primary source
  (book not accessible); descriptions above follow secondary summaries.
- No study found on wait time *in recorded video* (as opposed to live class); the adaptation is an inference.
- Randolph (2007) response-card meta-analysis effect sizes not retrieved.

## 3. How popular Indian exam coaches teach (CA, UPSC, mass-exam teachers)

### Takeaway
Public sources (press, coaching listings) consistently praise **conceptual clarity, simplified explanations,
everyday/relatable examples, exam orientation, humour and storytelling, and plain or vernacular language**. I found
no reliable public source that specifically documents mnemonic use, "write this down" signalling, or PYQ linking by
named teachers; those are widely observed habits but unsourced here. Sources are mostly promotional or light press.

### Cited Findings
- CA faculty listings: praise for "conceptual depth", "simplify complex accounting standards into logical and
  easy-to-understand concepts", "clear conceptual explanation, structured teaching style, and strong exam-oriented
  focus", "practical examples, real-world case studies, and lucid explanations" (promotional pages) —
  [Zeroinfy CA Parveen Sharma](https://zeroinfy.in/collections/ca-parveen-sharma); [Zeroinfy CA Aakash Kandoi](https://zeroinfy.in/collections/ca-aakash-kandoi); [CA Point on CA Shubham Singhal](https://capoint.in/blogs/blog/why-ca-shubham-singhal-is-becoming-a-top-choice-for-ca-law-students)
- Khan Sir (Patna): everyday examples, local idioms, humour, storytelling; plain language over academic register;
  maps and board turn GK/history/geography into relatable stories; theatrical delivery and concise explanations;
  teaches in Hindi — [EdisonOS](https://www.edisonos.com/blog/how-about-teaching-in-vernacular-like-khan-sir-from-patna); [Inventiva](https://www.inventiva.co.in/?p=439559); [Education21](https://education21.in/?p=15503)
- Vikas Divyakirti (UPSC): students cite relatable examples, concept clarity, and use of personal experience to
  explain complex topics (light press) — [Equentis](https://equentis.com/blog/?p=44933); [ScoopWhoop](https://www.scoopwhoop.com/amp/career/vikas-divyakirti-neeraj-arora-youtube-teachers-make-learning-easy/)
- General research: humour can raise interest, participation, attention and enjoyment and reduce anxiety —
  [European Journal of Humour Research](https://europeanjournalofhumour.org/ejhr/article/download/994/857)

### Inferences (move catalogue: exam-coaching moves, kept honest for an AI-voiced course)

**C1. Exam signal ("this is asked").** Flag high-yield lines with an evidence-based signal, not hype.
- Say: "Mark this one. IIBF has asked the spot-date question in several recent cycles." Board: small "EXAM" stamp
  beside the boxed rule. Use only where `importance_map.md` supports it; never claim "comes every time".
- Why: Indian coaching norm; our version is grounded in recalled items. [P]

**C2. "Write this down" / "underline this" cue.** Reserve for the one line per chapter that must be verbatim.
- Say: "Underline 'working day'. Not calendar day — working day." Board: underline draws under "working".
- Why: practitioner signalling; aligns with Mayer signalling. [P/R]

**C3. Everyday-life analogy before the rule (Khan Sir style).** "Spot is like a courier that always takes two working
days — book on Friday, it lands Tuesday."
- Board: courier van moves along calendar.
- Why: relatable examples praised across sources; concreteness fading (section 4). [P/R]

**C4. Previous-question link at the end of a chapter.** Show a question *in the style* of recalled items (our own
wording) right after the rule, then solve it.
- Why: exam orientation praised; practice testing has high utility (section 5). [P/R]

**C5. Light humour, one per chapter, never at the learner's expense.** A self-contained joke tied to the concept
("the rupee and the dollar on a see-saw — and the RBI is the uncle who sometimes sits on one end").
- Why: humour raises attention and lowers anxiety (general research). [R, general]

**C6. Energy and rhythm.** Theatrical delivery is part of the appeal of mass-exam teachers; for an AI voice this means
varied pace, emphasis tags on key words, and real pauses (see section 6). [P]

**Honesty constraint:** no "Sir" persona or personal back-story (project rule); the persona-based moves of human
coaches (personal anecdotes, "when I was in the bank…") are replaced by *branch situations* told in third person.

### Gaps
- No reliable public source on JAIIB/CAIIB/RBI Grade B teachers' delivery styles specifically.
- No public source found documenting mnemonic or PYQ-linking habits of named Indian faculty (common knowledge, but
  unsourced here).
- Sources are mostly promotional pages and light press; treat as indicative, not evidence of effectiveness.

## 4. Storytelling, analogies, misconception confrontation, concreteness fading

### Takeaway
Two strong findings: (1) **state and refute the misconception** — learners rate such videos as more confusing but
learn more than from "clear" expository videos (Muller); (2) **concreteness fading** — start concrete and fade
explicitly to the abstract rule (Fyfe et al. 2014 review).

### Cited Findings
- Muller (PhD, Sydney 2008): clear, concise expository science videos were not particularly effective and increased
  viewers' confidence in existing wrong ideas; videos that first stated then refuted the misconception were rated
  more confusing but produced more learning — [THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx); [Wikipedia: Derek Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Muller); [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2012/06/expert_gently_asks_whether_khan_academy_videos_promote_meaningful_learning.html)
- Concreteness fading: begin with concrete materials and "gradually and explicitly" fade toward abstract ones, rather
  than choosing one; benefits include linking abstract symbols to concrete referents and grounding abstract thinking
  — [Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone 2014 (Educ. Psych. Review), IU repository](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/f1ea5994-0d9f-4215-abec-152a03149277); [arXiv summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.14562)
- Effective teachers anticipate errors and warn about them — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)

### Inferences (move catalogue)

**S1. Story-first opening (branch situation).** Open with a customer at the counter whose problem the rule solves.
- Say: "Friday, 3 p.m. An exporter walks in: 'I've sold dollars today — when does the money hit my account?'"
- Board: counter sketch, question bubble, calendar with Friday circled.

**S2. Misconception confrontation ("some of you think X").** State the wrong idea explicitly, let it sit, then
break it with a case.
- Say: "Many of you think spot means 'on the spot — today'. Let's test that. Monday deal, spot date?… Wednesday.
  So 'spot' is *not* today." Board: "SPOT = TODAY?" written, then struck through in red; "T+2" written beside.
- Why: Muller refutation effect. Accept that it feels harder; don't soften the refutation away. [R]

**S3. Concreteness fading in three beats.** Concrete (courier/calendar picture) → bridge (same calendar with T, T+1,
T+2 labels) → abstract (rule card "Cash T, Tom T+1, Spot T+2, Forward > T+2").
- Board: the picture literally fades while labels remain. [R]
- Forex: see-saw of dollar demand/supply: real players on each end (importer, exporter) → arrows labelled D and S →
  supply–demand diagram / rule "more demand for USD → USD appreciates vs INR".

**S4. Analogy with a stated limit.** Choose an analogy that matches the *structure* (not just surface) and say where
it breaks. "The see-saw shows direction, but not *how much* — RBI intervention is outside the see-saw."
- Why: practitioner craft; prevents analogy-induced misconceptions. [P]

**S5. "Why before what" (Feynman/Lewin).** Give the purpose of a rule before its text: "Why would FEMA count a rupee
draft drawn by a London bank as foreign exchange? Because the money is ultimately claimed from abroad." Then the
boxed definition. [P]

### Gaps
- Muller's dissertation not read directly; findings via secondary coverage.
- No source found specifically on analogy *selection* criteria by expert teachers (structure-mapping literature not
  searched within budget).

## 5. Memory devices: mnemonics, chunking, choral repetition

### Takeaway
Use mnemonics sparingly and for **lists and arbitrary labels**, not for reasoning. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate the
keyword mnemonic **low utility** (narrow materials, short retention); **practice testing and distributed practice**
are high utility. Choral repetition helps short lists. Chunking (grouping into ≤3–4 units) follows small-steps advice.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky et al. 2013 (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*): low utility — summarisation, highlighting,
  keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading; keyword mnemonic "difficult to implement in some contexts" and
  benefits "a limited number of materials and … short retention intervals"; high utility — practice testing,
  distributed practice; moderate — elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice —
  [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654); [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/26173288/)
- Present material in small steps; effective teachers did not overwhelm; weekly and monthly review — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- Choral responding for vocabulary and lists — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf); [PMC (Heward)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297777)

### Inferences (move catalogue)

**M1. Number-and-name the chunk.** "Three pockets of foreign exchange. Pocket one…" Board: three numbered trays.
Keep lists to three or four where the source allows.

**M2. Acronym/hook only for genuinely arbitrary lists, then test it.** Build our own hook, show it once, then ask the
viewer to unpack it later in the lesson (retrieval does the real work).
- Say: "Cash, Tom, Spot — think 'C-T-S, zero-one-two'." Board: C T S over 0 1 2. Later: "What's the '2'?"
- Why: mnemonics give access; Dunlosky says durability comes from testing and spacing. [R]

**M3. Say-it-with-me for a 3-item list** (see Q3). Never for a multi-condition rule.

**M4. Elaborative "why" question instead of a mnemonic** for rules with a reason (e.g., "Why does a forward contract
exist at all?"). Why: elaborative interrogation rated moderate utility. [R]

**M5. Spaced callback across lessons.** Open the next lesson with two retrieval questions from this one. Why:
distributed practice + Rosenshine daily review (5–8 min in the classroom study; ~30–60 s fits our video length). [R/O]

### Gaps
- Peg systems and rhyme: no evidence retrieved within budget (Bellezza, Levin keyword reviews not fetched).
- No evidence retrieved on mnemonics specifically for adult professional learners.

## 6. Pacing and voice: pauses, slowing on key lines, repetition, signposting

### Takeaway
Evidence supports segmenting (short chapters), small steps, explicit pauses (wait time), and conversational tone.
"Repeat the key line 2–3 times", "slow down on the key line" and signposting are strong practitioner norms but I found
no controlled study on them within budget.

### Cited Findings
- Conversational narration beats formal; segment into short chunks (6–9 min average at Khan) — [Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-videos-are-made-to-help-you-learn/)
- Board lectures' slower pace extends short-term memory — [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/716362)
- Wait time ~3 s — [Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/wait-time-three-seconds)
- Feynman's informal voice preserved deliberately — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/lecture-notes-1.14290)

### Inferences (move catalogue: voice, for an ElevenLabs script)

**P1. Signpost the count.** "Three things decide the value date. Number one… Number two… And the third — the one
people miss — …" Board: 1-2-3 slots fill in turn.

**P2. The key line, three times, three ways.** Say it plainly → slower with emphasis → as a question back to the
learner. "Spot is the second working day. … The *second* … *working* … day. … So, Thursday deal — spot?"
Board: line written once, then underline on "second", then on "working". (Script: emphasis tags + `[HOLD]`.)

**P3. Pause before and after the key line.** ~1 s before (anticipation), ~2 s after (processing). Board is still
during the pause — no new animation competes with the line.

**P4. "The one thing to remember."** Once per chapter: "If you remember one thing from this chapter…" Board: the
boxed rule pulses.

**P5. Slow the pace for numbers.** Read amounts and dates in groups ("one crore, twenty lakh"); show calculator key
presses one by one in sync.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence retrieved on repetition count or speech rate for key lines in instructional video.

## 7. Closing the lesson: retrieval, exit ticket, rebuilding the board

### Takeaway
Close with **retrieval, not re-telling**: learners recall before seeing the summary (practice testing is high
utility), then the full board/mind map is shown as the check, then a short exit ticket.

### Cited Findings
- Practice testing: high utility — [Duke Scholars (Dunlosky 2013)](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654)
- Summarise the main idea in one or two sentences; weekly and monthly review — [Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)
- Exit Ticket is TLAC technique 26 — [Structural Learning TLAC guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques)
- Board record stays up as reference (bansho) — [Nottingham blog via search summary](https://blogs.nottingham.ac.uk/primaryeducationnetwork/?p=7486)

### Inferences (move catalogue)

**E1. Blank-map recall.** "Before I show the map, say the four branches out loud." Board: mind-map skeleton with
empty branches, `[HOLD 5s]`, then branches fill one by one.

**E2. One-sentence summary.** "Finish this sentence: 'The value date is…'" Board: sentence stem with blank; then the
model sentence.

**E3. Exit ticket (2–3 questions)** mixing one fact, one process ("why"), one application case; answers with the
reason for each wrong option. Forex: "Deal on 30 Dec, holiday on 1 Jan at the foreign centre — spot?"

**E4. Walk the final board once (B7).** Left-to-right, 20–30 s, voice naming each region as it glows.

**E5. Forward link.** "Next lesson: what if the customer wants delivery in three months? That's the forward."
Board: arrow off the right edge of the timeline.

### Gaps
- No study retrieved comparing teacher-led recap vs learner-retrieval close specifically in video lessons (inference
  from general testing-effect evidence).

### Cross-cutting summary for the script writer (inference)
A chapter template that uses the evidence: **Situation (S1) → misconception stated (S2) → concrete picture fading to
rule (S3, B2–B5) → key line ×3 with pauses (P2–P3) → cold-call check with 3 s hold (Q1–Q2) → worked example with
think-aloud (B6) → exam-style question (C4/Q7) → "one thing to remember" (P4)**; lesson close = blank-map recall
(E1) → final board walk (E4) → exit ticket (E3) → forward link (E5).
