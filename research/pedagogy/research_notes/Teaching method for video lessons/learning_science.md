# Learning science for narrated video lessons: what makes a concept stick (adult professionals, CAIIB)

Scope: evidence on durable learning and exam performance, graded strong / moderate / weak, with what it means for a
narrated lesson script (what the AI teacher says, asks, repeats, and when). Worked teacher lines use the forex example
"value date: cash = same day (T), tom = next working day (T+1), spot = second working day (T+2), forward = beyond spot".
Researched 2026-11-21. Numbers are as reported by the cited source; where a number could not be checked in the source,
it is listed under Gaps, not used.

Evidence grading used here: **Strong** = several meta-analyses or a large, replicated classroom literature;
**Moderate** = one meta-analysis or consistent lab work with some classroom support, or known boundary conditions;
**Weak** = single studies, mixed results, or mostly theory.

## 1. Retrieval practice / testing effect

### Takeaway
**Strong.** Making the learner pull an answer out of memory beats re-reading or re-hearing by about half a standard
deviation (g about 0.50 to 0.70), more so at longer delays, with correct-answer feedback, and with recall-type prompts.
In-lecture quizzes on video also cut mind-wandering roughly in half. Every chapter of a lesson should end in (and the
next lesson start with) answer-it-yourself questions, with a pause, then the answer.

### Cited Findings
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006): students who took repeated free-recall tests remembered 61% of a passage after one week vs
  40% for the repeated-study group; at 5 minutes the study-only group was ahead (81% vs 75%), i.e. re-study wins on the
  spot and loses later. The study-only group had read the passage about 14 times vs 3.4 for the test group —
  [WashU press release](https://source.washu.edu/2006/03/repeated-testtaking-better-for-retention-than-repeated-studying-research-shows/);
  [paper PDF](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)
- Rowland (2014) meta-analysis (61 studies): testing vs restudy g = 0.50; retention intervals over a day g = 0.69, under a day g = 0.41 —
  [review in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703); [Frontiers meta-analysis of ten techniques](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full)
- Rowland: initial **recall** tests give larger benefits than **recognition** tests; feedback is one of the reliable moderators — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6288371)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), *Review of Educational Research* 87(3): practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions; overall g = 0.70 (as reported by a 2023 review); same format practice/final test g = 0.63, different format g = 0.53; benefit significant for every test format —
  [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703); [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842)
- Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021), *Psychological Bulletin* 147(4): classroom quizzing, 222 studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.499 on achievement — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Feedback: testing with feedback beats testing without; giving the correct answer beats right/wrong-only feedback; adding long explanations beyond the correct answer generally gives no reliable extra gain; a second test adds benefit with diminishing returns; older adults benefited as much as college-age learners (single study) — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)
- Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013, *PNAS*): short memory tests interpolated in an online video lecture cut mind-wandering by about half, roughly tripled note-taking and improved final retention; a pause/re-study control without tests did not — [Harvard psychology](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-online); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/04/130404122240.htm)
- Interpolated quizzing in video lectures also improves calibration between predicted and actual performance — [Schacter lab review](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/09/stl-0000011.pdf)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing "high utility" (one of only two) — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/learning-techniques.html); [AFT summary](https://www.aft.org/ae/fall2013/dunlosky)

### Inferences
- A video cannot grade an answer, but it can force a *covert* retrieval: ask, pause 3–5 seconds with an on-screen
  "pause and say it", then give the correct answer (correct-answer feedback is what carries the effect).
- Prefer recall prompts ("What is the value date for tom?") over recognition ("Is tom T+1, yes or no?"). Use MCQ in the
  practice set because the IIBF exam is MCQ (same-format g 0.63 > different format 0.53), but in the narration lead with
  recall then show the options.
- Frequency: one check-in per chapter (every 3–6 minutes) fits the Szpunar design; a "retrieval opener" at the start of
  each lesson on the previous lesson gives the spaced, delayed retrieval that shows the larger g (0.69 at >1 day).
- Feedback line should be short: correct answer + one-clause reason, not a re-lecture.
- Teacher lines (forex):
  - "Before I show the next slide, say it aloud: a spot deal done on Monday settles when? … Wednesday. T plus two working days."
  - "Quick one from chapter 1. Cash, tom, spot. Which one settles the next working day? … Tom. Tomorrow. T plus one."

### Gaps
- Exact Adesope overall g could not be read in the original (PDF blocked); 0.70 is as reported by a 2023 review. Some
  coaching/teacher sites quote 0.61; not verified, do not use.
- No meta-analytic estimate found for *covert* (silent, unscored) retrieval in narrated video specifically; inference is
  from Szpunar-type interpolated tests, which required overt answers.
- Optimal delay of feedback in video (immediate vs delayed) is unresolved; lab hints favour delayed, no numbers found.

## 2. Pre-questions, prediction and pretesting

### Takeaway
**Moderate.** Asking a question before teaching the answer reliably helps learners remember *that answer*
(g = 0.54) but barely helps with material not asked about (g = 0.04). Wrong guesses do not hurt if the answer follows
soon. Use pre-questions as targeted spotlights on the testable points, not as general warm-ups.

### Cited Findings
- Carpenter & Toftness (2017): prequestions before short video segments (three 2.5-minute Easter Island clips) improved answers to the prequestions and to new questions; video may suit prequestions because learners cannot skip ahead — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/heres-simple-way-boost-your-learning-videos-prequestion); [Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/11/2-1)
- Toftness, Carpenter et al.: with longer, authentic lecture videos the effects were limited — [Royal Holloway](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/the-limited-effects-of-prequestions-on-learning-from-authentic-le/)
- 2023 meta-analysis (*Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): specific (prequestioned) effect g = 0.54 (k = 97); general (non-prequestioned) effect g = 0.04 (k = 91) — [doi 10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8)
- Classroom: pre-questions improved end-of-lecture quiz scores mainly for the prequestioned material — [CogSci 2020](https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0321/index.html)
- Benefit appears only for prequestioned information the learner can notice during the lecture — [APS 2019 poster](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/conventions/archive/2019annual/paper/25415/)
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009): unsuccessful retrieval attempts (guessing wrong first) enhanced later learning; "taking challenging tests instead of avoiding errors may be one key" — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/)
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009): pretesting on concepts in an essay improved learning across 5 experiments, even when pretest answers were wrong — [PDF](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751074/)
- Grimaldi & Karpicke (2012): the benefit did not appear when a delay separated the failed retrieval attempt from the study of the answer — [PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2012/2012_Grimaldi_Karpicke_MC.pdf)

### Inferences
- Script: 1–3 pre-questions per chapter, each targeting a point the exam asks, and the answer must come *in the same
  chapter, clearly flagged* ("here is the answer to the question I asked you").
- Do not overload openings with pre-questions hoping for general priming (general effect ~0).
- Make the answer noticeable when it arrives (glow, rule card) — the benefit depends on noticing it.
- Teacher lines:
  - "Here's a question for you to hold: if a customer books a deal today for settlement in 10 days, is that spot or forward? Guess now. We'll know in three minutes."
  - "Remember the question I asked you? Ten days out is beyond T plus two. So it's a forward."

### Gaps
- No study found on pre-questions with adult professionals for regulatory/financial content specifically.

## 3. Generation, elaboration, "why" questions, self-explanation; generative learning

### Takeaway
**Moderate.** Prompting learners to explain *why* a rule is so (elaborative interrogation) or to explain steps to
themselves (self-explanation) improves understanding; Dunlosky rated these moderate utility. Fiorella & Mayer list eight
generative strategies (summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting).
Works best when learners have enough prior knowledge to generate a sensible answer, which suits bank officers.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), *PSPI* 14(1): practice testing and distributed practice high utility; summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text and rereading low utility — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/learning-techniques.html); [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654); [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/resources/resource-library/improving-students-learning-effective-learning-techniques-promising)
- Bisra et al. (2018), *Educational Psychology Review* 30:703–725: meta-analysis of 64 studies, about 6,000 participants, prompted self-explanation produced better outcomes than instructor explanation or other uses of study time; most studies were on undergraduates — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); [TES](https://www.tes.com/magazine/article/your-students-should-explain-themselves-literally)
- Fiorella & Mayer (2016), "Eight ways to promote generative learning", *Ed Psych Review* 28(4):717–741, grounded in Wittrock's generative model and the select–organize–integrate framework — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9); [UMPI summary](https://www.umpi.edu/ctl/?p=5173)

### Inferences
- A video teacher can *prompt* generation even though the learner answers silently: "Why do you think…? Pause. Here's why."
- "Why" prompts suit rules that have a reason (spot = T+2 because historically two days were needed to confirm and move
  funds across time zones); avoid "why" for arbitrary conventions with no reason, where it wastes time.
- Mind-map building (mapping) and "teach it to a colleague" prompts are generative; the growing map in our kit is a
  natural fit if the learner is asked to predict the next branch before it appears.
- Teacher lines:
  - "Why would the market settle spot on the second working day and not the same day? Think of two banks in two time zones… Right: time to confirm and arrange funds in both centres."
  - "Explain this to the officer at the next desk in one line: what makes a deal 'forward'?"

### Gaps
- Bisra 2018 overall g (often quoted as 0.55) not verified in the search results; do not quote a number.
- Fiorella & Mayer effect sizes per strategy not retrieved.
- Elaborative interrogation meta-analytic effect size not retrieved (Dunlosky's "moderate" rating from memory of the paper; the APS/AFT pages confirm only the high- and low-utility lists).

## 4. Spacing, cumulative review and interleaving

### Takeaway
**Strong for spacing; moderate for interleaving.** Re-meeting material after a gap beats massing it; the best gap grows
with how long you need to remember (roughly 20–40% of a one-week retention interval, 5–10% of a one-year interval).
Interleaving (mixing similar categories) helps discrimination, g = 0.42 overall, most when categories look alike — and
it *hurts* for simple word lists. Our exam needs months-long retention, so reviews must recur across lessons, weeks and
revision videos, not only at chapter ends.

### Cited Findings
- Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), *Psychological Science*: >1,350 participants, gaps up to 3.5 months, final test up to a year; optimal gap ≈ 20–40% of a 1-week test delay falling to ≈ 5–10% of a 1-year delay; retention rises then falls as the gap grows — [USF](https://scholarcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1766); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- Follow-up (Cepeda et al. 2009): an optimal gap improved final recall by up to 150% in lab studies with delays to six months — [USF](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1764)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013): distributed practice rated high utility — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/learning-techniques.html)
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, interleaving g = 0.42; paintings/visual g = 0.67; maths g = 0.34; expository texts nonsignificant; **words: blocking better (g = −0.39)**; stronger when categories are similar to each other and items within a category vary, and for more complex material — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [manuscript](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Overlearning vs dropping an item after one correct answer: d = 0.75 (Driskell et al. 1992) — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)

### Inferences
- Within a lesson: chapter 2 opens with a 20–30 second recall of chapter 1; chapter 3 recalls 1 and 2 (cumulative).
- Across lessons: each lesson opens with 2–3 retrieval questions from the previous lesson and one from 2–4 lessons back;
  revision videos (the 4 in our plan) should be mostly mixed retrieval, not re-explanation.
- Interleave where confusion is the exam trap: cash/tom/spot/forward; usance vs sight; FCNR(B) vs NRE vs NRO; mixed
  "which one is it?" sets after each family is taught blocked first. Teach new terms blocked, then mix (words-list result).
- Teacher lines:
  - "Three lessons ago we met value dates. No peeking: Monday deal, tom — which day? … Tuesday."
  - "Now let me mix them up. Deal on Thursday, spot. Careful — Saturday and Sunday aren't working days. … Monday."

### Gaps
- Cepeda 2006 meta-analysis numbers not retrieved.
- No evidence found on spacing *within a single 15-minute video* (most studies space over days); in-lesson recaps are
  best treated as retrieval, with the real spacing done across lessons and revisions.

## 5. Repetition of key terms, summaries, previews and advance organisers; pretraining

### Takeaway
**Moderate (multimedia principles) / weak (exact exposure counts).** Pretraining (teach names and features of key
terms first) has a large median effect in Mayer's tests; signaling (cueing what matters) also helps. Learner-made
summarization is low utility per Dunlosky — so the *teacher's* recap should be a retrieval exercise (ask, pause, reveal
the map) rather than a passive re-telling. No reliable evidence gives a "magic number" of repetitions.

### Cited Findings
- Pretraining principle: supported in 7 of 7 tests, median effect size 0.92; segmenting (learner-paced segments) 3 of 3, median 0.98; modality (spoken words with graphics) 21 of 21, median 0.97 — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning/A9E77D0172F905AC957689D1771E2888); search summary of the chapter
- Mayer (2021 summary handout): signaling effect size 0.70; coherence (cut extraneous material) 0.86; redundancy (adding on-screen captions to narrated graphics) only 8 of 12 positive tests, 0.10 — [UNH handout](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)
- Summarization rated low utility (as a learner technique) — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/learning-techniques.html)
- Rosenshine: begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning; review weekly and monthly — [Rosenshine 2012 PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/58e151c946c3c418501c2f88/t/5c4b6a0fc74c50461d18f37c/1548446224187/Rosenshine+Principles+red.pdf)

### Inferences
- Start each lesson with a 20–40 second "names first" pretraining card for new terms (cash, tom, spot, forward,
  value date, T) before the explanation that uses them.
- Repeat the key term *in varied contexts* (definition, example, contrast, question, trap card, final map) rather than
  verbatim; aim for each core term to be met in roughly 4–6 distinct uses in the lesson — a design choice, not an
  evidence-based number.
- Do not put full narration text on screen (redundancy); show key words only (signaling).
- Teacher lines:
  - "Four words before we start: cash, tom, spot, forward. They're just the settlement dates, counted from today, T."
  - "Let's build the summary together. What goes on the first branch? … Cash. Same day. Now the next…"

### Gaps
- No primary evidence found on an optimal number of exposures to a term in an instructional video. Ausubel advance
  organiser meta-analytic effects not retrieved in this pass (older literature, small and inconsistent effects in
  memory; treat as weak).
- Personalization (conversational style) effect size not retrieved.

## 6. Concrete and worked examples, fading, analogies, misconception refutation, cognitive load

### Takeaway
**Strong (worked examples for novices), moderate (refutation texts, fading), moderate/theory (cognitive load).** Show
full worked numericals step by step, then fade (leave the last step for the learner, then the last two). As learners gain
expertise, full worked examples help less or even hurt (expertise reversal) — so later in the module switch to "try it,
then check". Naming and refuting a misconception beats simply stating the correct rule (g = 0.41).

### Cited Findings
- Expertise reversal: the relative effectiveness of instructional methods reverses as learner knowledge rises; guidance that helps novices can hinder experienced learners — [Springer, Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8); [Kalyuga overview, ASU PDF](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)
- Backward fading (omit the last step first, then the last two…) had positive effects in probability studies (Renkl, Atkinson et al.); adaptive fading did best on a delayed posttest in a classroom study — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9102-0); [Salden et al.](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)
- Schroeder & Kucera (2022), *Ed Psych Review*: refutation text vs other texts g = 0.41 across 44 comparisons (n = 3,869), consistent across contexts — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8784251)
- Danielson et al. (2024), *Educational Psychologist*, pre-registered: 76 studies, 294 effect sizes, consistent significant advantage of refutation texts; moderators did not change it — [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10629626)
- Coherence principle (remove extraneous material) 0.86 — [UNH handout](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)

### Inferences
- Numericals (forex, ALM, Basel): model one full worked example with calculator key presses; second example leave the
  last step ("you press the last key"); third example learner tries, pause, then show. Matches Rosenshine "models →
  guided practice → independent practice".
- Refutation structure for trap cards: (1) state the common wrong belief, (2) say plainly it is wrong, (3) give the
  correct rule, (4) give the reason. Our "some of you think X, others Y" device fits exactly.
- Cognitive load: one idea per picture, no decorative clutter, spoken (not printed) explanation of diagrams.
- Analogies: useful for structure (Gentner) but no numbers retrieved; use one well-mapped analogy, say where it breaks.
- Teacher lines:
  - "Many officers think spot means 'on the spot', today. It doesn't. Spot is T plus two. 'Cash' is the same-day one."
  - "Worked example: deal Friday, spot. Saturday, Sunday don't count. Monday is T plus one, Tuesday T plus two. Now you: deal Thursday, spot?"

### Gaps
- No effect sizes retrieved for the worked-example effect, Gentner's analogy work, or Kendeou's refutation mechanism
  papers in this pass. Sweller's CLT "germane load" concept is contested in recent theory; not verified here.

## 7. Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012)

### Takeaway
**Moderate–strong (synthesis of process–product classroom research, cognitive science and master-teacher practice).**
The ten principles map almost one-to-one onto a narrated lesson: daily review, small steps, many questions, models,
guided practice, check understanding, aim for ~80% success, scaffolds, independent practice, weekly/monthly review.

### Cited Findings
- Principles: begin with a short review of previous learning; present new material in small steps with practice after
  each; ask many questions; provide models; guide practice; check for understanding; obtain a high success rate;
  provide scaffolds; require independent practice; weekly and monthly review. Drawn from cognitive science, classroom
  research and master-teacher practice — [Rosenshine 2012 PDF (AFT reprint)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/58e151c946c3c418501c2f88/t/5c4b6a0fc74c50461d18f37c/1548446224187/Rosenshine+Principles+red.pdf); [Teacherhead](https://teacherhead.com/2018/06/10/exploring-barak-rosenshines-seminal-principles-of-instruction-why-it-is-the-must-read-for-all-teachers/comment-page-1/)
- Optimal success rate about 80% during guided practice (Rosenshine 2012, p. 17, as quoted) — [St Mary's summary](https://www.stmarys.ac.uk/partnerships/docs/research-and-literature-summaries/rl-summary-rosenshines-principles-of-instruction.pdf); [Victoria DET playbook](https://sites.google.com/education.vic.gov.au/oscplplaybook/osc-big-10/obtain-a-high-success-rate)

### Inferences
- In-video check-ins should be pitched so most learners get ~80% right: early check-ins easy (recall of what was just
  said), later ones harder (application to a new date). Questions that nearly everyone fails belong *before* teaching
  (pre-questions), not as checks.
- Teacher line: "Most of you will get this one. Deal on Tuesday, cash. … Tuesday itself. Good. Now a harder one."

### Gaps
- The 80% figure comes from older classroom observation research summarised by Rosenshine; no modern meta-analysis.

## 8. Desirable difficulties vs illusions of fluency

### Takeaway
**Strong for the phenomenon.** A smooth, confident explanation raises learners' *feeling* of learning but not their
actual learning; restudy beats testing at 5 minutes and loses at a week. A polished AI-voiced video is exactly the
format most at risk of this illusion, so the script must build in effortful moments (retrieval, prediction, mixed
problems) and tell learners why.

### Cited Findings
- Carpenter, Wilford, Kornell & Mullaney (2013), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*: a fluent vs disfluent instructor video changed perceived learning and instructor ratings but not actual learning — [Altmetric/DOI](https://springeropen.altmetric.com/details/1454460); [BPS digest](https://digest.bps.org.uk/2013/05/14/engaging-lecturers-can-breed-overconfidence/)
- Toftness, Carpenter et al. (2018), 31-minute video lecture: same pattern, higher perceived learning without better actual learning — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11409-017-9175-0)
- Restudy 81% vs testing 75% at 5 min, reversed at 1 week (40% vs 61%) — [WashU](https://source.washu.edu/2006/03/repeated-testtaking-better-for-retention-than-repeated-studying-research-shows/)
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork: challenging tests rather than error avoidance may be key — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/)
- Interpolated tests improve calibration of predicted vs actual performance — [Schacter lab](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/09/stl-0000011.pdf)

### Inferences
- Keep the voice warm and clear (fluency is not harmful to learning), but never let "it was clear" be the end: every
  chapter has at least one effortful retrieval.
- Say once, early in the course, why the pauses exist.
- Teacher line: "If this felt easy, good — but easy now doesn't mean remembered next month. So, without looking: tom is T plus…?"

### Gaps
- Bjork & Bjork primary reviews not fetched in this pass; the concept is well established but effect sizes are per technique (see sections 1, 2, 4).

## 9. Curiosity, emotion and story

### Takeaway
**Moderate (lab neuroscience, limited classroom evidence).** States of curiosity improve memory for the answer and
even for incidental material shown during the curious state, via dopaminergic midbrain–hippocampus circuits. A branch
"what would you do?" scene with the answer withheld opens a curiosity gap.

### Cited Findings
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014), *Neuron* 84(2): curiosity enhanced memory for target answers and for incidental material presented during curious states; effect tied to SN/VTA (midbrain) and hippocampus activity and their connectivity — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/500062)
- PACE framework (Gruber & Ranganath 2019): curiosity enhances encoding through attention, exploration and information seeking — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6891259)

### Inferences
- Open each chapter with a short real branch situation and an unanswered question; place the rule card right after the
  gap peaks (do not leave it open too long — compare pre-question timing).
- Teacher line: "A customer at your branch books a dollar sale on Friday and asks when the rupees hit his account. He's planning payroll on Monday. Will he make it? Hold that thought."

### Gaps
- Gruber 2014 used trivia questions with lab participants; no field evidence found for adult professional video lessons.
  No meta-analytic effect size found for story/narrative formats on retention.
