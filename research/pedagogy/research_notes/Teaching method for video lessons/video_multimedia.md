# Evidence on narrated, animated educational video (AI voice, animated notebook board, captions, no on-screen human)

Researched 2026-11-21. Evidence grades used below: **Strong** = many experiments plus meta-analysis, consistent;
**Moderate** = several experiments or one meta-analysis with caveats; **Weak** = single study, correlational data,
vendor study, or secondary report only. Most multimedia research is short lab lessons (a few minutes, college
students, immediate tests), so effects in a 12–15 minute exam-prep lesson for working bank officers are an inference.
Mayer himself notes this: "most of the supporting research involves short-term laboratory studies"
([Mayer 2023, APA Div. 2 chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).

## 1. Mayer's multimedia principles: which apply, how strong, and their boundary conditions

### Takeaway
Temporal contiguity (d 1.31), modality (1.00), personalization (1.00), coherence (0.86), spatial contiguity (0.82),
pre-training (0.78), voice (0.74), signaling (0.70), generative activity (0.71) and segmenting (0.67) are well
supported; redundancy is weak (d 0.10) and explicitly reverses for second-language learners, short text and slow or
learner-paced lessons. A static image of a speaker adds almost nothing (d 0.19), so "no on-screen human" costs little.

### Cited Findings
All test counts and median effect sizes below are Mayer's (2021, *Cambridge Handbook*, 3rd ed.) meta-analytic summary,
as reported in [Mayer 2023 chapter, Tables 2–4](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf):
- **Coherence** ("delete extraneous material"): 18 of 19 tests, d = 0.86. Strongest for low working-memory learners,
  **system-paced** lessons (a video is system-paced) and when the extraneous material is highly interesting. Strong.
- **Signaling** ("highlight essential material"): 26 of 28 tests, d = 0.70. Verbal signaling (outline, headings,
  underlining, **voice emphasis on key words**, Mautone & Mayer 2001) 15 of 16, d = 0.69; visual signaling
  (**arrows, flashing, spotlighting, pointing**) 11 of 12, d = 0.71. Strongest for low-knowledge learners, complex
  material, and "when visual and verbal signals are used **sparingly**". Strong.
- **Redundancy** ("don't add on-screen captions to narrated graphics"): only 8 of 12 tests, d = 0.10. "Diminished or
  even reversed when the lesson is slow-paced or under learner control, the on-screen text is **short or in a reworded
  form**, the words are in the learner's **second language**, or the material lacks graphics." Weak as a rule.
- **Spatial contiguity** (printed words next to the part of the graphic they describe, not at the bottom as a
  caption): 9 of 9, d = 0.82; strongest for low prior knowledge and complex lessons. Strong.
- **Temporal contiguity** (narration at the same time as the matching graphic, not before or after): 8 of 8,
  d = 1.31 — the largest effect in the set; strongest "when lessons are long rather than short and when the lesson is
  system-paced". Strong.
- **Segmenting** (learner-paced parts rather than a continuous unit): 7 of 7, d = 0.67 (e.g., lightning animation in
  16 ten-second segments with a "continue" button); stronger when material is complex and the lesson fast-paced.
- **Pre-training** (names and characteristics of key concepts first): 10 of 10, d = 0.78; may not apply to high-prior-
  knowledge learners, simple material, or slow/learner-paced lessons.
- **Modality** (spoken rather than printed words with graphics): 18 of 19, d = 1.00. Boundary: "printed words may be
  effective when the verbal material contains **technical terms**, is in the learner's **second language**, or when
  the lesson is learner-paced"; spoken words do worse when segments are too long to hold in working memory.
- **Personalization** (conversational, "your lungs" not "the lungs"): 13 of 15, d = 1.00 (also Ginns, Martin & Marsh
  2013 meta-analysis); "may not apply for experienced learners or **long lessons**".
- **Voice** (appealing human voice over machine voice): 6 of 7, d = 0.74, based largely on older synthesizers;
  "Craig and Schroder (2017, 2018) found that advances in modern text-to-speech engines can allow them to be more
  human-like". Schroeder & Adesope (2014): "voice may be critical, whereas the image of the agent is not."
- **Embodiment** (human-like gesture, gaze, movement of an on-screen agent): 16 of 17, d = 0.58; includes Fiorella &
  Mayer (2016): learning better when the instructor **draws on the board while lecturing** than when gesturing at
  already-drawn graphics; pointing gestures help (Davis 2018 meta-analysis).
- **Image** (static picture of speaker on screen): 4 of 7, d = 0.19, 3 tests negative.
- **Generative activity**: 37 of 44, d = 0.71; Fiorella & Mayer (2015): self-testing d = 0.57 (76 tests),
  self-explaining d = 0.61, mapping d = 0.62, summarizing d = 0.50, imagining d = 0.65, teaching d = 0.77. Works best
  when scaffolded and not too demanding.
- Applied test: redesigning a medical-school slide lecture by these principles improved immediate transfer d = 0.76 and
  delayed transfer d = 1.17 (Issa et al. 2013, cited in the same chapter).
- **Signaling meta-analyses beyond Mayer's lab:** Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016) found r = 0.17 [0.11, 0.22] for text–
  picture signaling on comprehension/transfer, moderated by prior knowledge (larger for low prior knowledge)
  ([search summary of Richter et al.](https://b2find.eudat.eu/dataset/edf0e3e7-9eee-56de-9e2f-904511bfe462));
  Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020) d = 0.38 ([WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)).
  Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018, *Educational Research Review* 23, 1–24) is reported as a strong effect on
  retention and moderate on transfer, with larger effects for university learners than school pupils (a German
  clearinghouse table gives retention/transfer g ≈ 0.60/0.32 for university students, column labels only partly
  legible) ([TUM Clearinghouse summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/CHU_KR-22_-Schneider_2017_Lernmaterial_Hervorhebungen.pdf);
  [Springer secondary citation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10339-020-01002-5)). Note the spread: Mayer's
  in-lab median (0.70) is far above independent meta-analyses (r 0.17 ≈ d 0.35; d 0.38). Plan for a small-to-moderate
  real effect.
- Segmenting meta-analysis: Rey et al. (2019, *Educ. Psychol. Rev.* 31, 389–419), 56 investigations, 88 comparisons;
  segmenting d = 0.35 transfer, 0.42 retention; learner pacing control d = 0.45 transfer, 0.19 (n.s.) retention. Breaks
  must fall at **natural idea boundaries**: mid-sentence breaks hurt (Biard et al. 2018)
  ([Castro-Alonso et al. 2021, Educ. Psychol. Rev.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870);
  [Rey et al. abstract](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting)).
- Dynamic vs static visuals: two meta-analyses (Berney & Bétrancourt 2016; Castro-Alonso et al. 2019) show only small
  advantages for dynamic visuals, and many comparisons were confounded ([Castro-Alonso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870)).

### Inferences (rules for the notebook lesson, forex examples)
- **Temporal contiguity is the single most important sync rule**: the graphic for a word appears *as* the word is
  spoken, never a sentence before or after. Our `sk()`/`B()` beat-per-sentence method is directly supported. Example:
  the value-date ladder's "T+2" rung draws in on the word "spot", not during the previous sentence on "cash".
- **Spatial contiguity**: labels sit on the drawing (the "2nd working day" tag written at the T+2 rung), never in a
  caption strip at the bottom of the board.
- **Signaling, sparingly**: one cue per key idea. Circle "2nd working day" exactly as the voice says "spot"; underline
  "Tom" on "tomorrow"; arrow from "Cash" to "T+0". If everything glows, nothing is signalled. Pair a visual cue with
  voice emphasis on the same word (both channels count as signals).
- **Coherence**: no decorative clip-art, stock photos of currency notes, or background music under dense explanation.
  Our ducked music bed is a coherence risk: keep it very low or absent during definitions and numericals (coherence is
  strongest for system-paced lessons and interesting-but-irrelevant extras).
- **Pre-training**: open a chapter with a 20–30 s "names first" card (e.g., Cash / Tom / Spot / Forward; "value date";
  "working day") before explaining why spot settles on T+2. Also fits the CLAUDE.md JAIIB-refresher rule.
- **Modality with a technical-term exception**: explain in voice; write on the board only short keywords, terms and
  numbers (technical terms and L2 learners are the documented exceptions where print helps).
- **Personalization**: "your branch", "you book a forward", "I'll show you". Caution: effect may fade in long lessons
  and with experienced learners — so keep the conversational style but don't pad with chatter.
- **No on-screen teacher needed**: a static avatar is near-zero value (d 0.19) and an un-animated agent can even hurt;
  spend effort on drawing and cueing instead.

### Gaps
- Could not open Schneider et al. (2018) full text; exact pooled g values and its moderator on dynamic vs static signals
  are unverified. Same for Richter et al. 2016 and Adesope & Nesbit 2012 moderator tables.
- van Gog's signaling chapter (*Cambridge Handbook*) not retrieved.

## 2. Video-specific findings: drawing, instructor presence, embedded questions, learner control

### Takeaway
Watching content being drawn as it is explained beats pointing at a finished diagram (d ≈ 0.54); interpolated
quizzes in a video lecture cut mind-wandering by about half and raise final test scores substantially; Khan-style
continuous drawing is more engaging than slides. These directly favour the "animated notebook" format.

### Cited Findings
- **Dynamic drawing**: Fiorella, Stull, Kuhlmann & Mayer (2019, *J. Educ. Psychol.* 111(7), 1162–1171): two experiments,
  kidney video lecture; learning is enhanced "by specific instructor presence features, such as instructor dynamic
  drawing and instructor eye contact, rather than by merely having the instructor visible"
  ([ECR Hub summary](https://ecrhub.org/publications/instructor-presence-video-lectures-role-dynamic-drawings-eye-contact-and-instructor)).
  Dynamic-drawing group beat static-drawing group on the posttest, d = .54 (reported in search summary of
  [Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S11423-020-09749-6)). Moderate.
- Mayer, Fiorella & Stull (2020, *ETR&D* 68, 837–852) "Five ways to increase the effectiveness of instructional video":
  **dynamic drawing, gaze guidance, generative activity, perspective, subtitle**, plus one way not to (seductive
  details) ([ECR Hub](https://ecrhub.org/publications/five-ways-increase-effectiveness-instructional-video);
  [DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S11423-020-09749-6)).
- **Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014, L@S)**, 6.9 million edX sessions, four courses; engagement = watch time and problem
  attempts, **not learning** — the authors state this proxy "cannot capture whether a watcher" actually learned
  ([paper PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)). Findings:
  - "Median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length"; students "often make it less than
    halfway through videos longer than 9 minutes". Recommendation: chunks "shorter than 6 minutes".
  - Khan-style tablet-drawing tutorials were watched 1.5x–2x as long as PowerPoint/code screencasts; 40% vs 31% of
    sessions followed by a problem attempt. Recommendation: "Introduce motion and continuous visual flow"; if slides must
    be shown, "add emphasis by sketching over the slides".
  - Pre-production planned specifically for online beat chopped classroom lectures (55% vs 41% problem attempts).
  - Tutorials were engaged with for only 2–3 minutes regardless of length; students re-watch and skim tutorials →
    "add support for rewatching and skimming".
  - Weak as evidence for learning (correlational engagement data), strong as evidence of viewing behaviour on MOOCs.
- **Interpolated tests — Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013, PNAS 110, 6313–6317)**: 21-min statistics video in four
  ~5.5-min segments; after each, six questions (20 s each). Exp. 2: mind-wandering on probes 19% (tested) vs 39%
  (restudy) vs 41% (not tested); notes on 24% vs 9% vs 7% of slides; final cumulative test 90% vs 76% vs 68%; tested
  learners reported **less** anxiety about the final test (median 2 vs 4 vs 3.5 on a 7-point scale) and lower mental
  demand ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)). Small samples (n = 32, 48) but large
  effects, replicated by Jing, Szpunar & Schacter 2016 ([Harvard summary](https://psychology.fas.harvard.edu/publications/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-online)). Moderate–strong.
- **Brame (2016, CBE—Life Sci. Educ. 15(4) es6)**: signaling, segmenting, weeding, matching modality; conversational
  style; enthusiasm; embedded questions (Szpunar 2013; Vural 2013 question-embedded video improved later assessment);
  **guiding questions** answered while watching raised later test scores (Lawson et al. 2006); learner control over
  movement through the video improved outcomes (Zhang et al. 2006) — chapters are one way to give it; videos tightly
  tied to the associated exercises helped most (MacHardy & Pardos 2015)
  ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)). Review, not new data.
- **Learner control**: pacing control d = 0.45 transfer (Rey et al. 2019); a scrollbar helped novices only, and only
  after they were taught to use it (Hatsidimitris & Kalyuga 2013) ([Castro-Alonso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870)).

### Inferences
- The notebook should **write itself on the beat** (hand-drawn stroke reveal of the T+2 rung, the "spot" label being
  written) rather than fade in finished diagrams — this is our analogue of the instructor drawing.
- "Gaze guidance" with no human on screen → use a pointer/pen tip or moving highlight that travels to the thing being
  named (a pointing cue; Davis 2018 supports pointing gestures).
- Put an **in-video check-in at the end of every chapter** (every ≤6 min): 2–4 retrieval questions, a visible
  countdown "pause now" prompt, then the answer with the reason. Example after the value-date chapter: "A deal is struck
  Monday; Wednesday is a holiday in New York. What is the spot date?" — learner pauses, then the board shows the
  ladder skipping Wednesday.
- Open each chapter with a **guiding question** ("By the end of this part you'll know why a spot deal doesn't settle
  today"); answer it explicitly at the end.
- Give **chapter markers** in the player and a contents card so learners can re-watch a chapter (supports Guo's
  "rewatching and skimming" for tutorial-type numericals).

### Gaps
- Gaze-guidance and perspective experiments' details not retrieved; their transfer to a voice-only, no-human video is
  untested.
- No study found comparing "animated hand-drawn notebook" vs "polished vector animation" with learning outcomes.

## 3. Speaking rate, pauses, emphasis and prosody

### Takeaway
There is no strong evidence for an optimal words-per-minute figure. Natural speech is ~150 wpm; comprehension holds to
roughly 1.5x–2x, so a measured 140–165 wpm voice is safe and leaves headroom for learners who play at 1.25–1.5x.
Voice emphasis on key words is a verified form of signaling. Pause evidence is mostly indirect (segmenting at idea
boundaries).

### Cited Findings
- Guo et al. 2014: speaking rates 48–254 wpm (mean 156, sd 31); within a length band, engagement rose (up to 2x) with
  speaking rate; mid-speed (145–165 wpm) videos were least engaging, apparently because those instructors were least
  energetic; "speaking rate is merely a surface feature" that correlates with enthusiasm; they could understand even
  254 wpm "since the same information was also presented visually"
  ([paper](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)). Weak
  for learning (engagement, correlational, native-English MOOC learners).
- Brame (2016) turns this into a suggestion of "185–254 words per minute" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)) —
  this over-reads correlational data; treat with caution.
- Murphy et al. (2021, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*): 231 students, 13–15 min lecture videos at 1x/1.5x/2x/2.5x;
  1x–2x scored similarly, 2.5x worse; one-week delayed test 24/40 (1x) vs 21 (1.5x and 2x) vs 20 (2.5x); speech
  normally ~150 wpm, comprehension declines near ~275 wpm; authors caution speeding may not work for complex material
  ([UCLA newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/releases/learning-while-speed-watching-class-videos);
  [paper PDF](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)).
  Horvath (Tes) puts the drop above 1.5x ([Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/article/does-speeding-videos-podcasts-or-reading-affect-learning)).
  Note the delayed-test numbers show a modest drop already at 1.5x. Moderate.
- Voice emphasis on key words, with outline and headings, is a validated verbal signal (Mautone & Mayer 2001; verbal
  signaling 15 of 16 tests, d = 0.69) ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).
- Segment breaks help only at natural idea boundaries; mid-sentence breaks hurt (Biard et al. 2018)
  ([Castro-Alonso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870)).
- Slow-speaking videos in Guo's data were instructors writing while talking — continuous drawing seemed to sustain
  engagement even at 48–130 wpm ([Guo et al.](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Target **~140–160 wpm** for the AI voice for Indian L2-English learners of technical banking content (slower end of
  normal; learners can speed up, and the evidence says up to 1.5x costs little; a slow base helps L2 and dense terms).
  Energy and warmth matter more than raw speed (Guo's mid-speed dip was an enthusiasm effect).
- Planned **holds of ~0.5–1.5 s after each key rule** and at idea boundaries (never mid-sentence) give the drawing time
  to finish and act as micro-segments in a system-paced video. Example: "Spot means settlement on the second working
  day." [hold while the circle closes around "2nd working day"] "Not the second calendar day."
- Use the voice to stress the one key word per sentence that the board also signals ("the **second working** day").
- Numericals: slow down and pause after each key press shown; these are where transient load is highest.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on the optimal *pause length* after key points, or on prosody/emphasis effects for TTS
  voices specifically.
- No speech-rate study found for L2-English adult learners watching technical videos; the 140–160 wpm figure is an
  inference.

## 4. Segment (chapter) length and whole-lesson length

### Takeaway
The "6 minutes" figure is a MOOC engagement median, not a learning threshold. Learning evidence supports
segmenting at idea boundaries with pauses or questions between segments; Szpunar used ~5.5-minute segments inside a
21-minute lecture with strong results. A 12–15 minute lesson in chapters under 6 minutes, each closed by retrieval
questions, is consistent with the evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Guo: median engagement ≤ 6 min regardless of length; drop-off before halfway for videos > 9 min; producers had
  set "< 6 min" from intuition before the data ([paper](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)).
  Brame relays that engagement near 100% for < 6 min fell to ~20% for 12–40 min videos ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)).
  These are free-MOOC viewers with no exam at stake; motivated exam candidates may differ.
- Guo also raised a confound: producers suspected shorter videos were better planned ("it takes meticulous planning to
  explain a concept succinctly") ([paper](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)).
- Szpunar et al. 2013: four ~5.5-min segments with tests between → 90% vs 68% final; without tests mind-wandering was
  ~40% of probes ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)).
- Murphy et al. 2021 used 13–15 min videos with acceptable learning at 1x ([UCLA](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/releases/learning-while-speed-watching-class-videos)).
- Segmenting meta-analysis: small-to-medium benefit (d 0.35–0.42), larger for complex material and fast pace
  ([Castro-Alonso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870); [Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).
- Personalization effects may fade in long lessons ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Keep chapters **3–6 minutes**, each one idea, ending with a check-in; the full lesson can be 12–15 minutes (as our
  spec says) because segment boundaries + retrieval are what the learning evidence supports, not total length.
- Numerical walk-throughs behave like Guo's "tutorials" (watched 2–3 min, re-watched): keep each worked example as its
  own short chapter with a marker so learners can jump back.
- A visible chapter title card with the map node it adds = a segment boundary *and* a signal.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that directly varies total lesson length (e.g., 8 vs 15 vs 25 min) while holding segmenting and
  questions constant.

## 5. Transient information effect and how to counter it

### Takeaway
Spoken words and animations vanish; when segments of narration are long or complex, the modality advantage
disappears or reverses (longer audio worse than equivalent written text). The countermeasures are short segments and
a persistent, cumulative written record on screen.

### Cited Findings
- Leahy & Sweller (2011, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 25(6), 943–951): spoken information and animation are
  transient; written text and static graphics are permanent ([cited in koreascience follow-up](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201612460477292.page);
  [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/)).
- Leahy & Sweller (2016, *Instructional Science*): "longer audio text information within a presentation was inferior
  to the equivalent longer visual text … a reversal of the modality effect"; "shorter, audio-visual information was
  better than visual only … but longer, audio-visual information was worse" ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9)).
- Wong, Leahy, Marcus & Sweller (2012): animation beat static graphics for **short** sections but not long ones; a
  later study with children found the reversal does not always occur ([LEAD summary](https://lead.ube.fr/?p=5776)).
- Mayer's modality boundary: spoken words lose their advantage when segments are "too large to be held in the learner's
  working memory" ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).
- Transient-information chapter exists in the *Cambridge Handbook* 3rd ed. ([Cambridge](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/transient-information-principle-in-multimedia-learning/670D96C3B9520320CE558AA855A49EE9)).

### Inferences
- **Cumulative board**: what was said stays on the page. Each key term, rule and number spoken is left written in
  short form (keyword + number) and the page builds up; the growing mind map is the lesson-level version.
  Example: after "spot = T+2 working days", the rung "SPOT — T+2 (working)" remains while the voice moves to "forward",
  so a learner who drifted can still read it.
- **Short spoken units**: one idea per sentence, ~15–25 words; rules stated in one sentence, then the example.
- **Numericals**: every intermediate result stays on screen in the working column with its calculator keys; never
  speak a number that is not written.
- Recap = the finished map (already in our spec) — also the permanent record that offsets transience.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly tests "cumulative board vs replacing slides" in narrated video; the rule is inferred from
  the transient-information mechanism.

## 6. Captions and on-screen text for second-language English learners

### Takeaway
For L2 learners the redundancy principle is explicitly reversed or weakened: same-language captions help listening
comprehension and vocabulary in L2 video (large effects in the language-learning literature), and Mayer lists
"second language" and "short or reworded text" as boundary conditions. Evidence for captions on content lectures for
L2 students is mixed-to-positive. Offer captions (default on or easy to switch on), and keep on-board text as
keywords, not a copy of the narration.

### Cited Findings
- Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet (2013, *System* 41, 720–739): meta-analysis of 18 studies; captioned
  video had large effects on L2 listening comprehension (g ≈ 0.99) and vocabulary (g ≈ 0.87); proficiency level did
  not moderate ([search summary; Cambridge reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/9B61BAEF14F110F01148E398D171634A/core-reader)).
  Strong for language learning; note the outcome is *language* comprehension, not domain content learning.
- Mayer: redundancy reversed when "the words are in the learner's second language" or text is short/reworded;
  modality: printed words may help with technical terms or L2 ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).
- Adesope & Nesbit (2012, *J. Educ. Psychol.* 104, 250–263) meta-analysis: spoken+written beat spoken-only; spoken+
  written did not differ from written-only ([WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842)).
  Moderate.
- Mayer, Fiorella & Stull (2020) list a **subtitle principle** among five ways to improve instructional video
  ([ECR Hub](https://ecrhub.org/publications/five-ways-increase-effectiveness-instructional-video)).
- Content-lecture studies for L2 students: South African non-native English students — no significant effect of
  subtitles on performance or perceived load ([Research in Learning Technology 2020](https://journal.alt.ac.uk/index.php/rlt/article/view/2340/2629));
  Kruger's work argues same-language subtitles have educational potential and that faster subtitles push attention to
  slides and visuals ([Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/the-impact-of-lecture-style-language-and-subtitles-on-the-process/));
  a 2026 eye-tracking study reports same-language English subtitles help long-term comprehension and that slides reduce
  load (ahead of print, abstract only, via search). Weak–moderate, mixed.
- Murphy/Tes commentary claims verbatim captions with sped-up video hurt memory — secondary, unverified
  ([Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/article/does-speeding-videos-podcasts-or-reading-affect-learning)).

### Inferences
- Provide **closed captions (viewer-switchable)**, accurate to the spoken text (our `sentence_sync` word timings can
  generate them). For Indian officers using English as L2 on a phone, often in noisy settings, captions are on balance
  helpful; let the viewer turn them off.
- Keep captions **out of the drawing area** (a bottom band) and keep **on-board text short and reworded** (keywords,
  numbers) — not a second copy of the narration, which is where redundancy costs are documented.
- Technical terms (Tom, value date, nostro, LIBOR/ARR names) should appear written on the board as they are first
  spoken — supported by both the L2 and the technical-terms boundary conditions.
- Burned-in subtitles are not needed; platform captions avoid fixing text into the master.

### Gaps
- Lee & Mayer (2018), the study behind the "subtitle principle", was not retrieved; its exact population and effect
  size are unverified here.
- No study found on Indian bank officers or Indian-English L2 adults specifically.

## 7. Voice: AI/TTS voice vs human, and conversational style

### Takeaway
The old "voice principle" (human beats machine, d 0.74) was built on robotic synthesizers; with modern TTS, learning
outcomes are generally similar to a human voice, though learners may rate human narration as more engaging. Voice
quality and naturalness matter more than the human/AI label. Conversational "you/I" style is one of the strongest
principles (d 1.00).

### Cited Findings
- Voice principle 6 of 7 tests, d = 0.74 (Mayer, Sobko & Mautone 2003 used a machine-synthesized voice); Mayer now
  notes modern TTS "can allow them to be more human-like"; possible boundary: no voice benefit when other social cues
  are negative ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).
- Craig & Schroeder (2017, *Computers & Education* 114, 193–205): a modern TTS engine produced more transfer and greater
  efficiency than an old engine and was rated on par with a human voice; "call into question … the voice effect"
  (Craig & Schroeder 2019, *J. Educ. Comput. Res.*: modern TTS not different from human on most measures)
  ([summary via ReadSpeaker — vendor](https://www.readspeaker.com/blog/elearning-voice); [Springer 2022 review](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10639-022-11255-6)). Moderate.
- Recent AI-video studies: no significant differences between AI-generated and recorded instructional videos in
  satisfaction, motivation, trust or cognitive load; Netland et al. (2025) comparable learning, but human videos
  perceived as more engaging/personal ([Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-025-13654-x);
  [arXiv 2511.20660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.20660v1)). Evidence base described as sparse ([arXiv 2607.12235](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.12235)). Weak–moderate.
- TechSmith vendor study (768 workers): high-quality AI voice rated professional by 92% vs high-quality human 87%,
  low-quality human 73% ([TechSmith](https://techsmith.com/blog/ai-voices-avatars-in-training-videos)). Weak (vendor,
  perception only).
- Personalization 13 of 15 tests, d = 1.00 ("your lungs" vs "the lungs"); fades for experts and long lessons
  ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)).
- Guo: enthusiasm/energy, not speed per se, tracked engagement; "coach instructors to bring out their enthusiasm"
  ([paper](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)).

### Inferences
- The ElevenLabs voice choice is consistent with the evidence, provided it sounds natural and warm: remove hiss
  (enhancement step), avoid flat or mis-stressed readings; re-generate lines with wrong stress on key terms. Voice quality
  is the thing the evidence says matters.
- Disclosing "the voice is AI-generated" (a hard line here) is not shown to harm learning in the studies found; trust
  and satisfaction did not differ in the AI-video study.
- Script in conversational first and second person: "Suppose you're at the forex desk and a customer asks you…", "I'll
  circle the date for you", "you might think T+2 means two calendar days — it doesn't". Keep it purposeful (personalization
  wanes in long lessons): warmth in framing, crispness in rules.
- Since there is no embodied agent, carry social presence through voice and direct address, not an avatar (image d 0.19;
  low-embodiment agents can cancel voice benefits).

### Gaps
- No study found on Indian-English listeners rating Indian-accented TTS, or on the effect of the "AI voice" disclosure on
  learning.
- Craig & Schroeder papers were not read in full; details come from abstracts and a vendor blog.

## Synthesis: concrete rules (a)–(d) for an AI-voiced animated notebook lesson

### Takeaway
Draw-as-you-say, one sparing cue per key word, a cumulative board, 3–6 minute chapters with retrieval check-ins, a
natural ~140–160 wpm conversational voice, and switchable captions are the evidence-backed core.

### Cited Findings
- Temporal contiguity d 1.31; spatial contiguity 0.82; signaling 0.70 (in-lab) / ~0.35–0.38 (independent meta-analyses);
  coherence 0.86; pre-training 0.78; personalization 1.00; generative activity 0.71; segmenting 0.35–0.67
  ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf);
  [Castro-Alonso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870);
  [Alpizar et al. 2020](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)).
- Dynamic drawing d .54 ([Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S11423-020-09749-6));
  interpolated tests 90% vs 68% ([Szpunar et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)).

### Inferences
**(a) Narration–visual synchronisation**
1. Every visual appears on the word it illustrates (±0.3 s), never a sentence early or late. "Spot" → T+2 rung draws.
2. Labels are written on/next to their object, not in a caption strip.
3. Don't show a full paragraph the voice is reading; board text = keyword + number ("SPOT = T+2 working days").
4. Each new term is written as it is first spoken (L2 + technical-term exception to modality).
5. Numbers spoken = numbers written, with calculator keys, and they stay.

**(b) Board moves that direct attention**
1. One cue per key idea: circle, underline, arrow, glow or pointer — on the stressed word. Circle "2nd working day" as
   the voice says "spot"; arrow "Cash → T+0" on "today".
2. Draw rather than reveal finished diagrams (dynamic drawing); stroke-by-stroke for the key diagram, crossfade for
   housekeeping.
3. Dim/grey what is not being discussed rather than clearing it (keeps the cumulative record; signals focus).
4. Chapter headings and the growing map node are structural signals; show an outline card at the start.
5. Remove decoration that is not part of the idea (coherence); keep music very low or silent under rules and numericals.

**(c) Pacing, segments, in-video questions**
1. Chapters 3–6 min, one idea each, broken at idea boundaries; lesson 12–15 min total.
2. Pre-training card of key names at the start of a chapter that introduces several new terms.
3. Guiding question at chapter start; 2–4 retrieval questions at chapter end with a "pause now" prompt (3–5 s
   countdown), then answer + reason. E.g., "Deal on Friday, Monday is a US holiday — what's the spot date?"
4. Planned holds (~0.5–1.5 s) after each key rule and after each numerical step; never break mid-sentence.
5. Chapter markers for re-watching numericals; end with the finished map as recap.

**(d) Voice and conversational style**
1. Natural, warm, energetic modern TTS; fix hiss and wrong stress (quality beats the human/AI label).
2. ~140–160 wpm base for L2 learners (they can speed up to 1.25–1.5x with little loss).
3. "You/I/we" direct address; branch situations ("your customer wants to remit USD 10,000 today…").
4. Emphasise the one key word per sentence that the board also cues.
5. Switchable accurate captions; disclose the AI voice as required.

### Gaps
- All rules are extrapolated from short lab lessons and MOOC data; none was tested on Indian bank officers or 12–15 min
  exam-prep videos. Layer 8 (module exam test) and post-launch recall checks are the only local validation available.
