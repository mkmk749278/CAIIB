# Make every lesson force the recall

A concept "sticks" not because the teacher says it clearly, but because the learner is made to **notice it, organise it, and then pull it back out of memory** several times, with feedback, across the lesson and across later lessons. The strongest evidence in learning science backs exactly the moves the owner asked about, with one twist: circling, underlining and highlighting work when the *teacher* uses them sparingly, in sync with the spoken word (signaling, temporal contiguity), and "saying the key word again and again" works when each repeat makes the learner *do* something (answer, predict, contrast, fill a blank), not when the voice simply says it again. Retrieval practice with correct-answer feedback is the single best-supported technique (g about 0.50 to 0.70 across meta-analyses), and short quizzes placed between segments of a video lecture cut mind-wandering roughly in half and lifted a final test from 68% to 90% in the best-known study. The danger specific to our product is the **illusion of fluency**: a polished AI voice and clean animation raise how much learners *feel* they learned without raising what they actually learned, so a beautiful lesson with no effortful moments produces "I understood everything" followed by a failed exam. The recommended rewrite therefore changes the lesson's skeleton more than its tone: longer lessons (about 16–22 minutes) built from 3–6 minute chapters, each opening with a question, teaching one idea on a cumulative board drawn word-by-word, refuting the classic wrong belief, and closing with a pause-and-answer check; every lesson opening with retrieval from earlier lessons and closing with a blank-map recall rather than a narrated summary. Most of the evidence comes from short lab lessons with college students, so the rules below are well-grounded inferences for Indian bank officers, not tested facts; our own module exam test (layer 8) is the local check.

## Concepts enter through attention and leave through retrieval

The owner's phrase "inject the concept into the brain" maps onto a well-studied sequence. Fiorella and Mayer frame learning as **select, organise, integrate**: the learner must pick out the relevant words and pictures, arrange them into a coherent structure, and connect that structure to what they already know ([Fiorella & Mayer 2016, Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9)). A narrated video can strongly help the first two steps, through what is drawn, where the eye is sent, and how the board is laid out, but the third step and above all durable memory depend on the learner doing mental work. That is why simply saying a rule, however beautifully, is weak: re-hearing is re-reading by ear, and Dunlosky's landmark review rated rereading, learner highlighting and passive summarising as **low utility**, while practice testing and distributed practice were the only two techniques rated **high utility** ([APS release on Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/learning-techniques.html); [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654)).

The retrieval evidence is unusually solid. In Roediger and Karpicke's classic study, students who repeatedly *re-studied* a passage did better at five minutes (81% vs 75%) but **remembered 40% after a week, against 61%** for students who repeatedly *tested themselves*, even though the re-study group had read the passage about 14 times versus 3.4 ([WashU](https://source.washu.edu/2006/03/repeated-testtaking-better-for-retention-than-repeated-studying-research-shows/); [paper PDF](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)). Rowland's meta-analysis of 61 studies put testing over restudy at **g = 0.50**, rising to **0.69 when the retention interval exceeds a day**, with recall prompts beating recognition prompts and feedback as a reliable booster ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)). A 2021 classroom meta-analysis (222 studies, 48,478 students) found **g = 0.499** on achievement ([HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)). The feedback that carries the effect is the **correct answer**; long re-explanations after it add little ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)).

For video specifically, Szpunar, Khan and Schacter split a 21-minute statistics lecture into four segments of about 5.5 minutes. Learners quizzed after each segment mind-wandered on **19% of probes versus 39–41%**, took notes on 24% of slides versus 7–9%, scored **90% on the final cumulative test versus 76% (restudy) and 68% (no test)**, and reported *less* anxiety about the final test. Restudying the same question-answer pairs did not help; answering did ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699)). One honest gap: those learners wrote answers. Whether a voice prompt to "pause and say it" gives the same benefit as a real quiz is untested in what our researchers found, so our videos should push learners toward a real answer (say it aloud, write it, tap it in the practice set) rather than a vague "think about it".

Two companion findings shape *when* the questions go. Asking a question **before** teaching the answer improves memory for that specific answer (g = 0.54) but barely helps untargeted material (g = 0.04), so pre-questions are spotlights on testable points, not general warm-ups ([Psychonomic Bulletin & Review meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8)); wrong guesses do no harm if the answer follows soon, but the benefit vanished when a delay separated the failed attempt from the answer ([Kornell, Hays & Bjork](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/); [Grimaldi & Karpicke](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2012/2012_Grimaldi_Karpicke_MC.pdf)). And **spacing** matters for a May/June exam months away: Cepeda and colleagues (over 1,350 participants) found the best gap between study sessions is about 20–40% of a one-week retention interval, falling to 5–10% of a one-year one ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)). Our notes found no evidence on spacing *inside* one 15-minute video; in-lesson recaps should be treated as retrieval, and real spacing done across lessons and revision videos.

## Circling, underlining and drawing work only when timed to the word

The owner's instinct about highlighting and lining things on the board is right, with a distinction that matters. Dunlosky's "highlighting is low utility" refers to *learners* highlighting their own text. *Teacher* cueing is a different thing, called **signaling**, and Mayer's summary finds it helped in 26 of 28 tests (median d = 0.70), both verbal cues such as **voice emphasis on key words** (d = 0.69) and visual cues such as **arrows, flashing, spotlighting and pointing** (d = 0.71), strongest for low-knowledge learners and complex material and explicitly "when visual and verbal signals are used sparingly" ([Mayer 2023 chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)). Independent meta-analyses find a smaller real effect, around d = 0.35–0.38 ([Alpizar, Adesope & Wong 2020](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)), so plan for small-to-moderate gains, and remember the corollary: if everything glows, nothing is signalled.

The biggest single effect in the multimedia set is **temporal contiguity**: narration and the matching graphic at the same moment, not a sentence apart, helped in 8 of 8 tests with **d = 1.31**, strongest in long, system-paced lessons, which describes our videos exactly ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)). Labels placed on the drawing rather than in a strip below it (spatial contiguity, d = 0.82), stripping decoration (coherence, d = 0.86) and teaching key names first (pre-training, d = 0.78) round out the strongly supported core. **Drawing live** beats pointing at a finished diagram: in Fiorella and colleagues' video-lecture experiments the dynamic-drawing group outscored the static-drawing group (d ≈ 0.54), and learning was enhanced "by specific instructor presence features, such as instructor dynamic drawing... rather than by merely having the instructor visible" ([ECR Hub summary](https://ecrhub.org/publications/instructor-presence-video-lectures-role-dynamic-drawings-eye-contact-and-instructor); [Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S11423-020-09749-6)). A static picture of a speaker adds almost nothing (d = 0.19), so our no-human, animated-notebook format loses little; the pen tip or travelling highlight can stand in for the pointing gesture ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)). Viewing data agree: on edX, Khan-style tablet drawing was watched 1.5–2 times as long as slide screencasts, and the authors advise "if slides must be shown, add emphasis by sketching over the slides" ([Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)), though that is engagement data, not learning data.

A narrated video has a hidden weakness: speech vanishes. Leahy and Sweller found that **longer** spoken passages did worse than the same text in writing, a reversal of the usual advantage of voice over print, while short audio-visual units did better ([Leahy & Sweller 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-015-9362-9)). The cure is a **cumulative board**: every key term, rule and number said aloud stays written in short form, and the page builds up through the chapter, which is also what the Japanese *bansho* tradition and mathematicians' preference for blackboards describe as giving the audience an "expanded short-term memory" ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/716362)). This makes the final board a natural recap, and the growing mind map in our kit the lesson-level version of the same idea.

Captions deserve their own note because many of our learners hear English as a second language. Mayer's "don't add captions to narration" (redundancy) rule is the weakest in the set (8 of 12 tests, d = 0.10) and he lists the learner's **second language**, short or reworded text, and technical terms as conditions where it weakens or reverses ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)). Captioned video had large effects on L2 listening comprehension (g ≈ 0.99) in a meta-analysis of 18 studies ([Montero Perez et al. 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/9B61BAEF14F110F01148E398D171634A/core-reader)), though for content lectures the evidence is mixed ([Research in Learning Technology 2020](https://journal.alt.ac.uk/index.php/rlt/article/view/2340/2629)). The right reading: switchable captions in a bottom band, and on-board text kept to keywords and numbers rather than a copy of the narration, with every new technical term ("tom", "value date", "nostro") written the moment it is first spoken.

## Fluent voices breed false confidence, so build in effort and refutation

Our lessons are polished by design, which is precisely the risk. Carpenter and colleagues showed students a fluent instructor (upright, eye contact, no notes) or a disfluent one delivering the same content; the fluent one produced **higher judgments of learning and higher ratings but no more actual learning** ([Carpenter et al. 2013](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/instructor-fluency-leads-to-higher-confidence-in-learning-but-not/)), a pattern replicated with a 31-minute lecture ([Toftness et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11409-017-9175-0)). The mechanism is what Koriat and Bjork call foresight bias: learners judge their learning while the answer is in front of them, but the exam asks for it when it is absent, which "can instill a sense of competence during learning that proves unwarranted during testing" ([Koriat & Bjork](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Koriat_Bjork_2006_JEP-LMC.pdf)). Learners also hold wrong beliefs about method, preferring massing and rereading even after doing better with spacing ([Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_Dunlosky_Kornell_2012.pdf)). This bears directly on the owner's goal of "I fully understood this, I don't need another tutor": that feeling is only worth creating if it is **accurate**, and the only reliable way to make it accurate is to let learners test themselves inside the lesson, which also improves calibration between predicted and actual performance ([Schacter lab review](https://sites.harvard.edu/schacter-memory/files/2022/09/stl-0000011.pdf)). A learner who answered eight pause-questions correctly has earned their confidence; one who merely enjoyed the voice has not.

The voice itself is not the problem. The old "human voice beats machine voice" finding (d = 0.74) was built on robotic synthesizers, and Mayer now notes modern text-to-speech "can allow them to be more human-like" ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)); recent comparisons found no significant differences in satisfaction, trust or cognitive load between AI-generated and recorded instructional videos, though human videos felt more personal ([Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-025-13654-x)). The evidence says voice *quality* matters, so hiss removal and re-generating mis-stressed key terms are learning measures, not cosmetics. **Conversational style** ("your branch", "I'll circle it for you") is one of the strongest principles (13 of 15 tests, d = 1.00), with the caveat that it may fade in long lessons and with experienced learners ([Mayer 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf)), so warmth belongs in the framing and crispness in the rules.

The second deliberate source of effort is **refutation**. Derek Muller's doctoral work found that clear, concise expository science videos left viewers *more* confident in their existing wrong ideas, while videos that stated the misconception and then refuted it were rated more confusing yet produced more learning ([THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx)). Meta-analyses back the pattern: refutation texts beat other texts at g = 0.41 across 44 comparisons ([Schroeder & Kucera 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8784251)), and a pre-registered review of 76 studies found a consistent advantage ([Danielson et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10629626)). High-confidence errors are especially likely to be corrected after feedback, apparently because surprise focuses attention (hypercorrection) ([Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11409-006-6894-z)), which argues for having learners *commit* to an answer before the reveal. The spec's existing device, "some of you think X, others Y", is exactly the right shape; the rewrite makes it do more work by stating the wrong belief plainly, letting the learner commit, breaking it with a case, and giving the reason.

Three further moves have moderate support. **Self-explanation and "why" prompts** produced better outcomes than instructor explanation in a meta-analysis of 64 studies ([BPS Research Digest on Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)), and suit rules that have a reason, not arbitrary conventions. **Worked examples, then fading** (full solution, then leave the last step, then learner tries) help novices, but the same guidance can hinder experienced learners, the expertise reversal effect ([Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9107-8)), so later lessons in a module should shift to "try it, then check". **Interleaving** confusable categories helps discrimination (g = 0.42) but *hurts* for simple word lists (g = −0.39), so new terms are taught blocked first and mixed afterwards ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)). **Curiosity** states improve memory for the answer and even for incidental material shown during the curious state ([Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4252494)), which supports the branch-situation opener, though only from lab trivia studies.

## Great teachers rehearse what looks spontaneous, and ask far more questions

The craft literature agrees with the lab. Rosenshine's synthesis of classroom research and master-teacher practice lists ten principles that map nearly one-to-one onto a narrated lesson: begin with a short review, present new material in small steps with practice after each, ask many questions, provide models, guide practice, check understanding, aim for about **80% success**, scaffold, require independent practice, and review weekly and monthly ([Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)). In one maths study he cites, the least effective teachers asked **only nine questions in 40 minutes** and almost no process ("how did you get that?") questions. Effective teachers think aloud, model each step, and "anticipate students' errors and warn them" ([Rosenshine 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/periodicals/Rosenshine.pdf)). Lemov's *Teach Like a Champion* moves (Cold Call, Wait Time, Call and Response, No Opt Out, Right Is Right) are observational rather than individually tested, and have been criticised for producing compliance ([Structural Learning TLAC guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/teach-like-a-champion-techniques)), but several translate cleanly into recorded video: a direct "you, answer before I do" with a visible countdown; a scripted 3-second hold, since response quality rose sharply once wait time passed about 2.7 seconds in Rowe's work ([Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/wait-time-three-seconds), secondary source); "say it with me" for short lists, where choral responding has small-study support ([Heward lab, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297777)); and re-asking a commonly missed question in a new form a few minutes later so every viewer ends right.

Celebrated explainers look relaxed because they over-prepare. Walter Lewin's lectures came from "weeks of exacting preparation and rehearsal", with a dress rehearsal from 5:30 a.m. on lecture day ([CS Monitor](https://csmonitor.com/World/Making-a-difference/2011/0727/Walter-Lewin-is-hands-on-helping-students-grasp-physics)) and demonstrations choreographed "down to the minute and millimeter" ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2011/walter-lewin-lecture-book-0518)); Feynman's conversational voice was deliberate, and an editor's rewrite into textbook prose was reverted ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/lecture-notes-1.14290)). Khan Academy builds its videos on Mayer's principles: conversational narration, short segments followed by practice, words written as they are spoken, and a moving circle or cross that follows the narration to show where to look ([Khan Academy blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/how-khan-academy-videos-are-made-to-help-you-learn/)). For our pipeline the lesson is that the cue sheet *is* the rehearsal: the board should be designed backwards from its final state, with every stroke assigned to a word.

Popular Indian exam teachers are praised in public sources for conceptual clarity, everyday examples, exam focus, humour and plain or vernacular language ([EdisonOS on Khan Sir](https://www.edisonos.com/blog/how-about-teaching-in-vernacular-like-khan-sir-from-patna); [Zeroinfy CA faculty listing](https://zeroinfy.in/collections/ca-parveen-sharma)); these are promotional or light-press sources, useful for style, not proof of effectiveness, and we take nothing else from them. The visible JAIIB/CAIIB learner voice is thin, but it points to the reasons people buy a second course: outdated questions, "no proper explanations" ([AppBrain review of a JAIIB app](https://www.appbrain.com/app/jaiib-exam-preparation-app/com.edugorilla.otherjaiib)), Hindi or Hinglish delivery when the learner wants English, and textbooks read aloud. So the "I don't need another tutor" feeling rests on four visible things beyond teaching quality: the rule-version line, a source caption for each rule, an explanation behind every practice answer, and an honest scope statement saying which syllabus sub-topics the lesson fully covered. Bandura's work on self-efficacy points the same way: lasting confidence comes mainly from **mastery experiences**, so an easy early win the learner gets right from counter experience builds belief that the hard parts are reachable ([Faculty Focus summary](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/online-education/online-course-delivery-and-instruction/promoting-students-self-efficacy-online-classroom/)).

On length, the owner's willingness to go longer is compatible with the evidence, as long as the unit of attention stays short. The famous "6 minutes" is a median *engagement* figure from free MOOC viewers ([Guo et al. 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf)); a credit-bearing college audience watched for median sessions of 12–13 minutes ([UMD summary of Lagerstrom 2015](https://pm.umd.edu/2020/12/09/video-production-guidance/)); and the strongest learning result (Szpunar) came from a 21-minute lecture cut into 5.5-minute segments with questions between them. No study our researchers found varied total length while holding segmenting and questions constant. Segmenting at **natural idea boundaries** helps (d 0.35–0.42), and breaking mid-idea hurts ([Castro-Alonso et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870)). Speech rate has no proven optimum; comprehension held from 1x to 2x playback, with a modest drop on a one-week test already at 1.5x ([Murphy et al. 2021](https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2022/03/Applied-Cognitive-Psychology-2021-Murphy-Learning-in-double-time-The-effect-of-lecture-video-speed-on-immediate-and.pdf)), so a calm 140–160 words a minute base suits second-language listeners and dense terms, and energy matters more than speed.

## The playbook

What follows turns the evidence into rules for scripts and cue sheets. Evidence grades: **Strong** = several meta-analyses or a large replicated literature; **Moderate** = one meta-analysis, consistent lab work with some classroom support, or clear boundary conditions; **Weak** = single studies, observational or practitioner craft; **Design choice** = our decision where no evidence settles it. Every fact inside an example line below was checked against `research/bfm/facts/A1.1-A1.3_A00.md` (rows 2, 3, 12, 13, 13a, 15: FEMA s. 2(n); FEDAI Rule 5.2); anything not on that sheet is flagged **[verify]** and must not go into a script until a fact sheet carries it with its paragraph.

### 2. Script patterns with example lines

The patterns below are the building blocks. Each is followed by lines for the value-date topic and for FEMA's definition of foreign exchange. Facts used: cash/ready = same day; tom = next working day; spot = second working day after the deal; forward = beyond spot, with the forward period counted from the spot date; holidays are skipped, so a holiday on day 2 pushes spot later (FEDAI 5.2, fact rows 12–13a). FEMA s. 2(n): foreign exchange means foreign currency **and includes** (i) deposits, credits and balances payable in any foreign currency; (ii) drafts, travellers cheques, letters of credit or bills of exchange expressed or drawn in Indian currency but payable in foreign currency; (iii) the same instruments drawn by banks, institutions or persons outside India but payable in Indian currency (fact rows 2–3).

**P1. Branch situation with a withheld answer (curiosity, pre-question).** End on a question with two plausible answers on screen; promise when it will be answered.
> "Friday afternoon. An exporter at your counter has just sold dollars. He asks: 'When do the rupees reach my account? I pay salaries on Monday.' Some of you are thinking Monday. Others, Tuesday. Hold your answer. In four minutes, you'll know."

> "A London bank draws a draft on an Indian bank. It's payable in rupees. Is that foreign exchange under FEMA? Most of you will say no; it's rupees. Keep that answer. We'll test it."

**P2. Names first (pre-training), 20–30 seconds.** Introduce each term with the one feature that defines it; mention any alias once, then keep one name.
> "Four words before we start. Cash. Tom. Spot. Forward. Each one is just a settlement date, counted from the deal day. Cash is also called 'ready'. Same thing. In this lesson I'll say 'cash'."

**P3. The rule as the answer, one sentence, then the key line three ways** (plain, slower with stress, as a question back).
> "Spot means settlement on the second working day after the deal. … The *second* … *working* … day. … So, a deal on Monday. Spot is…? [HOLD 3s] Wednesday."

**P4. Refutation (state, commit, break, reason).**
> "Many officers believe 'spot' means 'on the spot', today. Are you one of them? Decide now. [HOLD 2s] Here's the test: Monday deal, spot. Today? No. Wednesday. 'Spot' is not today. The same-day deal has its own name: cash."

> "Earlier most of you said a rupee draft can't be foreign exchange. FEMA says otherwise. Pocket three: a draft drawn by a bank outside India, payable in rupees, *is* foreign exchange. It's rupees on paper, but it was drawn abroad."

**P5. Worked example with think-aloud, then fade.**
> "Watch how I do it. Deal on Friday, spot. First question: which days count? Working days only. Saturday, no. Sunday, no. Monday is working day one. Tuesday is working day two. So spot is Tuesday."
> "Now you do the last step. Deal on Thursday, spot. Friday is working day one. Working day two is…? [HOLD 3s] Monday."
> "Your turn, alone. Deal on Wednesday, tom. [HOLD 4s] Thursday."

**P6. "Why" prompt, only where the source gives a reason.** For the forward rule the fact sheet gives a structure (the period counts from spot) but no stated reason; so use a process question instead of a "why".
> "A one-month forward done today. Where does the month start counting, from today or from the spot date? [HOLD 3s] From the spot date. Spot is the base of the interbank market, so forwards are measured from it."

The often-repeated explanation that spot is T+2 because banks once needed two days to confirm and move funds across time zones is **[verify]**: it is not on our fact sheet, so it may not be used until a primary source supports it.

**P7. Chunk and count, "say it with me" (three or four items only).**
> "Foreign exchange under FEMA: foreign currency, plus three pockets. Pocket one: deposits, credits and balances in foreign currency. Pocket two: instruments in rupee terms but payable in foreign currency. Pocket three: instruments drawn outside India, payable in rupees. Say the three pockets with me: balances… rupee-face, foreign-pay… drawn abroad, rupee-pay."

**P8. Mixed check-in (interleaving after blocked teaching; ~80% success, easy first).**
> "Three quick ones. Easy first. Deal Tuesday, cash. [HOLD 3s] Tuesday. Next. Deal Tuesday, tom. [HOLD 3s] Wednesday. Now harder. Deal Thursday, spot, and Monday is a holiday. [HOLD 4s] Tuesday. The holiday doesn't count as a working day, so it pushes spot later."

(Whether a holiday at *either* centre counts is **[verify]**; the fact sheet states only that a holiday on day 2 pushes spot later. The preceding/next working-day rule applies to the delivery date of a *merchant forward contract*, fact row 13, and must not be generalised to spot.)

**P9. The one thing to remember (once per chapter).**
> "If you remember one thing from this chapter: count working days, and spot is the second one."

**P10. Dollar see-saw (second analogy topic), with its limit stated.** The fact sheet gives only the concept (row 15: the rate is set by demand for and supply of the currency); any named cause in a script needs its own fact row.
> "Picture a see-saw. On one side, people who need dollars. On the other, people who bring dollars. More people need dollars, so the dollar side goes down and the dollar gets dearer in rupees. The see-saw shows the direction. It doesn't show how far. That needs numbers, and we'll get there."

**P11. Honest framing of effort (once in the course, short reminder per lesson).**
> "This will feel easy as you watch. Easy now isn't the same as remembered in May. The pause questions are how you find out. Pause, answer out loud, then let me check you."

**P12. Exam signal only where the Importance Map supports it.**
> "Mark this. The spot date has been asked in recent cycles [cite the count from `importance_map.md`]."
Never say "comes every time".

### 3. Board moves synced to speech

The board for the value-date chapter is planned backwards from its final state: left, a small counter sketch with the customer's question; centre, a horizontal timeline from T with rungs T, T+1, T+2 and an open arrow beyond; right, a working column for examples. Nothing is erased; finished regions dim to grey when not under discussion.

| Spoken words (the cue lands on the word in bold) | Board action at that instant | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Friday afternoon. An exporter at your **counter**…" | Counter sketch draws; "Fri" circles on a small calendar | Concrete start; curiosity |
| "Four words: **cash**, **tom**, **spot**, **forward**." | Four tiles write one by one, each on its word | Pre-training; temporal contiguity |
| "Everything is counted from the deal day. We call it **T**." | "T" writes at the left of a timeline line | Term written as first spoken (L2, technical term) |
| "Cash settles the **same day**." | Rung at T; "CASH" label writes on the rung; arrow from tile to rung | Spatial contiguity; arrow carries meaning |
| "Tom settles the **next working day**." | Rung T+1; "TOM" writes; underline under "working" | One cue, on the stressed word |
| "Spot settles on the **second working day**." | Rung T+2 draws stroke by stroke; "SPOT" writes; circle closes around "2nd working day" during the hold that follows | Live drawing; signaling; hold lets drawing finish |
| "Not the second *calendar* day." | "calendar" written small and struck through in red | Refutation made visible |
| "And forward is anything **beyond spot**." | Open arrow extends past T+2; "FORWARD" writes along it; small note "counted from spot" | Completes structure |
| "Many think spot means **today**." | "SPOT = TODAY?" writes at right, then a red strike on "today" when the voice says "No" | Misconception shown, then broken |
| "Deal on Friday. **Saturday**, no. **Sunday**, no." | Calendar in working column; a grey X lands on each day as named | Think-aloud modelled |
| "Monday, working day **one**. Tuesday, working day **two**." | "1" and "2" tags write; "Tue" circles; answer box draws round "SPOT = Tue" | Answer boxed; stays on board |
| "If you remember one thing…" | The boxed rule pulses once; everything else dims | Single focus |
| Chapter check-in: "Pause now." | Question card with three-dot countdown; options grey; answer lights after the hold | Retrieval with feedback |

For FEMA's definition: a large box labelled "Foreign exchange (FEMA s. 2(n))" draws as the voice says "foreign exchange"; "= foreign currency" writes on the word "means"; "+ includes" writes on "includes"; three trays draw inside the box, numbered 1–3, each filling with a two-to-four word label exactly when the voice names that pocket; on pocket three, the words "payable in rupees" are underlined and "drawn outside India" circled, because that clause is the exam surprise. The box is the "learn this wording" signal; the trays are the chunking. For the see-saw: the plank tips on the word "so", never before, and the limit sentence ("doesn't show how far") gets a small dashed outline around the see-saw.

Five board rules follow. Cue on the word, within about 0.3 seconds, never a sentence early (design tolerance; the principle is d 1.31). One cue per key idea; if two things glow, one is noise. Keep a fixed role colour across the course, for example rule = blue, trap = red, numbers = green, example = grey (design choice; no study compares colour schemes). Never put a paragraph on the board; keyword plus number. Every number spoken is written and stays.

### 4. Chapter and whole-lesson templates

**Chapter template (3–6 minutes, one idea):**

| Beat | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| a. Re-entry line | 5 s | "Chapter 2 of 4: the spot date." Map node for this chapter appears faded |
| b. Recall of earlier chapters | 20–40 s | One or two questions from previous chapters, cumulative (chapter 3 recalls 1 and 2) |
| c. Hook and pre-question | 20–45 s | Branch situation or question with two plausible answers; "hold your answer" |
| d. Names first (if new terms) | 20–30 s | Term tiles |
| e. Teach | 1.5–3 min | Concrete picture → labelled picture → rule card (concreteness fading); key line ×3; refute the misconception; answer the hook explicitly ("remember my question?") |
| f. Model and fade | 0.5–1.5 min | Worked example with think-aloud; learner finishes the next |
| g. Check-in | 30–60 s | 2–3 pause questions, recall before options, easy to hard, ~80% target; correct answer + one-clause reason; for a wrong-but-sure answer, "if you said X, here's why it's tempting" |
| h. One thing to remember | 10 s | Boxed rule pulses; map node turns solid |

**Whole-lesson template (about 16–22 minutes, 4–5 chapters).** This revises `course/spec/lesson_spec.md`, whose shape stays, with retrieval moved into every chapter.

| Part | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Warm-up | 1–1.5 min | AI-voice disclosure; rule-version card ("as at 31 December 2026"); unique greeting; why it matters (true Importance Map number); one-line learning goal ("by the end you'll name the value date of any deal"); the honesty line about pauses (P11) |
| 1. Retrieval opener | 45–60 s | Two questions from the previous lesson, one from two to four lessons back; answers with reasons |
| 2. Early win | 20–30 s | One easy question the learner answers from branch experience |
| 3. Chapters 1–4 (or 5) | 3–6 min each | Chapter template above |
| 4. Worked numericals | own chapter(s) | Full, faded, try; calculator keys on screen and spoken; error-analysis line ("if you got X, you…") |
| 5. Trap chapter or trap cards | 1–2 min | Each trap as the tempting answer first, then the rule and the reason |
| 6. Mixed practice | 1–2 min | 3–5 interleaved exam-form questions (MCQ, "which is NOT", short case); answers with reasons |
| 7. Blank-map recall | 1–1.5 min | Map skeleton with empty branches; "say each branch before I fill it" [HOLD 5s]; branches fill one by one; then one walk of the full board/map |
| 8. Closing self-check | 30–45 s | "You can now…" list of 4–6 question kinds tied to sub-topic ids; scope statement ("this lesson fully covered A1.x"); "if any line made you hesitate, rewatch chapter N" |
| 9. Close | 20–30 s | One-line preview of the next lesson's question (never leaving today's question open); homework ("redraw this map tonight from memory"); unique goodbye |

At about 140 words a minute, this is roughly 2,000–2,800 spoken words plus holds; the spec's 1,200–2,000-word band and 12–15 minute target would need revising if the owner adopts the longer form. The voice-page limit (parts of about 9,000 characters) still works: such a script fits in two or three parts cut at chapter breaks.

### 5. Repetition rules for key terms

Evidence supports repeating *through retrieval and varied use*; it gives **no magic number** of repetitions. Rules marked [design] are our choices.

The key line of each chapter is said three ways in one place: plain, slower with stress on the one word the board also marks, then as a question back to the learner [design, built on verbal signaling d 0.69]. Each core term is met in at least five *different* uses across the lesson: names card, rule, worked example, refutation or contrast, pause question, recap map, and the next lesson's opener [design; the opener use is spacing, Strong]. A repeat that asks nothing of the learner counts for little; at least half of each term's appearances should be questions or blanks [design, built on retrieval, Strong]. One name per concept, always the same exact words ("second working day", never "two days later" or "T+2 days" interchangeably); an alias such as "ready" is introduced once, linked, and not used again [design; Right Is Right / Format Matters, Weak; exam options hinge on such words]. The voice stresses only one word per sentence, and it is the word the board cues [design, from signaling's "sparingly" condition]. Do not re-explain from scratch; repeat by recall, contrast or example, consistent with the spec's "explain once, then recap" rule. "Say it with me" only for lists of three or four short items, never for multi-condition rules [Weak–moderate, choral responding]. Every key term returns in the next lesson's opener and again in the module revision video, which should be mostly mixed retrieval, not re-teaching [Strong, spacing].

### 6. Anti-patterns to avoid

The polished-voice trap comes first: a lesson that is smooth from start to finish, with no point where the learner must produce an answer, will feel excellent and teach less than it seems (fluency raises judgments of learning, not learning). Close behind are the narrated summary ("so today we learnt…") in place of a blank-map recall; the answer shown on the same screen as the question, or given before the hold ends; pause prompts with a hold under 3 seconds; and asking "Got it?" or "Easy, right?", which invites false confidence instead of checking it.

Board anti-patterns: everything glowing or bouncing, so nothing is signalled; a diagram that appears a sentence before or after its words; a paragraph of narration printed on the board; labels in a caption strip far from what they name; wiping the board between ideas, which throws away the cumulative record; decorative clip-art, currency-note stock photos, or music under definitions and numericals.

Script anti-patterns: a clear explanation that never states the wrong belief (Muller: clear videos can *strengthen* misconceptions); pre-questions whose answers never come, or come lessons later; "why" questions about arbitrary conventions; explanations offered as fact that are not on a fact sheet (the "two days for time zones" story is the live example); synonyms rotating for one technical term; long spoken passages with nothing written (transient information); an analogy with no stated limit; full worked examples for every numerical deep into a module, where learners should be trying first; humour at the learner's expense or more than one aside per chapter; "this comes every time" hype; any "Sir" persona, personal anecdote, or claim that a human is speaking; any line, question or diagram resembling a coaching provider's material; rules stated without the version or the "issued, coming" note where it applies; ending on a cliffhanger that leaves today's question unanswered (the Zeigarnik memory effect is weakly replicated ([SpaceDaily summary](https://www.spacedaily.com/j-v-the-zeigarnik-effect-helps-explain-why-unfinished-goals-can-feel-louder-than-completed-ones-but-modern-research-suggests-the-minds-pull-toward-open-loops-is-far-more-conditional-than-the/))).

A reviewer (the beginner, examiner or expert session, or a fourth "pedagogy" pass) marks each item Yes / No / N.A. and records every No as a finding.

## Conclusion

The shift this research implies is from **explaining well** to **making the learner answer well**. Our pipeline already does the hard part of the visual side, a beat for every sentence, which is the single largest effect in the multimedia literature; what it lacks is a learner who is forced, every few minutes, to produce the rule from memory and be corrected. That is cheap to add in a script and does more for exam scores than any further polish to voice or animation, because polish is exactly what inflates confidence without adding memory. It also reframes the owner's goal: the feeling "I don't need another tutor" should be the *by-product* of a learner who has answered twenty pause questions and filled a blank map, not something the voice persuades them of.

Two things remain genuinely unknown and should be measured rather than assumed. No study tested voice-only "pause and say it" prompts against written quizzes, and none tested these rules on Indian bank officers watching on phones in the evening. The module exam test (layer 8) can approximate the first by comparing a fresh agent's answers on lessons built the new way against A07 as currently built; after launch, adding real tap-to-answer checks in whatever platform hosts the course would turn covert retrieval into the overt kind the evidence actually measured, and would give us data no competitor has.
