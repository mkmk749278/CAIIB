What this lesson must carry. A bank treasury's bond book consists of sovereign Government Securities (G-Secs — dated Central Government securities, Treasury Bills, Cash Management Bills, and State Government Securities, formerly State Development Loans or SDLs) and non-SLR corporate debt (debentures and bonds). Under RBI's Commercial Banks – Cash Reserve Ratio and Statutory Liquidity Ratio Directions, 2025 (updated 9 October 2026), SLR stands at 18% of NDTL, met through cash, bullion (valued not above market price), excess CRR, SDF balances, or unencumbered approved SLR securities — namely GoI dated securities (Market Borrowing Programme and MSS), GoI T-Bills (including CMBs), State Government Securities (SGS), RBI-notified approved securities, and STRIPS of eligible G-Secs; corporate bonds, debentures, and GoI Special Securities (oil, fertiliser, food, UDAY, and bank recapitalisation bonds) are non-SLR. G-Secs are held not only for SLR compliance but also for trading gains and liquidity management. Primary issuance runs through RBI's electronic e-Kuber auctions (yield-based for new issues, price-based for re-issues, spread-based for new FRBs; uniform or multiple price; 5% Non-Competitive Bidding in GoI dated securities and 10% in SGS, minimum ₹10,000). Every commercial bank must hold all securities — G-Secs and corporate bonds, listed or privately placed — only in dematerialised form (SGL with RBI, CSGL, or NSDL/CDSL/NSCCL). Bond varieties include fixed-rate, floating-rate (FRB), zero-coupon (ZCB — banks cannot buy corporate long-term ZCBs unless backed by a liquid sinking fund), STRIPS (extended to eligible State Government Securities w.e.f. 12 June 2025 alongside Central G-Secs, giving zero reinvestment risk), callable and putable bonds (embedded options alter pricing and carry a 1-year lock-in and 21-day notice for listed corporate debt under SEBI NCS Regulations), step-up/step-down coupon bonds, and convertible bonds (which fail SPPI). Under the RBI (Commercial Banks – Classification, Valuation and Operation of Investment Portfolio) Directions, 2025 (updated 1 October 2026), the old three-way HTM/AFS/HFT split is replaced by HTM, AFS, and FVTPL (with HFT as a sub-category inside FVTPL), driven by business objective and the SPPI test rather than SLR status; the Investment Fluctuation Reserve (IFR) was discontinued w.e.f. 18 May 2026. For bond valuation, a bond's price equals the present value of all future cash flows discounted at its YTM, computable on the basic 8-function IIBF calculator using repeated division; semi-annual bonds (standard for Indian G-Secs) require halving the annual coupon and yield and doubling the periods. Price and yield move inversely (discount => YTM > current yield > coupon; par => all equal; premium => YTM < current yield < coupon). Current yield measures annual coupon over market price, while YTM is the total-return IRR (approximated on a basic calculator via [C + (FV − P)/n] / [(FV + P)/2]). When a bond trades between coupon dates, the buyer pays the dirty price = clean price + accrued interest; Indian G-Secs use the 30/360 day-count convention (vs Actual/365 for T-Bills and Actual/Actual for listed corporate bonds), and banks must expense broken-period interest to P&L rather than capitalising it. Corporate bonds trade at a credit spread over equivalent G-Secs (at least 50 bps mark-up for unquoted rated corporate bonds). Finally, the yield curve plots yield against maturity for equal credit quality — taking normal (upward), flat, or inverted shapes — while bondholders manage interest-rate risk, reinvestment risk, liquidity risk, call risk, and credit risk.

| Bond valuation principle: the price of a bond equals the sum of the present values of all remaining future cash flows (periodic coupons plus redemption value) discounted at the bond's Yield to Maturity (YTM): P = Sum of C / (1 + r)^t + FV / (1 + r)^n. | PRIMER Paras 21–22, 24.4, Annex 7 | F25 |
| Semi-annual bond convention: Indian dated G-Secs pay coupons half-yearly; when pricing a semi-annual bond, halve the annual coupon (C/2), halve the annual YTM (r/2), and double the number of years (2n). Failing to halve the coupon/yield or double the periods produces a wrong price. | PRIMER Para 1.6(i), Para 24.4 Box III; AUC26 Annex Para 19 | F26 |
| Price–yield inverse relationship: bond price and market yield move in opposite directions. If market yield > coupon rate, the bond trades at a discount (P < FV); if market yield < coupon rate, it trades at a premium (P > FV); if market yield = coupon rate, it trades at par (P = FV). | PRIMER Para 23; SEBIFAQ Q13–Q15; FIMMDA General FAQ | F27 |
| Coupon yield = (Annual Coupon / Face Value) × 100 (fixed nominal rate); Current yield = (Annual Coupon / Current Market Price) × 100. Current yield captures only annual coupon income relative to purchase price and ignores capital gain/loss at redemption and reinvestment income. | PRIMER Paras 24.2–24.3; FIMMDA Current Yield FAQ; SEBIFAQ Q21(d) | F28 |
| Yield to Maturity (YTM) is the total expected annualised return (IRR) if a bond is held to maturity, incorporating coupon income, capital gain/loss from price to redemption value, and reinvestment of coupons at the YTM itself. Exact YTM requires trial-and-error/interpolation; on a basic exam calculator, the standard approximation is YTM_approx = [C + (FV − P)/n] / [(FV + P)/2]. For a discount bond, YTM > Current Yield > Coupon Rate; for a premium bond, YTM < Current Yield < Coupon Rate. | PRIMER Paras 23, 24.1, 24.4 Box III; FIMMDA YTM FAQ; SEBIFAQ Q21(a) | F29 |
| Clean price vs Dirty price and Accrued Interest: clean price is the quoted price excluding accrued interest; accrued (broken-period) interest is interest earned from the last coupon date up to one day prior to settlement date, paid by buyer to seller. Dirty price = Clean price + Accrued interest; Total consideration = Face value × (Dirty price / 100). | PRIMER Para 22, Annex 7; SEBIFAQ Q22–Q23 | F30 |
| Day-count conventions in India: dated G-Secs use 30/360 (30 days per month, 360 days per year); T-Bills and money-market instruments use Actual/365; listed corporate non-convertible securities under SEBI NCS Regulations use Actual/Actual. | PRIMER Paras 22, 25, Annex 7; SEBINCS Reg 14(1); FIMMDA Day Count FAQ | F31 |
| Bank accounting for broken-period interest: under RBI Investment Portfolio Directions 2025, a bank must not capitalise broken-period interest paid to the seller as part of acquisition cost; it must be treated as an item of expenditure under the Profit and Loss Account. | INV25 Para 98 | F32 |
| Credit spread and corporate bond valuation: credit spread (quality spread) is the additional yield on a corporate bond over an equivalent-maturity Central G-Sec, compensating for default and liquidity risk. FBIL publishes G-Sec/SDL valuation prices (since 31 Mar 2018); FIMMDA publishes corporate bond spreads; unquoted rated corporate bonds must be valued at a mark-up of at least 50 bps over equivalent Central G-Sec YTM. | PRIMER Paras 28.2, 28.4; SEBIFAQ Q16; INV25 Paras 74, 78(1)(i)(a) | F33 |
| Yield curve and shapes: the yield curve plots yield against maturity for bonds of the same credit quality (term structure of interest rates). Its three main shapes are: Normal / Positive (upward-sloping: long-term yields > short-term yields), Flat / Neutral (similar yields across maturities), and Inverted / Negative (downward-sloping: short-term yields > long-term yields). The Zero Coupon Yield Curve (ZCYC / spot curve) plots zero-coupon yields and avoids YTM's reinvestment assumption. | PRIMER Annex 7; FIMMDA Yield Curve & ZCYC FAQs | F34 |
| Bond investment risks: even default-free G-Secs carry interest-rate (market/price) risk (higher for longer maturities), reinvestment risk (coupons reinvested at lower rates when rates fall; zero for ZCBs/STRIPS), and liquidity risk (wide bid-ask spreads in off-the-run papers); corporate bonds additionally carry credit/default risk, and callable bonds carry call risk. | PRIMER Paras 12, 29.1–29.4; SEBIFAQ Q18; FIMMDA Risks FAQ | Not in this video |
