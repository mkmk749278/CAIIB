# Fact sheet · D6.1 Profit planning · D6.2 Risk aggregation and capital allocation · D6.3 Economic capital vs regulatory capital (video D07)
Layer 2 (PLAYBOOK §4c). Written 2026-11-24. Rules as at the 31 December 2026 cut-off (exam May/June 2027). Rows cited as [F1]–[F32]. Written in our own words; every row traces directly to an opened primary source with full URL and paragraph/section number.


What this lesson must carry. Profit planning in a bank is the disciplined planning of sustainable earnings at an acceptable level of risk, not the maximisation of short-term accounting profit. Under Section 29 of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 and RBI's 2025 Financial Statements Directions, bank profitability is driven by six operating variables that pull against one another: three income levers (interest earned under Schedule 13, fee/commission/brokerage income under Schedule 14(I), and treasury/trading gains under Schedule 14(II)–(V)) and three expense levers (interest expended under Schedule 15, staff costs under Schedule 16(I), and other operating expenses under Schedule 16(II)–(XII)), with Provisions and Contingencies charged above the line before arriving at net profit. Treasury trading gains are volatile and non-repeatable; heavy holdings of Government securities carry 0% credit risk weight but lower yields than advances; and Non-Performing Assets deliver a double hit by stopping interest accrual while triggering above-the-line provisions that erode profit and capital. Indian banks operate within a landscape reshaped by post-1991 Narasimham Committee I (1991) and II (1998) reforms, differentiated banks (Small Finance Banks with ₹300 crore minimum equity, 15% CRAR, 60% PSL target reduced from 75% from FY 2025-26, and at least 50% loans up to ₹25 lakh; Payments Banks with ₹100 crore equity, 15% CRAR, 3% leverage ratio, ₹2 lakh demand-deposit cap, no lending or credit cards, and 75% SLR G-Sec deployment), AIFIs including NaBFID (established under the NaBFID Act, 2021 with ₹1,00,000 crore authorised capital and no retail public deposits), and Public Sector Bank consolidation (10 PSBs merged into 4 on 1 April 2020, leaving 12 PSBs). To align profit with risk, banks separate Expected Loss (EL = PD × EAD × LGD, covered by pricing and provisions) from Unexpected Loss (UL, peak tail losses up to a chosen confidence level, covered by capital). Regulatory capital is the statutory Pillar 1 floor (9% CRAR plus 2.5% CCB = 11.5%), whereas economic capital is the bank's internal risk-sensitive estimate across all material risks, embedded in a Board-approved ICAAP reviewed at least annually. Aggregating risk across business lines yields a diversification benefit when correlation is below 1, whereas concentration or rising correlation raises economic capital even when accounting profit is unchanged.

| Expected Loss (EL) is the average level of credit loss a bank forecasts over a horizon, computed as EL = PD × EAD × LGD; banks treat EL as a regular cost of doing business and cover it through loan pricing (interest spread) and provisions, never through capital. | BIS05, Section 2 (pp. 2–4) & Section 4.4 (p. 7) (https://www.bis.org/publications/explanatory-note-basel-ii-irb-risk-weight-functions.pdf) | F24 |
| Unexpected Loss (UL) is the potential peak loss above Expected Loss up to the Value-at-Risk (VaR) threshold at a chosen confidence level (e.g., 99.9% over 1 year in Basel IRB); bank capital exists specifically to absorb Unexpected Losses and protect solvency. | BIS05, Section 2 (pp. 2–3), Section 4.4 (p. 7), Section 5.1 (p. 11) (https://www.bis.org/publications/explanatory-note-basel-ii-irb-risk-weight-functions.pdf) | F25 |
