# Fact sheet · A10.1–A10.7 Technology in international banking (video A22)


Layer 2 (PLAYBOOK §4c). Written 2026-11-24. Rules as at the **31 Dec 2026** cut-off (exam May/June 2027). Rows cited as [F1]–[F50]. Our own words; every plain row cites a primary text actually opened and read. Sister sheets: A02 (SWIFT, SFMS, correspondent banking basics) and A08–A09 (UCP 600 LC relay and documents).


**What this lesson must carry.** International banking has moved from paper and telex to structured electronic messaging, digital platforms, and distributed ledgers. **SWIFT** (a Belgian member-owned cooperative overseen by the **National Bank of Belgium** with **RBI** on the **SWIFT Oversight Forum**) carries messages, not money. Its **ISO 20022** migration for cross-border payments and reporting (**CBPR+**) ended coexistence with legacy **MT** messages on **22 November 2025** (after coexistence began in **March 2023**), with over **98%** of payment instructions now in ISO 20022 and structured or hybrid postal addresses targeted under **SR 2026** (supporting **FATF Recommendation 16**). India's **NG-RTGS** pioneered ISO 20022 on **20 December 2013**. **Swift GPI** adds real-time tracking via a **36-character UETR** (field 121), **Universal Confirmations** (mandatory since **22 November 2020**), and **Stop and Recall**, crediting nearly **60%** of payments within **30 minutes** and almost **100%** within **24 hours**. For electronic trade documents, ICC's **eUCP Version 2.1** supplements (never replaces) **UCP 600**, alongside **eURC Version 1.1** (for URC 522) and **URDTT Version 1.0**; legal recognition depends on national law (e.g., **UK Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023**, s. 3 in force **20 September 2023**). In India, **IDRBT** (set up by RBI on **10 June 1996**) created **INFINET** and **SFMS**, which **IFTAS** (RBI's wholly owned subsidiary) now operates **24×7×365**; **IDRBT** also initiated Indian banking blockchain work with its **January 2017** White Paper and domestic sight-LC Proof-of-Concept. **TReDS** (governed by **RBI (TReDS) Directions, 2026** dated **23 June 2026**, **₹25 crore** minimum net worth) discounts MSME Factoring Units **without recourse** to sellers with **CERSAI** assignment filing, while **IFSCA's ITFS** (revised **23 December 2024**) enables cross-border trade finance bidding at GIFT IFSC, and **CCIL** settles interbank USD/INR forex via **PvP**. **RBI's Regulatory Sandbox** (updated **28 February 2024**, **₹10 lakh** net worth, **9-month** cohort, crypto excluded, **DPDP Act 2023** mandatory) opened its **Second Cohort on Cross-Border Payments** in **December 2020** and made it **On Tap** on **5 September 2022**. **Payment Aggregators Cross-Border (PAs-CB)** (consolidated Master Direction **15 September 2025**) need **₹15 crore** rising to **₹25 crore** net worth and have a **₹25 lakh** per-transaction cap. Delivery channels include APIs, **UPI–PayNow** (Singapore, **February 2023**), **UPI acceptance in 10 countries** (as at **30 October 2026**, Uzbekistan 11th) via **NIPL**, **BBPS** cross-border bill pay, and **BIS Project Nexus** (RBI joined **30 June 2024** with four ASEAN central banks). On a **permissioned blockchain**, **smart contracts** and document tokens streamline the LC relay, though money settlement still requires payment rails or **CBDC**; benefits (speed, transparency, lower cost, fewer discrepancies) must be balanced against cyber/quantum risks (**Project Leap**), legal gaps, interoperability, data localisation (**6 April 2018** circular), and AML/CFT compliance under the **G20 Roadmap** (15 action items, 3 themes).

## Points — A10.6 Blockchain fundamentals and trade finance process


| # | Testable point | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 38 | **DLT vs Blockchain**: **Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT)** is a decentralised shared ledger updated by network nodes via a **consensus algorithm**; **blockchain** is a specific form of DLT where blocks are linked linearly using cryptographic hashes — **all blockchains are DLT, but not all DLTs are blockchains**. | S13 Section II |
| 39 | Core technical mechanisms: **cryptographic hash functions** (deterministic, irreversible, tamper-evident — each block contains the previous block's hash), **public-private key cryptography** (private key signs to prove origin; public key verifies/encrypts), **nodes**, and **tokens** (digital representation of an asset or ownership). | S13 Sections II.1–II.5, II.7 |
| 40 | **Permissioned vs Permissionless DLT**: Permissionless networks (e.g., Bitcoin, Ethereum) allow anyone to join and view all transactions, which is slow, energy-heavy under **Proof-of-Work (PoW)**, and lacks privacy; banks use **permissioned DLT** (e.g., **Hyperledger Fabric, Corda, Quorum**) requiring prior approval so each participant views only its own transactions. | S13 Sections II.6, III.1–III.2; S17 |
| 41 | **Smart contracts** are self-executing lines of code on a DLT that automatically verify predetermined conditions and execute agreed actions (such as triggering a payment instruction or transferring tokens) when conditions are met. | S13 Section III.3 |
| 42 | **Blockchain ≠ Cryptocurrency**: RBI has warned against private cryptocurrencies and excluded crypto services from the Regulatory Sandbox, while recognising the utility of underlying **permissioned DLT / blockchain** for trade finance, clearing, and interbank messaging. | S17; S13 Section VI; S7 para 6.3 |
| 43 | **Six-step blockchain LC / trade finance flow** (mirroring the traditional LC relay): (1) Buyer and seller agree sales terms and encode LC conditions as a **smart contract** on a **permissioned shared ledger**; (2) Issuing bank approves and records the digital LC on the ledger (visible instantly to advising/confirming bank and exporter); (3) Exporter ships goods and uploads **electronic trade documents / document tokens** hashed on the ledger; (4) Smart contract automatically verifies document data against LC conditions, flagging discrepancies early; (5) Matching conditions trigger the settlement instruction; (6) **Actual money settlement** still flows through integrated payment rails (correspondent banking/SWIFT, RTGS) or tokenised central bank money/CBDC, because DLT alone does not move central bank money. | S11 Ch. 5.12–5.13; S13 Sections II, III.3, VI Table 2 |

## Points — A10.2 & A10.7 Benefits, limitations, and challenges


| # | Testable point | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 44 | **Three core frictions** in traditional correspondent cross-border payments: (1) **multiple intermediaries** adding fees and delays; (2) **multi-jurisdictional AML, sanctions, and CDD checks**; and (3) **FX conversion and time-zone differences**. | S11 Ch. 5.1–5.2 |
| 45 | **G20 Roadmap for Enhancing Cross-Border Payments**: launched in **2020** (19 building blocks) and revised in **2023** to **15 action items** across **3 priority themes**: (1) payment system interoperability and extension, (2) cross-border data exchange and message standards (ISO 20022, APIs), and (3) legal, regulatory and supervisory frameworks (FSB, FATF Recommendation 16). | S15 (About the programme); S11 Ch. 5.3–5.4 |
| 46 | **Benefits of technology and DLT in international banking**: faster turnaround (minutes/hours instead of days), lower operational and remittance costs, real-time end-to-end transparency, tamper-evident audit trails, higher straight-through processing (STP), and fewer manual discrepancies and investigations. | S11 Ch. 5.12; S1; S3; S13 Sections II.7, VI |
| 47 | **Challenges — Cyber security & Quantum threats**: digital channels increase exposure to cyber-attacks, fraud, and system breaches (mitigated via **SWIFT CSP**, **ReBIT**, and annual **CISA/DISA System Audit Reports**); **quantum computing** threatens existing cryptography (**BIS Innovation Hub Project Leap**, Dec 2025, demonstrated post-quantum cryptography migration). | S11 Ch. 5.14; S12 Section E; S14 Ch. 19.12, 22.14, 24.6 |
| 48 | **Challenges — Data privacy & Localisation**: RBI's **6 April 2018** circular (*Storage of Payment System Data*) requires full end-to-end payment data to be stored **only in India** (foreign-leg data may also be stored abroad if required); the **DPDP Act 2023** governs personal data protection; cross-border pre-validation (**PPV**) must navigate differing privacy laws. | S14 Ch. 20.14; S7 para 8.2; S11 Ch. 5.15 |
| 49 | **Challenges — Interoperability, Legal recognition, Cost & AML/CFT**: fragmented messaging and DLT platforms ("digital islands"), uneven national legal recognition of electronic transferable records, high legacy IT integration costs, and multi-jurisdictional **AML/CFT/KYC** compliance (**FATF Recommendation 16**). | S15; S11 Ch. 5.5, 5.13, 5.15; S2 |
| 50 | **Stablecoins vs CBDC**: RBI (citing BIS 2025) notes private **stablecoins** fail the three monetary tests of **singleness of money, elasticity, and integrity**, risk de-pegging and capital-control circumvention; **CBDC** (RBI launched **CBDC-Wholesale** pilot on **1 November 2022** and **CBDC-Retail** pilot on **1 December 2022**) delivers digital efficiency as sovereign fiat without fragmentation. | S11 Ch. 5.10–5.11; S12 Section D(i) |
