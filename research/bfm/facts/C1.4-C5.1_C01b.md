# Fact sheet · C1.4 Organisation of treasury · A1.4 Foreign exchange dealing room operations · C5.1 Supervision and control of treasury (dealer level) (video C01b)
Layer 2 (PLAYBOOK §4c). Written 2026-11-24. Rules as at the 31 December 2026 cut-off (exam May/June 2027). Rows cited as [F3]. Our own words; every row cites its primary text and paragraph/rule number opened during Deep Research. Sister sheets: C01 (Integrated treasury: concept, functions, profit centre, globalisation, forex market features) and C07 (full treasury risk management: VaR, duration, stop-loss governance, market/credit risk).
What this lesson must carry. A commercial bank's treasury is a distinct reportable business segment under AS 17 (Commercial Banks – Financial Statements Directions, 2025, Para 6(3)(v)) that may be structured as a Head Office department or as a specialised treasury branch (maintaining its own general ledger and P&L, operating as an Authorised Dealer Category-I, and settling directly via SWIFT, RTGS/NEFT, NDS-OM, and CCIL), led by a senior treasury head and supported by an investment cell, accounts, IT systems, and connectivity units. The cardinal control principle across both investments (Commercial Banks – Classification, Valuation, and Operation of Investment Portfolio Directions, 2025, updated 1 October 2026, Para 91(1)) and foreign exchange (RBI Internal Control Guidelines for Foreign Exchange Business, 2011, Para 1.2) is strict functional segregation into Front Office (dealing room: executes deals and primary bids, never settles or accounts, never signs confirmations), Mid Office (independent risk monitoring, limit/stop-loss checks, MTM valuation, accounting policies, and MIS — reporting outside the treasury head, reinforced by CRO independence and fixed-pay-weighted control-staff remuneration under Commercial Banks – Governance Directions, 2025, Paras 29–31, 59, 63(4)), and Back Office (independent counterparty confirmation, value-date settlement, accounting, reconciliation, and breach flagging). Dealing rooms track both funds position (Nostro liquidity) and currency position (overbought/long vs oversold/short), governed by Board-approved policies and limits communicated to RBI via CIMS/email (Master Direction – Risk Management and Inter-Bank Dealings, updated 1 October 2026, Part C Paras 1–2 & Annex I): NOOPL capped at 25% of total capital (Tier I + II) (shorthand method: higher of total net longs or total net shorts), AGL capped at 6× total capital (replaceable by Board-approved PV01/VaR), internal deal-size, currency-wise, daylight (> overnight), and stop-loss limits, and a 5% per-broker annual cap on brokered securities turnover (Primary Dealers exempt). Two key 2026 updates apply at the 31 December 2026 cut-off: effective 10 April 2026 (RBI/2025-26/252, 27 March 2026), ADs must keep onshore deliverable NOP-INR within USD 100 million at day end; and from 6 April 2026, FBIL (not FEDAI) publishes the daily USD/INR spot rate for NOOP calculation (FEDAI retains month-end revaluation rates). RBI's revamped NOP and 9% forex capital-charge framework (Capital Adequacy Tenth Amendment Directions, 2026, 24 June 2026) is issued, coming (effective 1 April 2027). Core internal controls include non-dealer EDP access rules, auto-stamping, 2-month voice-recording retention, 2-week compulsory continuous annual dealer leave, written FEDAI/FIMMDA Code of Conduct undertakings, thrice-daily rate-scan reports, bans on broker principal trading and bank-name substitution, real-time Nostro reconciliation (100% provision on unreconciled debits > 2 years; unreconciled credits > 3 years to Blocked Account for CRR/SLR), monthly concurrent audit to the CEO, and annual dealing-room system audit. Forex dealing exposes banks to operational, exchange/market, credit (pre-settlement replacement cost and settlement / Herstatt risk, mitigated by PVP per BIS RMA20), liquidity, systemic, country, sovereign, gap, and legal risks (mitigated by ISDA/IFEMA/ICOM). Finally, under FEMA 1999 and RBI's ETP Directions, 2025 (16 June 2025), residents may trade forex only with Authorised Persons on RBI-authorised ETPs or recognised exchanges; RBI's Alert List (95 unauthorised entities as on 19 November 2025) is non-exhaustive, and LRS margin remittances to overseas forex platforms are prohibited.

	F3
	The Board of Directors frames the bank's comprehensive Investment Policy and Treasury risk policy, sets authority to put through deals, and delegates oversight to senior management/ALCO/Investment Committee; the treasury is headed at senior executive level and backed by support units (investment cell for primary market/proposals, accounts, IT/EDP systems, and market connectivity such as SWIFT, RTGS/NEFT, NDS-OM, and CCIL).

	F4
	Cardinal segregation rule (investments): Every commercial bank must maintain a clear functional separation of four activities: (a) trading, (b) settlement, (c) monitoring and control, and (d) accounting, as well as separate trading and back-office functions for the bank's own investment account versus PMS/constituents' accounts.
	INV25 Para 91(1)–(2)
	F5
	Cardinal segregation rule (forex dealing): A dealing department must strictly segregate three distinct offices: (i) Front Office (Dealing Room), (ii) Mid Office (Risk Management, Accounting Policies, and MIS), and (iii) Back Office (Settlement, Reconciliation, and Accounting).
	ICG11 Paras 1.1–1.2

| Exchange (Market) risk & Gap (Interest Rate) risk: Exchange risk is the risk of loss from adverse currency-rate moves on an open position. Gap / Interest-rate risk arises from maturity mismatches in foreign-currency deposits, forwards, swaps, FRAs, and option deltas when implied or actual interest-rate differentials move adversely. | ICG11 Para 3.3.3; RMID Part A Sec I Para 1(i)(c) | F34 |
| Credit risk — Pre-settlement vs Settlement (Herstatt) risk: Pre-settlement risk is the replacement cost if a counterparty defaults before settlement date. Settlement risk (Principal risk / Herstatt Risk) is the risk of losing the entire principal value of a trade when a bank irrevocably pays away the sold currency (past the unilateral payment cancellation deadline) and the counterparty fails to deliver the bought currency, often across time zones. Mitigated by Payment-versus-Payment (PVP) settlement (e.g., CLS Bank / CCIL) and binding counterparty limits. | ICG11 Para 3.3.1; BIS20 Paras 20.3, 20.19, 20.27 | F35 |
| Liquidity risk (Funding vs Market): Funding liquidity risk is inability to meet payment obligations on time (worsened if a counterparty fails to deliver a currency leg); Market liquidity risk is inability to exit or offset a position quickly at a reasonable price. | ICG11 Para 3.3.2; BIS20 Paras 20.44–20.48 | F36 |
| Operational risk & Legal risk: Operational risk covers system breakdowns, manual/fat-finger errors, confirmation delays, and dealer fraud/malpractices — mitigated by STP, contingency/BCP plans, and functional segregation. Legal risk is the risk that contracts or netting clauses are unenforceable on default — mitigated by internationally accepted Master Agreements (ISDA, IFEMA, ICOM), corporate Board Resolutions, and legal opinions. | ICG11 Paras 3.3.4, 3.3.5, 3.7; BIS20 Paras 20.52–20.68 | F37 |
| Country risk, Sovereign risk & Systemic risk: Country risk is the risk that economic, social, or political events in a foreign country prevent borrowers/counterparties there from meeting obligations (banks use ECGC's 7-category country risk scale for provisioning/disclosure until moving to internal ratings); Sovereign risk is the risk of default or exchange restrictions by a foreign government/central bank; Systemic risk is contagion where one major counterparty or FMI failure disrupts the wider financial system. | FS25 Para 10(5)(iii); BIS20 Paras 20.46, 20.50 | F38 |
