[warmly] Hello, everyone. Keep a pen and your basic calculator close; tonight we unpack options, one choice at a time. Phone on silent. Two quick recall questions first. From last class on swaps: fixed-rate loans, floating deposits, fearing rising rates. Pay fixed or receive fixed? Pay fixed, receive floating. That locks your floating deposit cost. From treasury risk control earlier: which open position limit is larger, daylight or overnight? Daylight limit, because dealers square intraday positions before market close. Easy start. Calls, puts, moneyness, and option payoffs were tested across four recent exam cycles, including case sets. Tonight you will read calls and puts, split premiums, compute payoffs and profits, and hedge while keeping upside. Picture Meera, an illustrative investor, holding one hundred shares of Deccan Infra bought at five hundred rupees. She expects the share to stay flat for a month and wants extra income without selling. Should she buy a put option, or sell a call option? Pick one. Hold that choice. By chapter four, we solve Meera's exact rupees on your calculator. Chapter one. How does an option differ from a forward or a future? Forwards and futures bind both sides. An option gives the buyer a right, with no obligation. The seller, or writer, accepts the binding obligation if the buyer chooses to exercise. For that privilege, the buyer pays an upfront fee called the premium to the seller. A call option gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying at a fixed strike price. A put option gives the buyer the right to sell the underlying at a fixed strike price. Quick check: if you expect a stock or currency to rise, do you buy a call or a put? Buy a call, because buying at a lower strike pays off when prices climb. Remember this asymmetry: the buyer's maximum loss is strictly limited to the premium paid. On the other side, the seller's maximum gain is strictly limited to that same premium received upfront. Now check exercise style. Many learners think European and American options mean where the contract trades. Geography has nothing to do with it. Style only tells you when the buyer can exercise. A European option exercises only on expiry day. An American option exercises on any day up to expiry. A Bermudan option sits in between, exercisable only on specified dates. Which style do India's exchange-traded currency options and equity options use? European style. Under R-B-I directions and S-E-B-I rules, Indian exchange currency, equity, and retail O-T-C options are all European. Exchange currency options cover seven pairs, cash-settled in rupees at the F-B-I-L reference rate; lot size is one thousand dollars. Pause check, chapter one. First: who carries the binding obligation in an option contract? The option seller or writer, because the buyer holds only a right. Second: can you exercise an Indian exchange U-S-D I-N-R call ten days before expiry? No, because Indian exchange currency options are European and exercise only on expiry day. One thing to remember: buyer holds the right with capped loss; India's exchange options are European. Chapter two. Recall from chapter one: what is the maximum amount an option seller can ever earn? Only the premium received upfront. Now compare spot price against strike price — called moneyness. Start with a call, the right to buy at K. When is a call immediately valuable? When spot exceeds strike. For a call: spot above strike is In-the-Money, equal is At-the-Money, below is Out-of-the-Money. Watch the classic exam trap. Many assume a put is also In-the-Money when spot exceeds strike. Flip it. A put is the right to sell at K. Selling at five hundred when market is four eighty wins. So a put is the mirror: spot below strike is In-the-Money, equal is At-the-Money, above is Out-of-the-Money. Now split option premium into two blocks. Write this down: Premium equals Intrinsic Value plus Time Value. Intrinsic value is immediate exercise value. Because you never exercise at a loss, it is never negative. For a call, it is max of spot minus strike or zero. For a put, strike minus spot or zero. Every At-the-Money and Out-of-the-Money option has zero intrinsic value; its entire premium is time value. Time value is Premium minus Intrinsic Value. It pays for the chance of a favourable move before expiry. Quick sum one. Illustrative Deccan Infra call: spot five twenty-five, strike five ten, premium twenty-two rupees. Five two five minus five one zero equals fifteen rupees intrinsic value. Twenty-two minus fifteen equals seven rupees time value. As expiry nears, time value decays daily and hits zero at expiry. Options are eroding assets. Under Black-Scholes used by Indian exchanges, five inputs drive premium: spot, strike, time, volatility, and interest rate. Higher volatility lifts both call and put premiums, because wider swings raise payoff odds while losses stay capped. Sneaky trap: is forward premium one of the five Black-Scholes drivers of option price? No. The five inputs are spot, strike, time, volatility, and interest rates; interest rates already capture carry. Pause check, chapter two. First: spot is four eighty, strike five hundred. Is a put I-T-M or O-T-M? In-the-Money, because spot is below strike, giving twenty rupees of intrinsic value. Second: an Out-of-the-Money call trades at nine rupees. What is its intrinsic value? Zero, because intrinsic value never goes below zero; all nine rupees are time value. Third: volatility jumps suddenly. What happens to a put option's premium? It rises, because higher volatility lifts both calls and puts. One thing to remember: puts flip moneyness, intrinsic value stops at zero, and volatility lifts both sides. Chapter three. Recall from chapter two: on expiry day, what is any option's time value? Zero. So on expiry day, payoff equals intrinsic value alone. Now guard against the second big misconception: confusing gross payoff with net profit. Payoff ignores upfront premium. Buyer net profit is Payoff minus Premium; seller net profit is Premium minus Payoff. Picture four hockey-stick lines: Long Call, Long Put, Short Call, and Short Put. Long Call breaks even at strike plus premium; loss caps at premium, profit is unlimited. Long Put breaks even at strike minus premium; loss caps at premium, profit rises as price falls. Short Call and Short Put flip below zero: gain caps at premium, while seller losses are open-ended. That open-ended risk is why exchanges collect daily S-P-A-N margin from writers, while buyers pay premium on T plus one. Sum two, full worked. Illustrative U-S-D I-N-R European call: ten lots, meaning ten thousand dollars. Strike eighty-four rupees, premium sixty paise per dollar, expiry F-B-I-L rate eighty-five twenty. Unit payoff: eight five point two minus eight four equals one point two zero rupees. Net profit per dollar: one point two minus zero point six equals zero point six zero rupees. Multiply by ten thousand dollars: buyer gains six thousand rupees; seller loses six thousand rupees. Cross-check: breakeven is eighty-four sixty. Eighty-five twenty minus eighty-four sixty times ten thousand gives six thousand rupees. Exact match. Commonest error: stopping at twelve thousand rupees, which is gross payoff before deducting six thousand premium. Sum three, faded: Deccan Infra put, one hundred shares, strike five hundred, premium eighteen, expiry spot four sixty-five. Five hundred minus four sixty-five equals thirty-five payoff. Minus eighteen equals seventeen, times one hundred equals one thousand seven hundred rupees. Your turn, Sum four. Call on one hundred shares: strike five twenty, premium fourteen, expiry spot five zero five. Find buyer profit. Spot five zero five sits below strike five twenty, so the call lapses. Payoff is zero; net loss is one thousand four hundred rupees. The buyer walks away and loses only the premium — never fifteen or twenty-nine rupees per share. Pause check, chapter three. First: a put has strike ninety and premium four. What is breakeven? Eighty-six, because put breakeven is strike minus premium. Second: which single option position carries theoretically unlimited loss? A naked short call, because underlying prices have no ceiling. One thing to remember: payoff stops at zero, profit always counts premium, and naked call writers face unlimited risk.
```

```
Chapter four. Recall from chapter three: if a call expires Out-of-the-Money, who keeps the premium? The seller. Now ask why treasurers pay option premium instead of booking a free forward. A forward locks both sides; buying an option sets a safety floor while keeping favourable upside open. Two hedging strategies matter for exams. First, a protective put: hold the asset and buy a put. Like car insurance, the put caps worst-case loss while keeping upside open — though it covers price risk, not physical damage. Second, a covered call: hold the asset and sell a call. Remember Meera from our opening? If you picked "sell a call", you nailed it. Writing a call against shares she owns is a covered call. Sum five, Meera's numbers: one hundred shares bought at five hundred, plus one hundred short calls, strike five twenty, premium fifteen. Scenario A: shares jump to five forty. Stock gains forty per share, four thousand rupees. On the short call, she pays twenty payoff against fifteen premium, losing five per share, five hundred rupees. Combined net profit: four thousand minus five hundred equals three thousand five hundred rupees. Her upside caps at strike minus cost plus premium. Scenario B: shares slip to four ninety. Stock loses one thousand rupees, the call lapses, and she keeps one thousand five hundred premium. Net profit is five hundred rupees; the premium cushioned the drop down to breakeven four eighty-five. One vital regulatory line from R-B-I's O-T-C market-maker directions: plain-vanilla versus structured options. Plain-vanilla European calls, puts, interest rate caps, and floors are generic derivatives. Retail users may only buy them. Any other O-T-C package — barriers, digitals, or multi-component structures — is a structured derivative, allowed only for non-retail users with enhanced due diligence. Leveraged derivatives are strictly barred. And one bond link: callable and putable bonds carry embedded options inside debt. Pause check, chapter four. First: does a covered call cap your upside or cap your downside? It caps your upside at the strike price, while the premium only cushions a small drop. Second: can a retail user write a covered foreign exchange call option with an Indian bank? No; under R-B-I directions, covered F-X options and structured options are permitted only for non-retail users. Mixed check: an Indian exchange U-S-D I-N-R put has strike eighty-five, premium one rupee, and expiry spot eighty-four. Is it I-T-M, what is net profit per dollar, and could the buyer exercise a week early? I-T-M because spot is below strike; net profit is zero, one minus one; and no early exercise because it is European. Blank mind map on screen. Five seconds to recall our four branches: Styles, Moneyness and Premium, Four Payoffs, and Hedging and Structures. You can now classify calls and puts, split premiums, solve payoff and covered-call sums, and separate vanilla from structured options. Homework tonight: Meera holds one hundred shares at five hundred and buys one hundred puts, strike five hundred, premium twelve. Find her net loss if shares fall to four fifty at expiry. Next class brings R-B-I's derivative rulebook: if an exporter cancels a currency derivative in November twenty twenty-six, can the bank rebook it? [warmly] That completes our options toolkit, from premium to payoff. Close your notebook, rest well, and I'll meet you in the next class.
