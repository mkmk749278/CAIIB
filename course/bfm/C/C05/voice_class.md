Recall from Chapter Three: what is the extra yield on a corporate bond over an equivalent G-Sec called? Credit spread, or quality spread, which widens as credit quality weakens. Plot yields across maturities for identical credit quality, like G-Secs from 1 to 30 years: that is the yield curve. Three shapes: a normal curve slopes upward because investors demand higher yields for longer maturities. A flat curve shows similar yields across tenors; an inverted curve slopes downward, with short yields above long yields. Desks also build a Zero Coupon Yield Curve from STRIPS and bootstrapping, avoiding YTM's constant-reinvestment assumption. Even default-free G-Secs carry three risks: interest-rate risk, reinvestment risk on coupons, and liquidity risk in thin papers. Corporate bonds add credit risk, while callable bonds add call risk when falling rates trigger early redemption. Chapter Four pause check. First: on an inverted yield curve, are 10-year yields higher or lower than 1-year yields? Second: which sovereign bond variety eliminates reinvestment risk completely? Answers: one, lower, because an inverted curve slopes downward; two, zero-coupon bonds or STRIPS, having no interim coupons. One thing to remember: sovereign bonds still carry rate, reinvestment, and liquidity risk, and the curve maps rates across time. Mixed final check: a discount G-Sec has a 7% coupon. Is YTM above or below 7%, and which day count applies? Above 7% for a discount bond, and 30/360 for G-Sec accrued interest. Look at the blank six o'clock branch of our trading-desk map.
