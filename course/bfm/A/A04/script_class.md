## Chapter 1 · Reading a quote

N005 · Chapter one. Every quote is a pair. Euro-dollar one point zero eight five zero means one euro costs one point zero eight five zero dollars.
> 1 S: chapter title "1 · Reading a quote" · 2 S: a balance scale: "1 EUR" on the left, "1.0850 USD" on the right [F1]

N006 · The currency on the left is the base. One unit of it. The one on the right is the quote, the price in that currency.
> 1 N: left pan "base (1 unit)" · right pan "quote (the price)" [F1]

N007 · Quick one. In dollar-rupee eighty-five point six zero, which is the base?
> 1 S: scale "1 USD = 85.60 INR" · H: 3 s

N008 · The dollar. One dollar costs eighty-five point six zero rupees. That's a direct quote: so many rupees for one unit of foreign currency. India quotes directly.
> 1 N: "base = USD ✓" · 2 C: card "direct = ₹ per 1 (or 100) foreign units · India's merchant quotes" [F2]

N009 · Turn it round, and you get an indirect quote: so much foreign currency for one hundred rupees. One point one six eight two dollars per hundred rupees.
> 1 C: card "indirect = foreign units per ₹100 (e.g. USD 1.1682 per ₹100)" [F2]

N010 · And remember the two sides. The bid is where the bank buys the base currency. The ask is where it sells it.
> 1 N: "bid: bank buys the base · ask: bank sells the base" [F3]

N011 · Now the word for today. A cross rate is the rate between two currencies, fixed through a third. Usually the dollar.
> 1 C: card "cross rate = rate between two currencies through a third (usually USD)" [F4] · 2 B: box "through a third"

N012 · Even F-B-I-L does this. Its euro, pound and yen reference rates are crossed through dollar-rupee. And yen is quoted per hundred.
> 1 N: "FBIL: EUR, GBP, JPY reference rates crossed via USD/INR · JPY per 100" [F5]

N013 · [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter one: base on the left, one unit. And a cross rate goes through a third currency.
> 1 S: the scale and the card; finished parts dim to grey

## Chapter 2 · Cross rates: multiply or divide

N014 · Chapter two. First, from chapter one. In euro-dollar, which currency is the base?
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · Multiply or divide" · H: 3 s

N015 · The euro. Now look where the dollar sits. In euro-dollar, it's on the right. In dollar-rupee, it's on the left. Opposite sides.
> 1 N: "base = EUR ✓" · 2 S: two scales side by side; the USD glows right in one, left in the other [F7]

N016 · Rule one. When the dollar sits on opposite sides, multiply. The dollars cancel, and you're left with euro-rupee.
> 1 C: card "USD on opposite sides → MULTIPLY" [F7] · 2 S: "EUR→USD × USD→INR = EUR→INR"

N017 · And multiply like with like. Bid with bid. Ask with ask.
> 1 B: box "bid × bid · ask × ask" [F7]

N018 · Press along. Euro-dollar one point zero eight five zero, one point zero eight five five. Dollar-rupee eighty-five point six zero, eighty-five point six two.
> 1 S: quote cards (illustrative)

N019 · Bid: one point zero eight five, times eighty-five point six. Ninety-two point eight seven six zero.
> 1 S: keys 1.085 × 85.60 = · H: 6 s · 2 B: box "EUR/INR bid 92.8760"

N020 · Ask: one point zero eight five five, times eighty-five point six two. Ninety-two point nine four zero five.
> 1 S: keys 1.0855 × 85.62 = · H: 6 s · 2 B: box "EUR/INR ask 92.9405"

N021 · Many people multiply the euro bid by the rupee ask. That gives ninety-two point eight nine seven seven. Wrong. Bid goes with bid.
> 1 C: trap card "bid × ask = 92.8977 ✗ · bid × bid = 92.8760 ✓" [F7]

N022 · Your turn. Pound-dollar one point two six five zero, one point two six six zero. Same dollar-rupee. Find pound-rupee, bid and ask. Pause.
> 1 S: card "GBP/USD 1.2650 / 1.2660 · USD/INR 85.60 / 85.62" (illustrative) · H: 20 s

N023 · Bid, one point two six five, times eighty-five point six. One hundred eight point two eight four zero. Ask, one point two six six, times eighty-five point six two. One hundred eight point three nine four nine.
> 1 B: boxes "GBP/INR 108.2840 / 108.3949"

N024 · Now rule two. Dollar-yen one forty-nine point five zero, one forty-nine point six zero. Here the dollar is the base in both quotes. Same side.
> 1 S: two scales, USD on the left in both [F8]

N025 · When the dollar is the base in both, divide. Rupees per dollar, divided by yen per dollar, gives rupees per yen.
> 1 C: card "USD the base in both → DIVIDE" [F8] · 2 S: "(INR per USD) ÷ (JPY per USD) = INR per JPY"

N026 · And here the sides cross. For the bid, divide the rupee bid by the yen ask. For the ask, divide the rupee ask by the yen bid.
> 1 B: crossed arrows "bid ÷ ask · ask ÷ bid" [F8]

N027 · Why? Ask yourself which answer must come out smaller. The bid. Dividing by the bigger yen number gives the smaller answer.
> 1 N: "bid must be the smaller → divide by the bigger yen figure" [F9]

N028 · Press along. Eighty-five point six zero, divided by one forty-nine point six zero, times a hundred, for per hundred yen. Fifty-seven point two one nine three.
> 1 S: keys 85.60 ÷ 149.60 × 100 = · H: 6 s · 2 B: box "JPY/INR bid 57.2193 per 100"

N029 · Ask: eighty-five point six two, divided by one forty-nine point five zero, times a hundred. Fifty-seven point two seven zero nine.
> 1 S: keys 85.62 ÷ 149.50 × 100 = · H: 6 s · 2 B: box "JPY/INR ask 57.2709 per 100"

N030 · If you divided bid by bid, you got fifty-seven point two five seven five. That's the trap. Check: is your bid below your ask?
> 1 C: trap card "bid ÷ bid = 57.2575 ✗" · 2 B: box "check: bid < ask" [F9]

N031 · Pause check for chapter two. One. Euro-dollar and dollar-rupee: multiply or divide?
> 1 S: card "EUR/USD & USD/INR → ?" · H: 3 s

N032 · Multiply. The dollar sits on opposite sides. Two. Dollar-rupee and dollar-yen?
> 1 N: "multiply ✓" · H: 3 s

N033 · Divide. The dollar is the base in both. Three, the twist. For the yen-rupee bid, which yen figure do you divide by?
> 1 N: "divide ✓" · H: 3 s

N034 · The yen ask, the bigger one. So the bid comes out smaller.
> 1 N: "÷ yen ask ✓" [F8]

N035 · [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter two: dollar on opposite sides, multiply, like with like. Dollar the base in both, divide, and cross the sides.
> 1 S: the two rule cards; finished parts dim to grey

## Chapter 3 · The chain rule

N036 · Chapter three. First, from chapter two. Bid times bid, or bid times ask?
> 1 S: chapter title "3 · The chain rule" · H: 3 s

N037 · Bid times bid. Now, sometimes a question gives three or four links. That's the chain rule. Think of rings, each holding the next.
> 1 N: "bid × bid ✓" · 2 S: a chain of rings [F10]

N038 · How many rupees for one pound? One pound is one point one seven euro. One euro is one point zero eight five dollars. One dollar is eighty-five point six zero rupees.
> 1 S: rings fill: "1 GBP = 1.17 EUR" · "1 EUR = 1.085 USD" · "1 USD = 85.60 INR" (illustrative)

N039 · See how each ring starts with the currency the last one ended in? Pound to euro, euro to dollar, dollar to rupee.
> 1 B: underline the joining currencies EUR, USD [F10]

N040 · So just multiply along the chain. One point one seven, times one point zero eight five, times eighty-five point six. One hundred eight point six six four nine.
> 1 S: keys 1.17 × 1.085 × 85.60 = · H: 8 s · 2 B: box "₹108.6649 per GBP"

N041 · Your turn, the other way. At that rate, how many pounds for five lakh rupees? Pause.
> 1 S: card "₹5,00,000 → GBP ?" · H: 15 s

N042 · Divide. Five lakh, divided by one hundred eight point six six four nine. Four thousand, six hundred one pounds, thirty pence.
> 1 S: keys 500000 ÷ 108.6649 = · 2 B: box "GBP 4,601.30"

N043 · [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter three: link the rings so each starts where the last one ended, then multiply along.
> 1 S: the chain; finished parts dim to grey
