Her policy pays when the buyer abroad fails. But who is the bank's borrower? The exporter herself. So the bank needs its own insurance, against her failing. E-C-G-C calls it E-C-I-B: export credit insurance for banks. Two words for that failing. Protracted default: she just doesn't repay by the due date. Insolvency: she goes bankrupt. After shipment, post-shipment covers, P-S. Then a choice. The bank covers all its export accounts in one go: whole turnover. Or it picks accounts one by one: individual. Whole turnover suits a big exporting bank, generally fifty accounts or more. Because it covers everything, cover is higher and premium lower. How much does a cover pay? Guess for the individual packing credit cover. Two-thirds. Sixty-six and two-thirds per cent. Individual post-shipment: seventy-five per cent, if she holds her own whole-turnover policy and the buyer isn't her associate. Whole-turnover covers move in a band, set by the bank's own claims record. The bands stay on the card. Her packing credit falls due, and she doesn't pay. When must the bank report the default? Within four months of the due date, or within one month of recalling the advance, whichever is earlier. Then it recalls the whole loan before claiming, and claims within six months of when the default report was due. Many of you think: the bank claims first, and does the paperwork later. Is that safe? No. A late report, no recall, or a compromise without approval, and E-C-G-C can refuse the claim. Duties come before money. The exporter's claim has its own clock. When the buyer simply doesn't pay, E-C-G-C waits four months after the due date. If the buyer goes insolvent, it can be sooner: one month after the debt is admitted. Chapter three. First, a recall. How are recoveries after a claim shared? In the same ratio as the loss. Now, insurance is one leg. Who lends for exports where ordinary banks hesitate? EXIM Bank. The Export-Import Bank of India. Set up by its own Act of nineteen eighty-one, in force from January nineteen eighty-two, head office Mumbai. Fully owned by the Government of India. Not a company, not a commercial bank: a statutory corporation. R-B-I regulates it as an all-India financial institution, like NABARD and SIDBI. Many of you think: EXIM Bank only finances exports. Its biggest tool: lines of credit. It lends to a foreign government or bank, so buyers there can import Indian goods. Many are Government of India lines, under a scheme called IDEAS. At least seventy-five per cent of the value must come from India. Next, buyer's credit: a loan to one overseas buyer, so he pays our exporter in cash, with no recourse to her. Careful. For big projects, EXIM Bank gives buyer's credit to foreign governments, backed by the National Export Insurance Account, N-E-I-A, run by E-C-G-C. At home, it gives exporters packing and post-shipment credit, term loans to export units, and loans to invest in joint ventures abroad. And for small firms with promise, a programme called Ubharte Sitaare: money plus handholding. The Act also lets it refinance banks, give guarantees, open L-Cs, and run research on trade. And it keeps an Export Development Fund, for loans banks wouldn't ordinarily give, each with the Government's prior approval. An exam favourite: which facility can't EXIM Bank give? Some of you say guarantees. Others, export credit insurance. Guarantees, it gives: the Act says so. Export credit insurance, it can't: that's E-C-G-C's job. And two limits in its Act. Deposits only for twelve months or more, no savings or current accounts. And no loans against its own bonds. Pause check for chapter three. One. EXIM Bank's Act is of which year? Nineteen eighty-one, in force from January eighty-two; work started that March. Two, the twist. Under a line of credit, whom does it lend to? The foreign side: a government or bank abroad. Our exporter simply gets paid. [seriously] One thing from chapter three: E-C-G-C insures, EXIM Bank lends.
