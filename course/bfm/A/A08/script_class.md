## Chapter 1 · Four ways to get paid

N006 · Chapter one. There are four ways to get paid in trade. Put them on a ladder, safest for the exporter at the top.
> 1 S: chapter title "1 · Four ways to get paid" · 2 S: an empty four-rung ladder

N007 · Top rung: advance payment. The buyer pays before shipment. Safest for her, riskiest for him.
> 1 S: rung 1 "advance payment" [—]

N008 · Bottom rung: open account. She ships, and he pays later. Safest for him, riskiest for her.
> 1 S: rung 4 "open account"

N009 · In between, two rungs where banks handle the documents. Quick one. Which do you think is safer for her: a collection, or a letter of credit?
> 1 S: rungs 2 and 3, blank · H: 3 s

N010 · The letter of credit. In a collection, banks only pass the documents; the buyer may still refuse. In an L-C, a bank promises to pay.
> 1 S: rung 2 "letter of credit: a bank promises" · rung 3 "collection: banks pass documents only"

N011 · Here's the promise in one line. A letter of credit is the issuing bank's definite, irrevocable undertaking to pay, against documents that comply.
> 1 C: card "LC = issuing bank's definite undertaking to honour a complying presentation · irrevocable" [F3, F4]

N012 · Irrevocable, even if the L-C doesn't say so. Under U-C-P six hundred, there is no revocable credit.
> 1 B: underline "irrevocable" · 2 C: trap card "revocable LC ✗ (UCP 600: irrevocable even if silent)" [F3]

N013 · And two ideas carry the whole subject. First, the L-C is separate from the sale contract. The bank isn't bound by what buyer and seller agreed.
> 1 C: card "Art. 4: LC independent of the sale contract" [F5]

N014 · Second, many of you think the bank checks the goods. It doesn't. Banks deal in documents, not goods.
> 1 C: card "Art. 5: documents, not goods" [F6] · 2 S: a bank officer looking at papers; the cartons faded out

N015 · Pause check for chapter one. One. Which rung is riskiest for the exporter?
> 1 S: card "riskiest for the exporter?" · H: 3 s

N016 · Open account. Two, the twist. The goods arrive damaged, but the documents comply. Must the bank pay?
> 1 N: "open account ✓" · H: 3 s

N017 · Yes. Documents, not goods. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter one: an L-C is a bank's promise, paid on documents, separate from the sale.
> 1 N: "yes: documents ✓" · 2 S: the ladder; finished parts dim to grey

## Chapter 2 · The relay

N018 · Chapter two. First, from chapter one. Does an L-C bank look at goods, or documents?
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · The relay" · H: 3 s

N019 · Documents. Now picture a relay race. The documents run one way. The money runs back the other way.
> 1 S: the relay: four figures in a row, left to right; an arrow "documents →" above, "← money" below

N020 · Start on the buyer's side. The buyer asks his bank to open the L-C. He's the applicant.
> 1 S: figure 1 "applicant (the buyer, Rotterdam)" [F2]

N021 · His bank is the issuing bank. It makes the promise, and it is bound from the moment it issues the L-C.
> 1 S: figure 2 "issuing bank: makes the promise · bound from issue" [F2, F8]

N022 · The issuing bank sends the L-C to a bank in India, near the exporter. That's the advising bank.
> 1 S: figure 3 "advising bank (India)" [F2]

N023 · And our exporter, in whose favour the L-C is opened, is the beneficiary.
> 1 S: figure 4 "beneficiary (the exporter, Tiruppur)" [F2]

N024 · Quick one. The advising bank passes on the L-C. Does it also promise to pay?
> 1 S: card "advising bank: promise to pay?" · H: 3 s

N025 · No. It only checks that the L-C looks genuine, and that its advice matches. No promise to pay.
> 1 C: card "advising bank: apparent authenticity only · no undertaking to pay" [F10]

N026 · Now, what if our exporter doesn't trust the issuing bank, or its country? She asks an Indian bank to add its own promise. That's confirmation.
> 1 S: a second promise badge pinned on figure 3

N027 · The confirming bank's promise is in addition to the issuing bank's. Now she has two banks promising. And it pays without recourse: it can't claw the money back from her.
> 1 C: card "confirming bank: definite undertaking in addition to the issuing bank's · without recourse" [F9]

N028 · Then there's the nominated bank: the bank where the L-C can be used. It may pay, accept or negotiate. But unless it has confirmed, it doesn't have to.
> 1 C: card "nominated bank: no obligation unless it confirms or expressly agrees" [F12]

N029 · Negotiation means the nominated bank buys the documents, paying her before the issuing bank pays it back.
> 1 N: "negotiation = nominated bank buys the documents, pays in advance" [F4]

N030 · And a reimbursing bank may pay the claiming bank on the issuing bank's behalf. Its rules, U-R-R seven twenty-five, come next class.
> 1 N: "reimbursing bank → URR 725 (A09)"

N031 · Who can change the L-C? Many of you think the buyer and his bank can. To amend or cancel, three must agree: issuing bank, confirming bank if any, and beneficiary.
> 1 C: card "amend / cancel: issuing bank + confirming bank (if any) + beneficiary" [F11]

N032 · And what are banks not responsible for? Whether documents are genuine, the goods themselves, delays in the post, strikes, wars. U-C-P lists these, articles thirty-four to thirty-seven.
> 1 C: card "banks not liable for: genuineness of documents, the goods (34) · transmission delays (35) · force majeure (36) · acts of an instructed party (37)" [F13]

N033 · Pause check for chapter two. One. Who asks for the L-C to be opened?
> 1 S: card "who asks?" · H: 3 s

N034 · The applicant, the buyer. Two. Which bank adds a second promise?
> 1 N: "applicant ✓" · H: 3 s

N035 · The confirming bank. Three, the twist. The advising bank finds the documents comply but doesn't pay. Has it broken a promise?

## Chapter 3 · Types by story

N037 · Chapter three. First, from chapter two. Who must agree to amend an L-C?
> 1 S: chapter title "3 · Types by story" · H: 3 s

N038 · Issuing bank, confirming bank, and beneficiary. Now, every L-C must say how it's used. Four ways.
> 1 N: "issuing + confirming + beneficiary ✓" · 2 S: four tiles "sight · acceptance · deferred payment · negotiation" [F7]

N039 · Sight: paid when compliant documents are presented. Acceptance: the bank accepts a bill of exchange, and pays at maturity.
> 1 S: tiles fill "sight: pay on presentation" · "acceptance: accept a draft, pay at maturity" [F4]

N040 · Deferred payment: also paid later, but with no bill of exchange. Just the bank's promise to pay on a date. Many mix these two up.
> 1 C: trap card "acceptance = with a draft · deferred payment = no draft" [F4]

N041 · Negotiation: a bank buys the documents and pays early. Restricted, if only one named bank may do it. Freely negotiable, if any bank may.
> 1 S: tile "negotiation: restricted (one bank) · freely negotiable (any bank)" [F7]

N042 · Now the special types, by story. A buyer reorders the same goods every month. One L-C, that refills itself after each use. A revolving L-C.
> 1 S: story card "same goods, every month" → "revolving LC (by time or value)" [F17]

N043 · An exporter needs cash to buy cotton before she ships. The L-C lets the bank advance her money before shipment. A red clause.
> 1 S: story card "advance before shipment" → "red clause" [F17]

N044 · And if that advance is secured by the goods, stored in a warehouse in the bank's name? A green clause.
> 1 C: card "red = advance before shipment · green = advance against goods warehoused in the bank's name" [F17]

N045 · Who carries the risk if she takes the red-clause advance and never ships? The issuing bank, and so the buyer who asked for the clause.
> 1 N: "red clause risk → issuing bank → applicant" [F17]

N046 · Now a trader in Mumbai. He buys from a mill in Coimbatore and sells to Dubai. He has an L-C from Dubai. How does the mill get paid?
> 1 S: story card "mill (Coimbatore) → trader (Mumbai) → buyer (Dubai)" · H: 3 s

N047 · Two answers. If the Dubai L-C says "transferable", the trader passes part of it to the mill. One credit, transferred once only.
> 1 C: card "transferable: must say 'transferable' · one credit · transferred once · to more than one second beneficiary only if partial shipments are allowed" [F14]

N048 · The trader may then swap in his own invoice, and keep the difference: his margin.
> 1 N: "first beneficiary may substitute his invoice → keeps the margin" [F14]

N049 · Or, his own bank opens a second, new L-C for the mill, backed by the Dubai one. That's back-to-back. Two separate credits.
> 1 C: trap card "transferable = one credit, passed on · back-to-back = two separate credits" [F16]

N050 · Last type. A standby L-C. Here, the bank pays only if the buyer fails to pay. It works like a guarantee.
> 1 C: card "standby LC = used as a guarantee · paid on a statement of default · UCP 600 or ISP98" [F18]

N051 · In India, an import standby needs no special list of conditions now. The twenty twenty-six guarantee rules ask that the deal is permitted, and the guarantee reported each quarter.
> 1 N: "FEM (Guarantees) Regs 2026: underlying not prohibited · reported quarterly (Form GRN)" [F21]

N052 · Pause check for chapter three. One. A bill of exchange, accepted and paid at maturity. Which L-C?
> 1 S: card "draft accepted → ?" · H: 3 s

N053 · Acceptance. Two. An advance before shipment?
> 1 N: "acceptance ✓" · H: 3 s

N054 · Red clause. Three, the twist. Two separate credits, one backing the other?
> 1 N: "red clause ✓" · H: 3 s

## Chapter 4 · What can go wrong

N056 · Chapter four. First, from chapter three. Transferable: how many times?
> 1 S: chapter title "4 · What can go wrong" · H: 3 s

N057 · Once. Now, the risks. The biggest one for an exporter: documents that don't comply. One wrong date, and the promise falls away.
> 1 S: risk card, row 1 "documents don't comply → no payment (exporter bears it)" [F4]

N058 · Second, the issuing bank or its country may fail, or block payments. That's what confirmation covers.
> 1 S: row 2 "issuing bank / country risk → confirmation covers it"

N059 · Third, fraud. The L-C is independent of the goods. Courts stop payment only for clear fraud the bank knows of.
> 1 S: row 3 "fraud: the only narrow exception to independence (case law)" [F22]

N060 · Fourth, a red-clause advance that is never repaid. The issuing bank, and its buyer, bear that.
> 1 S: row 4 "red-clause advance not repaid → issuing bank / applicant" [F17] · 2 N: "details in A10's case"

N061 · [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter four: an L-C pays on perfect paper. Get the documents right.
