## Chapter 1 · Four ways to get paid

N006 · Chapter one. There are four ways to get paid in trade. Put them on a ladder, safest for the exporter at the top.
> 1 S: chapter title "1 · Four ways to get paid" · 2 S: an empty four-rung ladder

N007 · Top rung: advance payment. The buyer pays before shipment. Safest for her, riskiest for him.
> 1 S: rung 1 "advance payment"

N008 · Bottom rung: open account. She ships, and he pays later. Safest for him, riskiest for her.
> 1 S: rung 4 "open account"

N009 · In between, two rungs where banks handle the documents. Quick one. Which do you think is safer for her: a collection, or a letter of credit?
> 1 S: rungs 2 and 3, blank · H: 3 s

N010 · The letter of credit. In a collection, banks only pass the documents; the buyer may still refuse. In an L-C, a bank promises to pay.
> 1 S: rung 2 "letter of credit: a bank promises" · rung 3 "collection: banks pass documents only"

N011 · Here's the promise in one line. An L-C is the issuing bank's definite promise to pay, against documents that comply.
> 1 C: card "LC = issuing bank's definite undertaking to honour a complying presentation" [F4]

N011a · Comply means the documents match the L-C's terms. Same goods, same amounts, same dates.
> 1 N: "comply = match the LC's terms" [F4]

N012 · U-C-P six hundred is the I-C-C's rule book for L-Cs. Each L-C says it follows it. Under it, every L-C is irrevocable, even if it doesn't say so.
> 1 C: card "UCP 600 applies when the LC says so · irrevocable even if silent" [F1, F3]

N012a · Irrevocable means it can't be cancelled or changed without the exporter agreeing. There is no revocable credit.
> 1 C: trap card "revocable LC ✗" [F3, F11]

N013 · And two ideas carry the whole subject. First, the L-C is separate from the sale contract. The bank isn't bound by what buyer and seller agreed.
> 1 C: card "Art. 4: LC independent of the sale contract" [F5]

N014 · Second, many of you think the bank checks the goods. It doesn't. Banks deal in documents, not goods.
> 1 C: card "Art. 5: documents, not goods" [F6] · 2 S: a bank officer looking at papers; the cartons faded out

N015 · Pause check for chapter one. One. Which rung is riskiest for the exporter?
> 1 S: card "riskiest for the exporter?" · H: 3 s

N016 · Open account. Two, the twist. The goods arrive damaged, but the documents comply. Must the bank pay?
> 1 N: "open account ✓" · H: 3 s

N017 · Yes. Documents, not goods. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter one: an L-C is a bank's promise, paid on documents, separate from the sale.
> 1 N: "yes: documents ✓" · 2 S: the ladder; finished parts dim to grey

## Chapter 2 · The relay

N018 · Chapter two. First, from chapter one. Does an L-C bank look at goods, or documents?
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · The relay" · H: 3 s

N019 · Documents. Now picture a relay race, with four runners in a row.
> 1 S: four figures left to right; three lanes: "L-C →" · "← documents" · "money →"

N020 · On the left, the buyer. He asks his bank to open the L-C. He's the applicant.
> 1 S: figure 1 "applicant (the buyer, Hamburg)" [F2]

N021 · His bank is the issuing bank. It makes the promise, and it is bound from the moment it issues the L-C.
> 1 S: figure 2 "issuing bank: makes the promise · bound from issue" [F2, F8]

N022 · The issuing bank sends the L-C to a bank in India, near the exporter. That's the advising bank.
> 1 S: figure 3 "advising bank (India)" [F2]

N023 · And on the right, our exporter, in whose favour the L-C is opened. She's the beneficiary.
> 1 S: figure 4 "beneficiary (the exporter, Tiruppur)" [F2]

N024 · Quick one. The advising bank passes on the L-C. Does it also promise to pay?
> 1 S: card "advising bank: promise to pay?" · H: 3 s

N025 · No. It only checks that the L-C itself looks genuine, and that its advice matches. No promise to pay.
> 1 C: card "advising bank: apparent authenticity of the LC only · no undertaking to pay" [F10]

N025a · Now watch it run. The L-C travels out: buyer, his bank, our Indian bank, the exporter.
> 1 S: the top lane lights left to right

N025b · She ships, and hands the documents to her bank: invoice, bill of lading, insurance. They run back to the issuing bank, then to the buyer.
> 1 S: the middle lane lights right to left

N025c · And the money runs out to her. The issuing bank pays, and the buyer pays his bank.
> 1 S: the bottom lane lights

N025d · Quick one. Who holds the documents at the very end?
> 1 S: card "documents end with?" · H: 3 s

N025e · The buyer. He needs them to take his goods.
> 1 N: "buyer ✓"

N026 · Now, what if she doesn't trust the issuing bank, or its country? She asks the buyer for a confirmed L-C. The issuing bank then asks an Indian bank to add its own promise.
> 1 S: a second promise badge pinned on figure 3 [F9]

N027 · The confirming bank's promise is in addition to the issuing bank's. Two banks now promise her. And if it negotiates, it's without recourse: it can't claim the money back from her.
> 1 C: card "confirming bank: added at the issuing bank's request (Arts. 2, 8) · definite undertaking in addition · bound from confirmation · negotiates without recourse" [F9]

N027a · And if the Indian bank won't confirm? It tells the issuing bank at once. It may still advise the L-C, without its promise.
> 1 N: "unwilling to confirm → inform the issuing bank · may advise without confirming" [F9]

N028 · The nominated bank is the bank where she can present documents and get paid. Often, it's the same Indian bank. Unless it has confirmed, or agreed, it doesn't have to pay.
> 1 C: card "nominated bank: no obligation unless it confirms or expressly agrees" [F12]

N029 · Negotiation means the nominated bank buys her documents, paying her before the issuing bank pays it back.
> 1 N: "negotiation = nominated bank buys the documents, pays in advance" [F4]

N030 · Sometimes a third bank pays the nominated bank, for the issuing bank. That's the reimbursing bank. More next class.
> 1 N: "reimbursing bank → URR 725 (A09)"

N031 · Who can change the L-C? Many of you think the buyer and his bank can. To amend or cancel, three must agree: issuing bank, confirming bank if any, and beneficiary.
> 1 C: card "amend / cancel: issuing bank + confirming bank (if any) + beneficiary" [F11]

N031a · Can she accept half an amendment? No. Accepting part of it means rejecting all of it. And "accepted unless rejected in five days" is simply ignored.
> 1 C: card "partial acceptance = rejection · 'deemed accepted' clause disregarded" [F11]

N032 · What are banks not responsible for? Whether her shipping papers are genuine, the goods themselves, delays in the post, strikes, wars.
> 1 C: card "banks not liable for: genuineness of documents, the goods (34) · transmission delays (35) · force majeure (36) · acts of an instructed party (37)" [F13]

N032a · Careful. The advising bank checks the L-C looks real. No bank answers for whether the shipping papers are real. Banks still check that they look right on their face.
> 1 N: "LC's authenticity (advising bank) ≠ documents' genuineness (no bank) · documents checked on their face"

N033 · Pause check for chapter two. One. Who asks for the L-C to be opened?
> 1 S: card "who asks?" · H: 3 s

N034 · The applicant, the buyer. Two. Which bank adds a second promise?
> 1 N: "applicant ✓" · H: 3 s

N035 · The confirming bank. Three, the twist. The advising bank finds the documents comply but doesn't pay. Has it broken a promise?
> 1 N: "confirming bank ✓" · H: 3 s

N036 · No. It never promised. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter two: the issuing bank promises, a confirming bank adds one, and the advising bank only passes it on.
> 1 N: "no promise to break ✓" · 2 S: the relay; finished parts dim to grey

## Chapter 3 · How she gets paid

N037 · Chapter three. First, from chapter two. Who must agree to amend an L-C?
> 1 S: chapter title "3 · How she gets paid" · H: 3 s

N038 · Issuing bank, confirming bank, and beneficiary. Now a quick refresher from JAIIB. A bill of exchange, or draft, is a written order to pay.
> 1 N: "issuing + confirming + beneficiary ✓" · 2 C: card "bill of exchange = draft · at sight = paid when shown · usance = paid later, at maturity"

N038a · At sight, it's paid when shown. Usance means paid later, on its due date, the maturity. Every L-C must say which of four ways it pays.
> 1 S: four tiles "sight · acceptance · deferred payment · negotiation" [F7]

N039 · Sight: paid when compliant documents are presented. Acceptance: the bank signs her draft, promising to pay at maturity. That's accepting it.
> 1 S: tiles fill "sight: pay on presentation" · "acceptance: accept a draft, pay at maturity" [F4]

N040 · Deferred payment: also paid later, but with no draft. Just the bank's promise to pay on a date. Many mix these two up.
> 1 C: trap card "acceptance = with a draft · deferred payment = no draft" [F4]

N040a · One rule on drafts. An L-C can't ask for a draft drawn on the applicant. Drafts go on a bank.
> 1 C: trap card "draft drawn on the applicant ✗ (Art. 6c)" [F7]

N041 · Negotiation: a bank buys the documents and pays early. Restricted, if only one named bank may do it. Freely negotiable, if any bank may.
> 1 S: tile "negotiation: restricted (one bank) · freely negotiable (any bank)" [F7]

N041a · Pause check for chapter three. Paid later, but with no draft?
> 1 S: card "later, no draft → ?" · H: 3 s

N041b · Deferred payment. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter three: acceptance has a draft; deferred payment has none.
> 1 N: "deferred payment ✓" · 2 S: finished parts dim to grey

## Chapter 4 · Special types, by story

N042 · Chapter four. First, from chapter three. A bank buys her documents and pays early. What is that?
> 1 S: chapter title "4 · Special types, by story" · H: 3 s

N042a · Negotiation. Now, stories. A buyer reorders the same goods every month. Must he open a new L-C each time?
> 1 N: "negotiation ✓" · 2 S: story card "same goods, every month" · H: 3 s

N042b · No. One L-C that refills itself after each use. A revolving L-C.
> 1 S: "→ revolving LC (by time or value)" [F17]

N043 · She needs cash to buy cotton before she ships. Can the L-C help?
> 1 S: story card "cash before shipment?" · H: 3 s

N043a · Yes. A red clause lets the bank advance her money before shipment. If that advance is secured by goods warehoused in the bank's name, it's a green clause.
> 1 C: card "red = advance before shipment · green = advance against goods warehoused in the bank's name" [F17]

N045 · Who carries the risk if she takes the red-clause advance and never ships? The issuing bank, and so the buyer who asked for the clause.
> 1 N: "red clause risk → issuing bank → applicant" [F17]

N046 · Now a trader in Mumbai. He buys from a mill in Coimbatore and sells to Dubai. He has an L-C from Dubai. How does the mill get paid?
> 1 S: story card "mill (Coimbatore) → trader (Mumbai) → buyer (Dubai)" · H: 3 s

N047 · Two answers. If the Dubai L-C says "transferable", the trader passes part of it to the mill. He's the first beneficiary; the mill, the second.
> 1 C: card "transferable: must say 'transferable' · bank transfers only if it consents · transferred once" [F14]

N047a · It can be transferred only once. To more than one mill? Only if the L-C allows partial shipments.
> 1 N: "more than one second beneficiary → only if partial drawings or shipments allowed" [F14]

N047b · The transfer may cut the amount, the unit price and the dates. Only the insurance percentage may go up.
> 1 C: card "transfer may reduce: amount, unit price, expiry, presentation period, last shipment date · may increase: insurance %" [F14]

N048 · The mill's invoice is cheaper. So the trader replaces it with his own invoice, and keeps the difference. That's his margin.
> 1 N: "first beneficiary substitutes his invoice → keeps the margin" [F14]

N049 · Or, his own bank opens a second, new L-C for the mill, backed by the Dubai one. That's back-to-back. Two separate credits.
> 1 C: trap card "transferable = one credit, passed on · back-to-back = two separate credits" [F16]

N050 · Last type. A standby L-C. The buyer pays the seller as usual. The bank pays only if he fails, when the seller sends a statement of default.
> 1 C: card "standby LC = used as a guarantee · paid on a statement of default" [F18]

N050a · So it works like a guarantee. It can follow U-C-P six hundred, or its own rules, I-S-P ninety-eight.
> 1 N: "standby: UCP 600 or ISP98" [F18]

N051 · Many older notes list special R-B-I conditions for import standbys. Not any more. Under the twenty twenty-six guarantee rules, two things matter.
> 1 C: trap card "old RBI list of import-SBLC conditions ✗ (FEM (Guarantees) Regs 2026)" [F21]

N051a · The deal behind it must be allowed under FEMA. And the issuing bank reports it every quarter, in Form G-R-N.
> 1 N: "underlying not prohibited · reported quarterly, Form GRN" [F21]

N053 · Red clause. Two. Advance secured by goods in the bank's name?
> 1 N: "red clause ✓" · H: 3 s

> 1 N: "green clause ✓" · H: 3 s

## Chapter 5 · What can go wrong

N056 · Chapter five. First, from chapter four. Transferable: how many times?
> 1 S: chapter title "5 · What can go wrong" · H: 3 s

N057 · Once. Now, the risks. The biggest for an exporter: documents that don't comply. Then the bank may refuse to pay. She can correct them before expiry, or the buyer may waive.
> 1 S: risk card, row 1 "documents don't comply → bank may refuse · re-present before expiry, or buyer waives (exporter's risk)" [F4]

N058 · Second, the issuing bank or its country may fail, or block payments. That's what confirmation covers.
> 1 S: row 2 "issuing bank / country risk → exporter's, unless confirmed"

N059 · Many of you think: the goods were bad, so the buyer can always stop payment. No. Courts stop it only for clear fraud the bank knows of.
> 1 S: row 3 "fraud: a narrow exception to independence (case law)" [F22]

N060 · Fourth, a red-clause advance that is never repaid. The issuing bank, and its buyer, bear that.
> 1 S: row 4 "red-clause advance not repaid → issuing bank / applicant" [F17] · 2 N: "details in A10's case"

N060a · Pause check for chapter five. Which risk does confirmation cover?
