[warmly] Hi there, everyone. Today we follow the paper, not the goods. Last class, our Tiruppur exporter's buyer opened an L-C, a letter of credit. Today: what exactly is that promise? L-C questions came in six of the seven exam cycles we studied. Now she has a new buyer, in Hamburg. T-shirts worth sixty thousand dollars. She has never met him. Some of you would say, ship first and trust him. Others, ask for the money first. But then he may walk away. What would you advise? Hold that. By the end of the class, you'll give her a third answer, and name the exact kind. Chapter one. There are four ways to get paid in trade. Put them on a ladder, safest for the exporter at the top. Top rung: advance payment. The buyer pays before shipment. Safest for her, riskiest for him. Bottom rung: open account. She ships, and he pays later. Safest for him, riskiest for her. In between, two rungs where banks handle the documents. Quick one. Which do you think is safer for her: a collection, or a letter of credit? The letter of credit. In a collection, banks only pass the documents; the buyer may still refuse. In an L-C, a bank promises to pay. Here's the promise in one line. An L-C is the issuing bank's definite promise to pay, against documents that comply. Comply means the documents meet the L-C's terms. The right description, amounts and dates, on paper. U-C-P six hundred is the I-C-C's rule book for L-Cs. Each L-C says it follows it. Under it, every L-C is irrevocable, even if it doesn't say so. Irrevocable means it can't be cancelled or changed without the exporter agreeing. There is no revocable credit. And two ideas carry the whole subject. First, the L-C is separate from the sale contract. The bank isn't bound by what buyer and seller agreed. Second, many of you think the bank checks the goods. It doesn't. Banks deal in documents, not goods. Pause check for chapter one. One. Which rung is riskiest for the exporter? Open account. Two, the twist. The goods arrive damaged, but the documents comply. Must the bank pay? Yes. Documents, not goods. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter one: an L-C is a bank's promise, paid on documents, separate from the sale. Chapter two. First, from chapter one. Does an L-C bank look at goods, or documents? Documents. Now picture a relay race, with four runners in a row. On the left, the buyer. He asks his bank to open the L-C. He's the applicant. His bank is the issuing bank. It makes the promise, and it is bound from the moment it issues the L-C. The issuing bank sends the L-C to a bank in India, near the exporter. That's the advising bank. And on the right, our exporter, in whose favour the L-C is opened. She's the beneficiary. Quick one. The advising bank passes on the L-C. Does it also promise to pay? No. It only checks that the L-C itself looks genuine, and that its advice matches. No promise to pay. Now watch it run. The L-C travels out: buyer, his bank, our Indian bank, the exporter. She ships, and hands the documents to her bank: invoice, bill of lading, insurance. They run back to the issuing bank, then to the buyer. And the money runs out to her. The issuing bank pays, and the buyer pays his bank. Quick one. Who holds the documents at the very end? The buyer. He needs them to take his goods. Now, what if she doesn't trust the issuing bank, or its country? She asks the buyer for a confirmed L-C. The issuing bank then asks an Indian bank to add its own promise. The confirming bank's promise is in addition to the issuing bank's. Two banks now promise her. And if it negotiates, it's without recourse: it can't claim the money back from her. And if the Indian bank won't confirm? It tells the issuing bank at once. It may still advise the L-C, without its promise. The nominated bank is the bank where she can present documents and get paid. Often, it's the same Indian bank. Unless it has confirmed, or agreed, it doesn't have to pay. Negotiation means the nominated bank buys her documents, paying her before the issuing bank pays it back. Sometimes a third bank pays the nominated bank, for the issuing bank. That's the reimbursing bank. More next class. Who can change the L-C? Many of you think the buyer and his bank can. To amend or cancel, three must agree: issuing bank, confirming bank if any, and beneficiary. Can she accept half an amendment? No. Accepting part of it means rejecting all of it. And "accepted unless rejected in five days" is simply ignored. What are banks not responsible for? Whether her shipping papers are genuine, the goods themselves, delays in the post, strikes, wars. Careful. The advising bank checks the L-C looks real. No bank answers for whether the shipping papers are real. Banks still check that they look right on their face. Pause check for chapter two. One. Who asks for the L-C to be opened? The applicant, the buyer. Two. Which bank adds a second promise? The confirming bank. Three, the twist. The advising bank finds the documents comply but doesn't pay. Has it broken a promise? No. It never promised. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter two: the issuing bank promises, a confirming bank adds one, and the advising bank only passes it on. Chapter three. First, from chapter two. Who must agree to amend an L-C? Issuing bank, confirming bank, and beneficiary. Now a quick refresher from JAIIB. A bill of exchange, or draft, is a written order to pay. At sight, it's paid when shown. Usance means paid later, on its due date, the maturity. Every L-C must say which of four ways it pays. Sight: paid when compliant documents are presented. Acceptance: the bank signs her draft, promising to pay at maturity. That's accepting it. Deferred payment: also paid later, but with no draft. Just the bank's promise to pay on a date. Many mix these two up. Paying in any of these first three ways, the rules call honour. One rule on drafts. An L-C can't ask for a draft drawn on the applicant. Drafts go on a bank. Negotiation: a bank buys the documents and pays early. Restricted, if only one named bank may do it. Freely negotiable, if any bank may. Pause check for chapter three. Paid later, but with no draft? Deferred payment. Two, the twist. Can an L-C ask for a draft drawn on the buyer? No. Drafts go on a bank. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter three: acceptance has a draft; deferred payment has none. Chapter four. First, from chapter three. A bank buys her documents and pays early. What is that? Negotiation. Now, stories. A buyer reorders the same goods every month. Must he open a new L-C each time? No. One L-C that refills itself after each use. A revolving L-C. She needs cash to buy cotton before she ships. Can the L-C help? Yes. A red clause lets the bank advance her money before shipment. If that advance is secured by goods warehoused in the bank's name, it's a green clause. Who carries the risk if she takes the red-clause advance and never ships? Think. The issuing bank, and so the buyer who asked for the clause. Now a trader in Mumbai. He buys from a mill in Coimbatore and sells to Dubai. He has an L-C from Dubai. How does the mill get paid? Two answers. If the Dubai L-C says "transferable", the trader asks his bank to pass part of it to the mill. He's the first beneficiary; the mill, the second. It can be transferred only once. To more than one mill? Only if the L-C allows partial drawings or shipments. The transfer may cut the amount, the unit price and the dates. Only the insurance percentage may go up. The mill's invoice is cheaper. So the trader replaces it with his own invoice, and keeps the difference. That's his margin. Or, his own bank opens a second, new L-C for the mill, backed by the Dubai one. That's back-to-back. Two separate credits. Last type. A standby L-C. The buyer pays the seller as usual. The bank pays only if he fails, when the seller sends a statement of default. So it works like a guarantee. It can follow U-C-P six hundred, or its own rules, I-S-P ninety-eight. Many older notes list special R-B-I conditions for import standbys. Not any more. Under the twenty twenty-six guarantee rules, two things matter. The deal behind it must be allowed under FEMA. And the issuing bank reports it every quarter, in Form G-R-N. Pause check for chapter four. One. An advance before shipment? Red clause. Two. Advance secured by goods in the bank's name? Green clause. Three, the twist. Two separate credits, one backing the other? Back-to-back. [seriously] If you remember one thing from chapter four: read the story, then name the L-C. Each type answers one need.

Chapter five. First, from chapter four. Transferable: how many times? Once. Now, the risks. The biggest for an exporter: documents that don't comply. Then the bank may refuse to pay.
