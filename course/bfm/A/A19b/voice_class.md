[warmly] Good day, everyone. Last class we crossed the borrowing bridge; today we take the equity lane. Recall from last class. Under the twenty twenty-six E-C-B overhaul, what is the standard minimum average maturity? Three years, and a ceiling of one billion dollars or three hundred per cent of net worth. Earlier, under FEMA section six two-A, who makes rules for non-debt capital deals? The Central Government notifies Non-Debt Instruments Rules; R-B-I administers them and regulates debt. Today's case: Meridian Ventures of Singapore wants to invest in Sahyadri Sensors, an unlisted Pune startup. Meridian puts five crore into equity for eight per cent, plus thirty lakh in convertible notes. Your branch gets the funds. Some say eight per cent is below ten, so F-P-I. Others say unlisted is always F-D-I. Which side are you on, and what else matters? Hold that answer. Today we clear every toll booth on India's equity lane. Chapter one. Foreign investment follows the Non-Debt Instruments Rules, twenty nineteen, updated to September twenty twenty-six. Non-debt covers nine classes: company equity, L-L-P capital, R-E-I-Ts, Inv-I-Ts, A-I-Fs, property, and equity receipts. Mutual funds or E-T-Fs over fifty per cent equity are non-debt; fifty per cent or less, debt. G-Secs, corporate bonds, and commercial paper sit under R-B-I's debt regulations. Quick check: an Indian mutual fund holds sixty-five per cent equity. Non-debt or debt? Non-debt—equity exceeds fifty per cent. Now define foreign investment. Foreign investment is repatriable non-resident investment in Indian company equity or L-L-P capital. Resident-held shares with declared non-resident beneficial interest also count. Now the classic trap: F-D-I versus F-P-I. Many think ten per cent separates them in every company. Not in unlisted companies. Any repatriable equity stake in an unlisted company is F-D-I, even one per cent. Remember my opening question on Meridian's eight per cent stake? Sahyadri is unlisted, so it is F-D-I. The ten per cent line applies only to listed companies, tested on a fully diluted basis. Ten per cent or more is F-D-I; less than ten is F-P-I. What does fully diluted mean? Count every share outstanding if all convertible debentures, preference shares, warrants, and options converted today. Let's calculate. Listed Apex Auto has eighty lakh equity shares and twenty lakh one-for-one C-C-Ds. An F-P-I holds nine lakh shares. Dividing nine by eighty gives eleven point two five per cent—wrong. Add diluted shares: eighty plus twenty is one hundred lakh. Nine over one hundred is nine per cent—F-P-I. Faded check: eighty lakh shares plus ten lakh warrants converting one-for-one. F-P-I holds nine lakh shares. Diluted total is ninety lakh. Nine divided by ninety is ten per cent—that crosses into F-D-I. Your turn. Sixty lakh equity shares plus fifteen lakh C-C-P-S converting one-for-one. F-P-I buys eight lakh shares. F-D-I or F-P-I? Sixty plus fifteen is seventy-five lakh. Eight over seventy-five times one hundred is ten point six seven per cent—F-D-I. Once an investment is F-D-I, it stays F-D-I forever, even if it later drops below ten per cent. If an F-P-I breaches ten per cent, R-B-I's November twenty twenty-four circular gives five trading days from settlement. It divests the excess or reclassifies its entire stake as F-D-I—never in prohibited sectors. Reclassification needs prior Government approval if applicable and company concurrence beforehand; otherwise divestment is compulsory. Now the N-D-I snapshot: routes, caps, and pricing. Entry is automatic—no prior approval—or Government route via F-I-F-P. Unlisted non-prohibited sectors default to one hundred per cent automatic, except unlisted financial services. Twenty twenty-six update: Indian insurance companies moved to one hundred per cent, automatic route. Pricing rule: floor on entry, ceiling on exit, and never an assured exit price for a foreign investor. Unlisted fair value needs a C-A, SEBI Merchant Banker, or Cost Accountant certificate, valid ninety days. Pause check for Chapter one. First: five per cent equity in an unlisted company—F-D-I or F-P-I? F-D-I, because unlisted equity is always F-D-I. Second: a twelve per cent F-D-I stake drops to seven. What now? Still F-D-I. Third: how many trading days to divest an unapproved F-P-I breach? Five trading days from settlement. One thing to remember: ten per cent diluted tests listed companies; unlisted is always F-D-I. Chapter two. Recall from Chapter one: how many days is an unlisted-share valuation certificate valid? Ninety days. Now step up to toll booth one: who may invest under the N-D-I Rules? Any non-resident may invest under Schedule One, subject to five country and investor rules. One: under Press Note Three, any land-border entity, citizen, or P-M-L-A beneficial owner needs Government approval. Even a Singapore firm triggers this if beneficially owned from a land-border country—check Meridian. May twenty twenty-six carve-out: Multilateral Banks where India is a member are exempt. Two: Pakistan investors need Government approval and are barred from defence, space, atomic energy, and prohibited sectors. Pakistan and Bangladesh investors are barred from L-L-Ps, investment vehicles, and convertible notes; E-S-O-Ps need approval. Three: Nepal and Bhutan citizens, and N-R-Is there, must remit free foreign exchange for repatriable F-D-I. Four: O-C-Bs were derecognised sixteen September two thousand three; cleared ones invest as ordinary foreign companies. Five: a June twenty twenty-six Schedule Three overhaul for individual portfolio investors. Since twelve June twenty twenty-six, any individual non-resident may buy listed shares repatriably: below ten individually, twenty-four aggregate. N-R-I and O-C-I non-repatriation investments under Schedule Four have no limit and count as deemed domestic. Institutions: F-P-Is under SEBI's twenty nineteen regulations—not twenty fourteen—and F-V-C-Is under SEBI's two thousand rules. F-V-C-Is invest in ten specified sectors, startups in any sector, and Category-One A-I-Fs, at mutually agreed prices. Now toll booth two: four eligible investee entities—Indian companies, L-L-Ps, startups, and SEBI-regulated investment vehicles. Partnerships and proprietorships can receive money only from N-R-Is or O-C-Is on a non-repatriation basis. L-L-Ps allow FDI only in one hundred per cent automatic sectors without performance conditions—no F-P-Is or F-V-C-Is. Investment vehicles mean R-E-I-Ts, Inv-I-Ts, and A-I-Fs; old nineteen ninety-six venture capital funds do not qualify. Now startups—a big February twenty twenty-six update. Under N-D-I Rules, a startup company is a D-P-I-I-T-recognised private company. From February twenty twenty-six, startup turnover doubled to two hundred crore in ten years; Deep Tech: twenty years, three hundred crore. Downstream investment is when a foreign-invested Indian entity invests in another Indian entity. If foreign owned or controlled, that stake is indirect foreign investment—no domestic borrowings allowed. Bank shares acquired through loan restructuring or defaults are exempt; only strategic subsidiaries or joint ventures count. Second, foreign technology collaboration means foreign technology transfer, including franchise, trademark, brand-name, or management-contract licensing. Pause check for Chapter two. First: can an F-P-I invest in an L-L-P in a one hundred per cent automatic sector? No, F-P-Is are barred from L-L-Ps. Second: the twenty twenty-six regular startup turnover cap? Two hundred crore—three hundred for Deep Tech. Third: can foreign-owned firms use domestic loans downstream? No, only overseas funds or internal accruals. One thing to remember: check country and beneficial owner before checking the sector. Chapter three. Recall from Chapter two: how are Schedule Four N-R-I non-repatriation investments treated for sectoral caps? As deemed domestic investment, outside sectoral caps. Now toll booth three: eligible investment instruments. Rule two-K names four equity instruments: equity shares, compulsorily convertible debentures, compulsorily convertible preference shares, and share warrants. Trap: optionally, partially, or non-convertible debentures and preference shares are debt under E-C-B rules, not N-D-I. Write this down once, because exams constantly swap these two timelines. Partly paid shares need twenty-five per cent upfront, including premium, and balance in twelve months. Share warrants also need twenty-five per cent upfront, price fixed upfront, balance in eighteen months. Shares call in twelve months; warrants wait eighteen. Any optionality clause needs a one-year lock-in and no assured exit price. Now check Meridian's thirty lakh in convertible notes for Sahyadri. Who may issue them, and what are the two numbers? Only a D-P-I-I-T startup, for at least twenty-five lakh single tranche, convertible or repayable within ten years.
