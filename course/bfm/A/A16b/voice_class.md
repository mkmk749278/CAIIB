L-R-S stands on a few sections of FEMA, the foreign exchange law. First, the starting rule. Section four: a resident may not hold foreign exchange, foreign securities or property abroad, unless the Act allows it. So the ban comes first, and L-R-S is a door through it. Which section opens the capital door? Section six. Capital deals only as permitted: R-B-I sets the debt ones, the Government the rest, like shares. Now the counter. Section ten, sub-section five. Ten-five: before any forex deal, the bank takes her declaration that it breaks no rule. Then ten-six. She buys dollars for his fees, but spends them on lottery tickets there. What has she done? Broken the law. Ten-six covers three things: not using it as declared, using it for a barred purpose, or not surrendering it in time. Surrendering means selling unused foreign exchange back to a bank or money changer. And switching to another permitted purpose? R-B-I allows that. Who disciplines the banks? Section eleven: R-B-I directs them, and can fine a bank up to ten thousand rupees, plus two thousand a day. Anyone at all? [seriously] One thing from chapter one: four bans, six permits, ten declares, thirteen fines. Chapter two. First, a recall. Shares abroad aren't debt. Investments abroad. And a loan to a relative who lives abroad. All four share her one jar of two lakh fifty thousand dollars. And no bank credit for any of them. Investments come in two kinds. Portfolio: a few shares of a listed U-S company. That's Overseas Portfolio Investment, O-P-I. Direct: a real stake in a business. Overseas Direct Investment, O-D-I. Now Karthik's second plan. He has gone abroad to study, so for FEMA he's a non-resident. Can she lend him rupees? Yes, under L-R-S. Interest-free, for at least one year, paid into his N-R-O account: his rupee account in India as a non-resident. Some say "for at most one year". No: one year is the minimum. Where can he spend it? Only in India: his own needs, or his business here. It can't be sent abroad. One more door. GIFT City has India's International Financial Services Centre, the I-F-S-C. Pause check for chapter two. One. Ten per cent of a listed foreign company: O-P-I or O-D-I? O-D-I. Two, the twist. Karthik's five lakh loan, to pay his rent in the U-S. Allowed? Chapter three. First, a recall. The rupee loan to an N-R-I relative: the minimum period? One year. Now a puzzle. She sends money through two banks. How does the second bank know what the first sent? Next day, same trip, he buys three hundred dollars more. Cash again? No. Eight hundred and ninety dollars for the trip: seventy-five thousand, two hundred and ninety-four rupees. The limit counts the whole trip, not each purchase. Splitting it to stay below is what auditors look for. He comes home with two thousand six hundred dollars in notes, and some coins. What must he do? He may keep up to two thousand dollars in notes and traveller's cheques, for his next trip. Coins, any amount. The other six hundred he must surrender, that is, sell to a bank or money changer, within one hundred and eighty days of coming home. Older books say ninety days for notes. Today that's only for firms and companies. For an individual: one hundred and eighty. Pause check for chapter four. One. Notes for a trip to Dubai: at most? Three thousand dollars. Two, the twist. A year later he still holds fifteen hundred dollars in notes. Must he surrender them? No: within the two thousand he may keep. [seriously] One thing from chapter four: three thousand out, below fifty thousand in cash, two thousand kept. Back to our opening. The start-up stake, and the loan for his rent. Both fine? The stake, yes: O-D-I in an operating software company, from her own money, inside her jar. The loan, yes, but not for rent abroad. Quick mix. One. Section thirteen penalty, when the sum is known? Up to three times the sum. Two. Idle money in her GIFT City account: back within how long? One hundred and eighty days, unless reinvested. Three. Notes for a trip to Libya? Up to five thousand dollars. Now the map, from memory. First node: FEMA sections. Four bans, six permits, ten-five declares, ten-six catches misuse, eleven fines banks, thirteen fines anyone, fifteen settles. Second: capital uses. Account, property, O-D-I or O-P-I, a loan to a relative; one jar, no credit; O-D-I only in operating, non-financial companies. Third: reporting. Daily return by the next working day, nil if none; employer travel and most company payments sit outside L-R-S. Fourth: travel forex. Three thousand dollars in notes, five for Iraq and Libya; cash below fifty thousand; keep two thousand; surrender the rest in one-eighty days. Homework. This year: sixty thousand dollars for fees, forty thousand into the start-up, and a loan worth fifty thousand dollars. What's left in her jar? Next class, people who live abroad, and the four accounts they can hold in India. [warmly] That's the rest of the remittance story. Rest your eyes now, and I'll see you in the next class.
