# A1 · Buying or selling? The bank's counter and the rate ladder

- **Module / unit**: BFM Module A, Unit A1 (Exchange Rates and Forex Business: forex arithmetic)
- **Importance**: Tier A (forex arithmetic recalled in 7 of 7 exams, Jun 2023 – Jun 2026)
- **Rule version**: FEDAI Rules, 10th edition, as in force on 30 June 2026 (Nov/Dec 2026 exam cut-off)
- **Length**: about 12 minutes (1,250 words of narration plus calculator and practice pauses) · **Numbers**: `numericals.md` (checked by `tools/check/fx_merchant_rates.py`)
- Names, places and rates in this lesson are **illustrative**; the on-screen label says so.

---

### 1 · The branch moment (0:00–0:45)

**[Picture]** Monday, 11 a.m., the forex counter of a branch in Visakhapatnam. Label: ILLUSTRATIVE. Two customers in
the queue. A rate board behind the officer shows four numbers for the US dollar: 85.2719 · 85.3573 · 85.5054 ·
85.6337.

Two customers, one counter, one currency.

The first is a father. His daughter's university in the US wants its fee, eighteen thousand dollars, this week.

The second runs a shrimp export unit near the harbour. He has a sight bill for forty thousand dollars, and he wants
rupees today, not in three weeks.

**[Picture]** The four rates pulse. Two question marks, one over each customer.

Same dollar. Same morning. Four different prices on the board.

Which price does the father pay? Which price does the exporter get? By the end of this lesson you will answer both
in five seconds, and you will never mix them up again.

### 2 · The counter (0:45–3:00)

**[Picture]** Top view of the counter. A line down the middle: the bank on one side, the customer on the other.
Dollars slide across. Arrows into the bank glow green; arrows out of the bank glow red.

Start with one picture. The counter.

Stand on the bank's side. Always the bank's side, because the bank sets the price.

When dollars come **into** the bank, the bank is **buying** dollars. It pays rupees for them.

When dollars go **out** of the bank, the bank is **selling** dollars. It takes rupees for them.

**[Picture]** Green arrow in: "Bank BUYS → pays fewer rupees". Red arrow out: "Bank SELLS → charges more rupees".

Any shopkeeper does the same. He buys stock cheap and sells it dearer. The gap is how he earns.

So the bank's buying rate is always the lower rupee number. Its selling rate is always the higher one.

Now look at our two customers.

**[Picture]** The father's dollars leave the bank for the US: red arrow out.

The father needs dollars to send abroad. Dollars leave the bank. The bank sells. He pays the higher, selling side.

**[Picture]** The exporter's bill comes in; dollars will arrive later: green arrow in.

The exporter brings dollars in, through his bill. The bank buys. He gets the lower, buying side.

That is half the answer already. But there are four rates on the board, not two. So which selling rate, and which
buying rate?

### 3 · How the bank writes a price (3:00–4:30)

**[Picture]** A wholesale market screen: "USD/INR 85.40 / 85.42". Then the counter board below it.

First, how a rate is written.

In India, a bank quotes a customer in **direct terms**: so many rupees and paise for one unit, or a hundred units,
of foreign currency. That is FEDAI's Rule 5.7. So "85.40" means 85 rupees 40 paise for one dollar.

Between banks, the dollar trades with two prices side by side. Here, 85.40 and 85.42.

**[Picture]** 85.40 lights green, "bid: the price at which the market buys"; 85.42 lights red, "ask or offer: the
price at which it sells".

The first is the bid: what a buyer pays. The second is the ask, also called the offer: what a seller wants.

This is the wholesale price, the interbank rate. The counter is retail. So the bank adds its own margin before it
quotes you.

How big is that margin? FEDAI does not fix it. Each bank decides its own charges. In the exam, the question always
tells you the margin. Your job is to know where it goes.

### 4 · TT or bill: the rate ladder (4:30–7:30)

**[Picture]** A vertical ladder. The interbank quote sits in the middle rung. Two rungs above in red, two below in
green.

Here is the second picture. The rate ladder.

In the middle sits the interbank quote. Selling rates climb **up** from the ask. Buying rates step **down** from the
bid.

**[Picture]** First rung down: "TT buying". First rung up: "TT selling".

The nearest rungs are the **TT rates**. TT means telegraphic transfer: money that moves by message, at once.

TT buying is for dollars already sitting in the bank's account abroad, its Nostro account. The bank pays you rupees
and has nothing to wait for.

TT selling is for dollars the bank sends out straight away, like a fee remittance.

**[Picture]** Second rung down: "Bill buying". Second rung up: "Bill selling". Each with a little clock and a
document icon.

The outer rungs are the **bill rates**.

Bill buying is when the bank pays you rupees **today** for an export bill whose dollars arrive **later**. The bank
waits and carries the risk. So it buys at a lower price than TT buying.

Bill selling is for import bills: the bank pays the foreign supplier against documents and handles the paperwork. So
it sells at a higher price than TT selling.

**[Picture]** The ladder complete, top to bottom: Bill selling · TT selling · interbank · TT buying · Bill buying.

Read the ladder once from top to bottom. Bill selling is the most expensive dollar. Bill buying is the cheapest.
Bills sit outside, TTs sit inside, the market sits in the middle.

### 5 · The rate map: which rung for which deal (7:30–9:30)

**[Picture]** The counter again, now with rungs attached to each kind of deal. Green and red tags appear one by one.
Source line on screen: FEDAI Rules 2, 3, 4 and 6.

Now the deals. FEDAI's rules tell us which rung each one uses.

Money coming in:
- An **inward remittance**, a TT from abroad converted to rupees: **TT buying**. Rule 4.4.
- An **export bill purchased, discounted or negotiated**: **bill buying**. Rule 2.1(a).
- An **export bill sent for collection**: the bank waits for the money and converts it only after the dollars reach
  its Nostro. So **TT buying**. Rule 2.5(a).

Money going out:
- An **outward remittance**: **TT selling**. Rule 4.1.
- An **import bill retired** without a forward contract: **bill selling**. Rule 3.1(a).

**[Picture]** An amber arrow reverses a green one.

And one twist, in amber. Reversing a deal puts you on the other side of the counter.

If the bank bought an export bill and the dollars never come, it turns the dollar loan into a rupee loan. That is
crystallisation. It uses **TT selling**: the customer now owes the bank dollars, so it is as if the bank sells him
those dollars. Rule 2.1(b).

The same logic cancels forward contracts: a purchase contract is cancelled at **TT selling**, a sale contract at
**TT buying**. Rule 6.4. We will work those sums in lesson A4.

### 6 · Work it out (9:30–11:30)

**[Picture]** Data card: Interbank USD/INR 85.40 / 85.42. Bank's margins: TT buying 0.05%, bill buying 0.15%,
TT selling 0.10%, bill selling a further 0.15% over TT selling. Label: ILLUSTRATIVE. A basic calculator sits at the
side; keys light as they are named.

Let us build the board ourselves. The data is on screen.

Buying rates start from the bid, 85.40, and go **down**.

TT buying: press 85.40, times, 0.05, percent. The margin is 0.0427. Take it away: 85.40 minus 0.0427 is
**85.3573**.

Bill buying: 85.40 times 0.15 percent is 0.1281. Take it away: **85.2719**.

Selling rates start from the ask, 85.42, and go **up**.

TT selling: 85.42 times 0.10 percent is 0.08542. Add it: 85.50542, which we show as **85.5054**.

Bill selling: the bank loads 0.15 percent **on the TT selling rate**. 85.50542 times 0.15 percent is about 0.1283.
Add it: **85.6337**.

**[Picture]** The four answers fly onto the ladder rungs. They match the board from the opening.

Those are the four numbers on the board in the opening scene.

**[Picture]** Back at the counter. The father: USD 18,000 × 85.5054. The exporter: USD 40,000 × 85.2719.

The father sends eighteen thousand dollars by TT. The bank sells, TT selling. Eighteen thousand times 85.5054 is
**₹15,39,097.20**.

The exporter's sight bill is bought by the bank. Bill buying. Forty thousand times 85.2719 is **₹34,10,876.00**,
before the interest for the transit period, which the bank also recovers upfront. That is lesson A5.

Five seconds each, as promised.

### 7 · Trap cards (11:30–12:30)

**[Picture]** Split card. Left in grey: the wrong answer. Right in colour: the right one.

**Trap one.** "The father is buying dollars, so use the buying rate." Wrong side of the counter. The customer buys,
so the bank **sells**: TT selling. Always name the deal from the bank's side.

**Trap two.** "The export bill came back unpaid, the dollars were coming in, so TT buying." No. Reversing a purchase
means the bank sells: **TT selling**. Amber always flips the side.

**Trap three.** Adding the margin to a buying rate. Buying rates go **down** from the bid. Selling rates go **up**
from the ask. If your buying rate is higher than your selling rate, stop and check.

### 8 · Practice: pause now (12:30–13:15)

**[Picture]** Three questions on screen with a five-second countdown each. Answers after the pause.

1. A customer receives USD 2,500 from his son abroad. Which rate? → **TT buying**: dollars come in, already in the
   Nostro. At our rates, ₹2,13,393.25.
2. An importer retires a USD 12,000 bill and has no forward contract. Which rate? → **Bill selling**: ₹10,27,604.40.
3. Put these in order from highest to lowest: TT buying, bill selling, bill buying, TT selling. → **Bill selling,
   TT selling, TT buying, bill buying.**
