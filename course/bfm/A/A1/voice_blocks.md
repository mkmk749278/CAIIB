# A1 voice blocks (for the owner to generate in the ElevenLabs app)

No API use until the video is final (owner, 2026-11-14). Generate **two audio files**, one per voice, from the blocks
below, and put them in the Drive folder `CAIIB/BFM/A1/voice/` named `narrator.mp3` and `meera.mp3` (or send them in
chat). Each block is under 10,000 characters, so one generation each.

**How to generate**
- Copy everything inside the box, exactly as it is. Keep the blank lines between paragraphs: each blank line is one
  spoken line, and the pause after it lets the video cut the lines apart.
- Narrator: a calm, warm Indian-English voice (male or female), not Akash, so the course sounds different from Be
  Practical. Meera: a younger, lively Indian-English female voice.
- Latest model, the same settings for the whole block; download as MP3.
- If a line sounds wrong, regenerate the whole block rather than splicing.

## Block 1 · NARRATOR (58 lines, 5671 characters)
```
Same counter. Same dollar. Same morning. Four different prices on the board.

This father must send eighteen thousand dollars for his daughter's university fee. This exporter wants rupees today for a forty thousand dollar bill. Which price does each one get?

Hold your answer. Meera, a new forex officer at this branch, is going to answer first.

That is the answer most people give. It sounds right. And it is exactly backwards.

Here is why. The rate on that board is not the customer's price. It is the bank's price. So we must stand on the bank's side of the counter.

When dollars come into the bank, the bank is buying them. It pays you rupees.

When dollars go out of the bank, the bank is selling them. It takes your rupees.

Like any shopkeeper, the bank buys cheap and sells dear. The gap pays its salaries.

So the bank's buying rate is always the lower rupee number, and its selling rate is always the higher one.

Now the father. His dollars leave the bank and go to America. The bank sells. He pays the higher, selling side.

The exporter brings dollars in through his bill. The bank buys. He gets the lower, buying side.

Everyone does at first. One rule fixes it for good: name every deal from the bank's side.

Quick check. A son abroad sends two thousand five hundred dollars home to his mother. Does the bank buy or sell?

Dollars come in. The bank buys. Green.

For that we need a second picture. The rate ladder.

First, where the prices come from. Banks trade dollars with each other all day. Right now the market says eighty-five forty, eighty-five forty-two.

The lower number is the bid: what a buyer will pay. The higher one is the ask, or offer: what a seller wants.

And notice how the price is written: so many rupees for one dollar. That is a direct quote, the way FEDAI's rules say a bank must quote its customers.

That market price sits in the middle of our ladder. The counter is retail, so the bank adds its own margin. Selling rates climb up from the ask. Buying rates step down from the bid.

How big a margin? FEDAI leaves that to each bank. In your exam, the question always gives it.

The rungs nearest the middle are the TT rates. TT means telegraphic transfer: money that moves by message, straight away.

TT buying is for dollars already sitting in the bank's account abroad. TT selling is for dollars the bank sends out at once.

The outer rungs are the bill rates. With bill buying, the bank pays the exporter today, but the dollars arrive weeks later. It waits, and it carries the risk. So it buys lower.

With bill selling, the bank pays a foreign supplier against import documents and handles the paperwork. So it sells higher.

Read the ladder from the top. Bill selling, TT selling, the market, TT buying, bill buying. Bills outside. TTs inside.

Let us build the board ourselves. Here are the bank's margins.

Selling rates go up from the ask. TT selling: eighty-five forty-two, times point one, percent. That is point zero eight five four two. Add it. Eighty-five point five zero five four.

Buying rates go down from the bid. TT buying: eighty-five forty, times point zero five, percent. Point zero four two seven. Take it away. Eighty-five point three five seven three.

Now you do one. Bill buying: eighty-five forty, less point one five percent. Pause and press the keys.

The margin is point one two eight one. The rate: eighty-five point two seven one nine.

Last one, all yours. Bill selling is the TT selling rate, plus a further point one five percent.

Eighty-five point six three three seven. Look: those are the four prices from the opening board.

Now we put each deal on its rung. FEDAI's rules say which one. You guess first, every time.

A remittance arrives from abroad and is paid out in rupees.

TT buying. Dollars came in, already in the bank's account abroad.

The father's fee goes out by wire.

TT selling.

An exporter's bill is bought by the bank on the day he brings it.

Bill buying. The bank pays now and waits for the dollars.

An export bill is sent on collection, and the bank pays only after the dollars reach its account abroad.

TT buying. Nothing to wait for any more.

An importer pays for his goods, with no forward contract.

Bill selling.

Careful. That is the trap the exam loves.

When the bill is not paid, the bank turns the exporter's dollar debt into a rupee debt. That is called crystallisation.

The exporter now owes the bank dollars. It is as if the bank sells him those dollars. So it is TT selling, not TT buying.

The same flip happens when a forward contract is cancelled. A purchase contract is cancelled at TT selling. A sale contract at TT buying. We will work those sums in lesson A four.

Exactly. Amber always flips the side.

So, the opening question. The father's eighteen thousand dollars go out at TT selling: fifteen lakh thirty-nine thousand and ninety-seven rupees, twenty paise.

The exporter's forty thousand dollar bill is bought at bill buying: thirty-four lakh ten thousand eight hundred and seventy-six rupees, before the transit interest we meet in lesson A five.

Three to lock it in. One: an importer retires a bill with no forward cover. Which rate?

Bill selling.

Two: put these in order, highest first. TT buying, bill selling, bill buying, TT selling.

Bill selling, TT selling, TT buying, bill buying.

Three: a purchase contract is cancelled on its due date. Which rate?

TT selling. Amber flips the side.

One counter. Stand on the bank's side. In is buying, green and lower. Out is selling, red and higher. TTs sit near the market, bills sit outside. And reversing a deal flips the side.

Next lesson, we take this ladder into real exam sums.
```
