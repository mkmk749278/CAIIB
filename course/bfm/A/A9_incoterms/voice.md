## part_2.mp3 · Chapter 1 · What a three-letter word decides (1 turns, 2645 chars)

**1. Teacher** (N06a–N15b)
```
Okay. I know what many of you are thinking. C-I-F means cost, insurance and freight. The seller paid the freight, the seller bought the insurance. So the sea journey is the seller's job. So it's the seller's loss. And a few of you are thinking, hmm, wait. Maybe once the goods are on the ship, they're the buyer's problem. Well, that second group is right. Under C-I-F, the risk moves to the buyer the moment the goods are on board the ship at the port of shipment. Here, Chennai. After that, the sea is the buyer's risk. So why does the seller pay for freight and insurance at all? See, here's the thing. She's paying for a journey that's already at the buyer's risk. She arranges the ship and buys the insurance, but the insurance protects the buyer. So what happens now? She hands her documents to the bank. The bill of lading, the invoice, the insurance policy. If they match the LC, the bank pays. Remember, banks deal with documents, not goods. That's UCP six hundred, article five. And the buyer? He gets the insurance policy along with the documents. So he claims from the insurer. Nobody is left holding the loss. That's the whole point. If you said "seller", don't feel bad. Almost everybody does the first time. The word "insurance" in the name makes it sound like the seller is carrying the risk. She isn't. So, what is an Incoterm? It's a set of rules published by the International Chamber of Commerce, the ICC. Three letters, a place, and the version. Like "C-I-F Rotterdam, Incoterms twenty twenty". And those three letters answer three questions for the buyer and seller. One. Where does the seller deliver, and so, where does the risk pass? Two. Who pays which costs? Three. Who does the customs work, export and import? Now be careful here, because the exam loves this. Incoterms do not decide the price. They don't decide how or when payment is made. And they don't decide when ownership of the goods passes. That's for the sales contract. Also, Incoterms are not law. They apply only when the parties write them into the contract. That's why we always add the version, Incoterms twenty twenty. An old contract could still say twenty ten. And the version we follow today is Incoterms twenty twenty. It came into force on the first of January, twenty twenty. ICC has only just begun work on the next version, so for your exam, twenty twenty is the answer. Okay, that's part one. Still with me? Good. You already know the most important thing: risk and cost are two different things. Hold on to that. Now stretch for a second if you like. Part two is the picture that holds all eleven rules. I need full attention for this one.
```

## part_3.mp3 · Chapter 2 · The road (1 turns, 2850 chars)

**1. Teacher** (N16–N30)
```
So, here's our picture. A road. On the left, the seller's door. On the right, the buyer's door. And seven stops in between. Let me walk you through them. Stop one, the seller's own premises. Stop two, the goods are handed over to the carrier. Stop three, alongside the ship at the port. Stop four, on board the ship. Then comes the sea, or the air, or the long road. Stop five, the goods arrive at the destination, ready to be unloaded. Stop six, unloaded. And stop seven, cleared through import customs, with the duty paid. And two markers move on this road. The red baton is the risk. Wherever the baton is handed over, from there, any loss is the buyer's. The blue line is the cost. It shows how far the seller pays. Now let's put the eleven rules on the road, from the seller's door to the buyer's door. Just watch, don't write. The card at the end has all of it. First, E-X-W. Ex Works. The seller just keeps the goods ready at her own premises. The buyer comes, loads the truck, and does everything after that. Even the export clearance. This is the least the seller can do. Next, F-C-A. Free Carrier. The seller hands the goods to the carrier the buyer has chosen. If that happens at the seller's own premises, the seller also loads them. And she clears them for export. F-A-S, Free Alongside Ship. The goods are placed next to the ship at the port of shipment. And F-O-B, Free on Board. The goods are loaded on board the ship. In all these F rules, the buyer pays the main freight. Now the C rules. These are the clever ones. C-F-R, cost and freight. And C-I-F, cost, insurance and freight. The risk passes on board the ship, same place as F-O-B. But the seller pays the freight all the way to the destination port. Then C-P-T, Carriage Paid To. And C-I-P, Carriage and Insurance Paid To. Here the risk passes when the goods are handed to the first carrier. But again, the seller pays the carriage up to the destination. So see what happens with the C rules. The baton is handed over at the start, but the blue line keeps running to the end. Two different points. That's exactly where our Tiruppur exporter's case sits. And finally, the D rules. Delivered. D-A-P, Delivered at Place. The goods arrive at the destination, ready to be unloaded, and the buyer unloads them. D-P-U, Delivered at Place Unloaded. Same, but the seller also unloads them. It's the only rule where the seller unloads at the destination. And D-D-P, Delivered Duty Paid. The seller even clears import customs and pays the duty. This is the most the seller can do. So, E-X-W at one end, seller does the least. D-D-P at the other end, seller does the most. Everything else sits in between. That's a favourite question. And customs is simple. Export clearance is the seller's job in every rule, except E-X-W. Import clearance is the buyer's job in every rule, except D-D-P.
```

## part_5.mp3 · Chapter 3 · Insurance and the bank's desk (1 turns, 2758 chars)

**1. Teacher** (N37–N49a)
```
So, in which rules does the seller have to buy insurance? Only two. C-I-F and C-I-P. The "I" in the name is the clue. In every other rule, if anybody insures the goods, it's the buyer, for his own protection. And here's the twenty twenty change everybody asks about. Under C-I-P, the seller must buy wide cover, the Institute Cargo Clauses A. That's the all-risks kind. Under C-I-F, the minimum is still Clauses C, the basic kind. Unless the parties agree something else. Why the difference? ICC's thinking is this. C-I-F is used a lot for bulk commodities at sea, where basic cover is the custom. C-I-P is used more for manufactured goods. So C-I-P got the bigger umbrella. And how much cover? At least one hundred and ten percent of the contract price. Not one hundred. One hundred and ten. And the cover must be in the currency of the contract. The LC side says the same thing. Under UCP six hundred, article twenty-eight, if the LC is silent on the amount, the cover must be at least one hundred and ten percent of the C-I-F or C-I-P value. Okay, calculators out. Our exporter's price. Her goods on board at Chennai cost forty-five thousand dollars. That's her F-O-B value. Sea freight to Rotterdam, two thousand four hundred. Insurance premium, six hundred. Add them up. Forty-five thousand, plus two thousand four hundred, plus six hundred. Forty-eight thousand dollars. That's her C-I-F price. Cost, plus insurance, plus freight. The name is the formula. Now the minimum insurance cover. Forty-eight thousand, into one point one, equals. Fifty-two thousand eight hundred dollars. Simple. The same multiplier habit as before, no percent key needed. Your turn. A C-I-P price of thirty thousand euros. What's the minimum cover? Pause the video and press the keys. Done? Thirty thousand, into one point one. Thirty-three thousand euros. Did you get it? Great. Now be careful here, this is where people slip. Some of you will take one hundred and ten percent of the F-O-B value. That's wrong. The base is the C-I-F or C-I-P value. On our numbers, F-O-B would give you forty-nine thousand five hundred. Wrong option. Now think about what this means at the LC desk. If the LC says C-I-F or C-I-P, the seller's documents must include an insurance document. If it says F-O-B or F-C-A, the buyer insures, so the seller won't send one. And the insurance document must not be dated after the shipment date, unless it shows the cover started by then. Cover that starts after the goods have sailed is no cover at all. That's also article twenty-eight. And the bill of lading usually tells you who paid the freight. Under C and D rules you'd usually see "freight prepaid". Under F rules, "freight collect". It's a quick way to see whether the documents fit the term.
```
