# A00 script v1: beginner review (review v1, beginner)

**Verdict: ADOPT WITH FIXES**

Reviewer: independent beginner review (fresh session, 2026-11-20). Role played: a bank officer in South India, four
years in service, branch banking only, never worked in forex, studying for CAIIB on a phone after a long day. Comfortable
in plain English, not in economics words.

Read: CLAUDE.md, PLAYBOOK §5, §5a, §5b, `course/spec/lesson_spec.md`, `course/bfm/A/plans_A.md` §A00,
`course/bfm/greetings.md`, then `script.md` v1 line by line with its cue sheet. Facts were not checked (that is the
expert review); where a fix adds words, I kept to what the script already says or what a refresher needs.

## Overall, as the learner

The class feels warm and live. The opening receipt (85.20 yesterday, 85.65 today, "who changed it? RBI?") pulls me in,
because customers really ask this. The three pockets, the four calendar boxes and the see-saw are pictures I would
remember. Chapter breaks have check-ins, and the "some of you / others" moments work.

Where I get lost is jargon that arrives with no explanation, all of it from JAIIB economics I have half forgotten:
**current account** (to me that is a CA deposit account at my branch), **balance of payments**, **over the counter**
(the lesson has spent five minutes on *my* counter), **hedging, speculation**, **forward discount**, **Bretton
Woods**, **par value**, **value date**. The plan (§A00) itself names the JAIIB prerequisites (inflation, BoP,
RBI's role), but the script has no refresher. And the one link I most need, "a higher rate means a *weaker* rupee",
is never said in plain words.

**Length.** The header says about 1,700 spoken words and about 13 minutes. I counted the N lines: about **1,010
words**, so about 7 minutes of speech and under 8 with holds. That is well under the spec (1,200–2,000 words,
12–15 minutes). This is good news for the fixes: the refreshers and plain-word bridges below add about 350–450
words and bring it to about 10–11 minutes, still short. Nothing needs cutting; the content is not too much, it is too
compressed in places.

## Findings

### High

**1. High · N29, N40, N45 · "Current account" and "balance of payments" with no refresher.**
Learner: "Current account deficit? Our branch's current accounts? Then 'convertible on the current account' in N40,
and again in practice Q2. I don't know what this means here." The plan lists BoP as a JAIIB prerequisite; the script
needs the 30-second refresher (CLAUDE.md layer 6). Put it in Chapter 3, just before the factors, as a new line N28a:
> N28a · Quick refresher from JAIIB, thirty seconds. The balance of payments is India's account book with the rest of
> the world. It has two main parts.
> N28b · The current account holds trade and services, plus money sent home by Indians abroad. The capital account
> holds investment and loans coming in and going out.
> N28c · And careful, this current account is not the current account at your branch. Same name, different thing.
> N28d · When India spends more abroad than it earns on the current account, that gap is the current account deficit.
> More dollars are needed, so the rupee weakens.

Cue: a two-column ledger "India ↔ world", rows "current: trade, services, remittances" and "capital: investment,
loans"; a branch passbook marked "CA" with a small cross, tag "not this one". Then N29 can say "a big current account
deficit" without loss. Map: optional sub-node "BoP: current + capital".

**2. High · N27 (and N02, N44) · Rate up = rupee weaker is never said in plain words.**
Learner: "The dial says the needle moves up, '₹ weaker'. But the number went up. Up is good, no? And the receipt went
from 85.20 to 85.65; is that the rupee falling or rising?" This is the single most common beginner confusion, and the
opening question ("when the rupee falls") depends on it. Add after N27's second sentence, as N27a:
> N27a · See, the rate is rupees for one dollar. If you need more rupees to buy one dollar, the rupee has become weaker.
> N27b · So our customer's eighty-five sixty-five, up from eighty-five twenty, means the rupee fell a little overnight.

Cue: the price tag "₹ per 1 USD" from N12 returns; 85.20 → 85.65, an arrow up on the number, a small arrow down on a
rupee coin; tag "more ₹ per $ = weaker ₹".

**3. High · N33 (with N29) · "Higher interest rates strengthen the rupee" then "higher interest trades at a forward
discount".**
Learner: "Two lines apart, higher interest rate is good for the currency, then it is at a discount? And what is a
forward discount? 'Forward' was only a calendar box." Premium and discount are never explained, and the two ideas look
like a contradiction. Rewrite N33:
> N33 · One more idea, same style. Interest rate parity. It's about the price for a future date, not today's rate.
> N33a · If the price for a future date is lower than today's, we call it a forward discount. If higher, a forward
> premium.
> N33b · Now the rule. The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount. Not a premium, a
> discount.
> N33c · Why? Because otherwise you'd earn the higher interest and also gain on the rate, a free lunch. The market
> doesn't allow free lunches. The sums come in A-zero-five.
> N33d · And don't mix this up with chapter three's line. High rates pull money in today. The forward discount is
> only about the future price.

Cue: a timeline "today | future date"; two price tags, the future one lower, label "forward discount"; then the
trap card as now. (Expert to confirm the "free lunch" wording suits the fact sheet.)

**4. High · Header "Length" · The word count and timing claimed are wrong.**
About 1,010 spoken words, not about 1,700; about 7.5 minutes with holds, not 13. Correct the header, and use the room
for findings 1–3 and 5–9. Even with them all, the lesson stays under 12 minutes; if the author wants 12, the best
place for more time is a worked spot-date example (finding 8) and a second check-in in Chapter 4 (finding 16).

### Medium

**5. Medium · N14 · "Over the counter" clashes with *our* counter.**
Learner: "We have been at the branch counter since the start. Now the forex market is 'over the counter'? So it
happens at the branch counter?" Rewrite:
> N14 · The forex market is what's called over the counter, O-T-C. Careful, that's not our branch counter.
> N14a · O-T-C just means there's no exchange in the middle. Two parties deal with each other directly, mostly on
> screens. And it follows the sun.

Cue: "OTC = no exchange in between"; the branch counter icon with a small "not this" tag, then the dealer screens.

**6. Medium · N17–N18 · Hedging and speculation used but never explained; six players and five motives in two
breaths.**
Learner: "Companies 'hedge'? Then hedging and speculation as motives. Arbitrage got a card, these two got nothing."
Split N17 into two lines and add one line for the two words:
> N17 · Now, who's trading? The central bank, for its reserves and to steady the market. Banks, for their customers
> and their own trading.
> N17a · Companies, for trade and to protect themselves from rate changes. Investors and funds. Brokers who bring
> buyers and sellers together. And people like you and me.
> N18a · Two words here. Hedging means protecting yourself from a rate change. Speculation means taking a risk to
> profit from one.

Cue: a small umbrella icon "hedging = protect" and a dice icon "speculation = bet on a move" under the five tags.

**7. Medium · N21 · "Merchant market" sounds like shopkeepers.**
Learner: "Merchant? Like traders and shops only?" Add one clause:
> N21 · … A bank dealing with you at the counter is the merchant market. Merchant here just means any customer,
> not only traders.

**8. Medium · N23–N24 · "Value date" used before it is defined; holiday rule is abstract; too much in N24.**
Learner: "Value date? And 'a known holiday makes it earlier' is backwards to me. Give me a day." Define it in N22 and
split N24 with a concrete case:
> N22 (end) · When is a deal settled? The day the money actually changes hands is called the value date.
> N24 · Now be careful, this is where people slip. Spot does not mean today. Spot means two working days later.
> N24a · Say a spot deal on Monday falls due on Wednesday. If Wednesday is a holiday announced weeks ago, it settles
> on Tuesday.
> N24b · If Wednesday becomes a sudden holiday, it moves forward to Thursday. Known, go back. Sudden, go forward.
> N24c · That's chapter two. Deep breath.

Cue: the four boxes become Mon · Tue · Wed · Thu; Wed flagged; arrow back for "known", forward for "sudden". The hook
"Known, go back. Sudden, go forward." on a small card (says the rule a second way, §5b).

**9. Medium · N37 · "Par value" and "Bretton Woods" are dropped with no meaning.**
Learner: "Bretton Woods broke down. What was it?" Rewrite:
> N37 · India started with a par value system, a fixed official rate. That was part of the Bretton Woods system
> after the Second World War.
> N37a · Under it, currencies were fixed to the dollar, and the dollar to gold. When that broke down in nineteen
> seventy-one, the rupee was linked to the pound sterling.

(Expert to confirm wording against the fact sheet.)

**10. Medium · N39 · "Forty percent of receipts at the official rate": receipts of what, from whom?**
Learner: "Whose receipts? The bank's?" Rewrite:
> N39 · … A dual rate. Of every dollar an exporter earned, forty percent was converted at the official rate, and
> sixty percent at the market rate.

(Expert to confirm the exact scope of the 40:60 split, which covered receipts beyond exports.)

**11. Medium · N40 · Two jargons in one breath: "convertible on the current account" and "Article Eight of the
I-M-F".**
After finding 1, "current account" is known. Still add what convertible means:
> N40a · Convertible on the current account means you can freely change rupees into dollars to pay for trade,
> services or travel. Capital moves still have limits.

Cue: an arrow ₹ → $ with "trade · services · travel ✓" and "capital: limits" greyed.

**12. Medium · N42 · Too many numbers in one breath.**
Learner: "July twenty eighteen, fifteen minutes, eleven-thirty, twelve-thirty, one-thirty. I lost the thread." Split,
and say morning/afternoon for the voice:
> N42 · And the reference rate you see every afternoon? It's published by F-B-I-L, Financial Benchmarks India, since
> July twenty eighteen.
> N42a · It's built from real spot trades in a random fifteen-minute window, between eleven-thirty and twelve-thirty
> in the morning. It comes out at about one-thirty in the afternoon.

**13. Medium · N10 · Three pockets in one breath, and pocket two is hard to picture.**
Learner: "A draft drawn in rupees but payable in a foreign currency? When does that happen?" Give each pocket its own
line and one everyday example (the author to pick examples the expert accepts):
> N10 · Pocket one: deposits, credits and balances in a foreign currency. Like a dollar account an exporter holds.
> N10a · Pocket two: drafts, travellers cheques, letters of credit or bills drawn in rupees, but payable in a foreign
> currency.
> N10b · Pocket three is the surprise. The same kind of paper, drawn by someone outside India, but payable in rupees.

## What works well (keep)
- The receipt opening and "Who changed it? R-B-I?" Real, short, and it is answered at the end (N44).
- Three pockets, with pocket three as "the surprise"; four calendar boxes; the see-saw as "the whole engine".
- The inflation check (N31–N32): a true misconception, a clean answer, PPP named only as an idea.
- Chapter close lines ("Stretch for a second", "Deep breath") and the homework (draw the see-saw, two minutes).
- Numbers and years are in words; "R-B-I", "F-B-I-L", "A-zero-five" spelled for the voice. No sentence over 25 words
  (longest: N32, 24 words).
- Greeting and goodbye are new and match the register; N49 previews A01's question.
- No physical-room cues; no "Sir"; online-class tone throughout.

## Summary
A warm, well-pictured first class with a good hook, but it leans on JAIIB words it never refreshes. Fix findings 1–4
(BoP and current-account refresher, "higher rate = weaker rupee", forward discount explained and separated from "high
rates strengthen", and the wrong length claim) and the Medium jargon bridges (OTC, hedging/speculation, merchant, value
date with a worked day example, Bretton Woods, LERMS split, convertibility, FBIL numbers, pocket examples, FEDAI
pronunciation). The script is about 1,010 words, so all of this fits and brings it to about 10–11 minutes.
