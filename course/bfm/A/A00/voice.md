## part_2.mp3 · Chapter 4 · Who sets the rate in India? + Recap and practice + Close (N35–N50) (1 turns, 3778 chars)

```
Now the big question from the start. Does R-B-I fix the rate? First, the regimes. A fixed rate is set officially, and the central bank holds it there. A floating rate is set purely by the market. And a managed float is in between: the market sets it, the central bank smooths it, with no fixed target. And mind two words. An official cut in a fixed rate is a devaluation. A market fall in a floating rate is a depreciation, and a rise is an appreciation. India started with a par value system, a fixed official rate, under the Bretton Woods system after the Second World War. When that system broke down in nineteen seventy-one, the rupee was linked to the pound sterling. From September nineteen seventy-five, it was pegged to a basket of currencies, so no single currency could pull it around. Then came the crisis of nineteen ninety-one. In July, the rupee was devalued in two steps, about eighteen percent in all. In March nineteen ninety-two came the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System, LERMS. A dual rate: forty percent of exchange earnings at the official rate, sixty percent at the market rate. And in March nineteen ninety-three, the two rates were unified. From then, the rupee's rate is market determined. In August nineteen ninety-four, the rupee became convertible on the current account, as India accepted Article Eight of the I-M-F. Convertible on the current account means you can change rupees into dollars for trade, services or travel without special permission. Only a short list is banned or capped by the rules. Capital moves still have limits. So what does R-B-I do today? It doesn't fix the rate. India runs a managed float. In R-B-I's own words, it acts to keep the market orderly and contain excessive volatility. It buys or sells dollars from its reserves when the swings get jerky. And the reference rate you see every afternoon? It's published by F-B-I-L, Financial Benchmarks India, since July twenty eighteen. It's built from real spot trades in a random fifteen-minute slot, between eleven-thirty and twelve-thirty, around midday. It comes out at about one-thirty in the afternoon. It's a benchmark, used for things like converting figures in reports. It isn't a rate that R-B-I forces on your deal. Your counter rate comes from the interbank rate, with your bank's margin built in. So, back to our customer. What's the one-line answer? The rate moves with the market every day. R-B-I only steps in to stop wild swings, and our board follows the interbank rate. Okay, pause the video now, and answer three questions. One. A deal is struck on Monday for spot. When is it settled, if there's no holiday? Two. Which of these strengthens the rupee: a bigger current account deficit, higher inflation in India than abroad, or more foreign investment coming in? Three. Since when has the rupee's rate been market determined? Answers. One, Wednesday, the second working day. Two, more foreign investment coming in. The other two weaken it. Three, March nineteen ninety-three, when the dual rates were unified. Got all three? Well done. Now let's read the map we built. The market behind the counter. What forex is: the money, plus every claim in the three pockets. The players and their five motives. Deal dates: cash, tom, spot, forward. Three groups of factors pushing one see-saw. And regimes: from par value to a managed float, with R-B-I steadying, not fixing. Small homework. Draw the see-saw tonight, and put three factors on it, each on the side it pushes. Two minutes, that's all. And next class, we walk up to the counter itself. I'll ask you one question that trips up a lot of people. At a forex counter, is the bank buying, or selling?
```
