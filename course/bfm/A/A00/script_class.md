## Chapter 2 · The market and its players

N35 · Chapter two. The market. Before we start, from chapter one. Foreign exchange is the money, plus how many pockets?
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · The market and its players"; the coat small in the corner · H: 3 s

N36 · Three. Good. Now, where is this market? Picture it. A big building, like a stock exchange?
> 1 S: a building outline draws, dashed · H: 2 s

N37 · No. There's no building. The forex market is over the counter. O-T-C. Banks and dealers deal with each other directly, mostly on screens.
> 1 B: red cross over the building · 2 N: "OTC = no exchange in between" [F8] · 3 S: dealer screens linked by lines

N38 · Careful. Over the counter here doesn't mean our branch counter. It just means there's no exchange in the middle.
> 1 S: a small branch counter with a "not this" tag

N39 · And it follows the sun. The day starts in Sydney and Tokyo, and ends in New York. So somewhere, it's open round the clock on weekdays.
> 1 S: the world strip; centres light east to west as the sun moves · 2 N: "round the clock, weekdays"

N40 · In India, the normal hours for rupee deals are nine in the morning to five in the evening. And Saturday is not a working day for forex.
> 1 G: Mumbai · 2 C: "9 a.m. to 5 p.m. IST · FEDAI Rule 1.1" [F9] · 3 B: strike "Saturday"

N41 · Now, who trades here? Let me count them for you. One, the central bank. Two, banks. Three, companies.
> 1 S: player tags write one by one: "1 central bank" "2 banks" "3 companies" [F11]

N42 · Four, investors and funds. Five, brokers, who bring buyers and sellers together. And six, individuals like your customer.
> 1 S: "4 investors and funds" "5 brokers" "6 individuals"

N43 · Why do they trade? Five reasons. Trade, investment, hedging, speculation, arbitrage.
> 1 S: five motive tags drop in under the players

N44 · Two of these get mixed up. Hedging means protecting yourself from a rate change. Speculation means taking a risk to profit from one.
> 1 S: "hedging = protect" with an umbrella · 2 S: "speculation = bet on a move" · 3 B: underline "protect" and "risk" (one each, on the word)

N45 · Quick one. A company that has to pay dollars in three months books its rate today. Hedging or speculation?
> 1 S: a small calendar "pay $ in 3 months · rate fixed today" · H: 3 s

N46 · Hedging. It's protecting itself, not betting.
> 1 S: the umbrella glows; "✓ hedging"

N47 · Now arbitrage. Buy where it's cheap, sell where it's dear, at the same moment. With two centres or two currencies, it's simple arbitrage.
> 1 C: rule card "Arbitrage" [F14] · 2 S: two centres, an arrow cheap → dear · 3 S: "simple: 2"

N48 · With three or more, like rupee to dollar to euro and back to rupee, it's compound. And because arbitrageurs jump on every gap, prices in different centres stay close.

N49 · Last idea in this chapter. Two tiers. Banks dealing with each other is the interbank market, the wholesale side.

## Chapter 4 · Why rates move

N82 · Chapter four. Why rates move. First, from chapter three. Spot settles on which day?
> 1 S: chapter title "4 · Why rates move" · H: 3 s

N83 · The second working day. Good. Now back to our customer. Why was the dollar dearer today?
> 1 S: the customer and receipt return, small, top left

N84 · Picture a see-saw. On one side, people who want dollars. Importers paying bills. Students paying fees. Investors taking money out.
> 1 S: the see-saw draws live; dial "₹ per $" [F15] · 2 S: left pan "want $" with three icons

N85 · On the other side, people who have dollars to sell. Exporters. Money sent home from abroad. Investors bringing money in.

N86 · When more people want dollars than offer them, the dollar side goes down. So the dollar gets dearer, and the rupee weakens.

N87 · When dollars pour in, the other side goes down, and the rupee strengthens. That's the whole engine. One limit: the see-saw shows the direction, not how far.
> 1 S: right pan sinks; needle to "₹ stronger" · 2 S: a dashed outline round the see-saw, "direction, not size"

N88 · Now, careful with the numbers. The rate is rupees for one dollar. If you need more rupees for one dollar, is the rupee stronger or weaker?
> 1 S: price tag "₹ per 1 USD" returns · H: 3 s

N89 · Weaker. So our customer's eighty-five sixty-five, up from eighty-five twenty, means the rupee fell a little overnight.
> 1 S: "85.20 → 85.65 · more ₹ per $ = weaker ₹" [F15a] · 2 S: the receipt gets a small "₹ fell" stamp

N90 · A thirty-second refresher from J-A-I-I-B. The balance of payments is India's account book with the world. The current account holds trade, services, income and remittances.
> 1 S: the ledger "India ↔ world" draws [F15b] · 2 S: column 1 "current: trade · services · income · remittances"

N91 · The capital account holds investment and loans, in and out. And no, this current account is not the one at your branch.
> 1 S: column 2 "capital: investment · loans" · 2 S: a branch passbook "CA" with a red cross

N92 · So what pushes the see-saw? Three groups of factors. Fundamental, technical and speculative. Say them with me. Fundamental. Technical. Speculative.
> 1 S: three empty boxes under the see-saw · 2 S: labels write on each word [F16–18]

N93 · Fundamental means the economy itself. A big current account deficit means India spends more abroad than it earns. So the rupee weakens.
> 1 S: box 1 "economy" · 2 S: "CAD: spend > earn → ₹ weaker"

N94 · Higher interest rates pull money in, and strengthen the rupee. Then growth, the government's finances, foreign investment and political stability.
> 1 S: "interest rates ↑ → money in → ₹ stronger" · 2 S: "growth · fiscal · FDI/FPI · stability"

N95 · Technical means how the market is set up. Government controls, limits on capital, and the demand and supply in the market that day.
> 1 S: box 2 "controls · capital limits · the day's demand and supply" [F17]

N96 · Speculative means expectations. If traders expect the dollar to rise, they buy it, and it rises.
> 1 S: box 3 "expectations" · 2 S: loop arrow "expect up → buy → up" [F18]

N97 · Now the classic trap. Some of you are thinking: if inflation in India runs higher than in the U-S, the rupee gets stronger. Others: weaker. Which is it?
> 1 S: cloud "Some of you": "inflation India > US → ₹ stronger" · 2 S: cloud "Others": "→ ₹ weaker" · 3 C: pause card · H: 5 s

N98 · Weaker. If the same basket of goods gets dearer faster in India than in the U-S, each rupee buys less. So over time, it's worth less against the dollar.
> 1 S: left cloud crossed, right ticked · 2 S: two baskets, India's price tag grows faster [F19] · 3 S: rupee coin shrinks against the dollar coin

N99 · That idea has a name: purchasing power parity. You only need the idea today.
> 1 N: "PPP = the idea only"

N99a · One more idea, and a second trap. Interest rate parity is about the price for a future date. Think first. The currency with the higher interest rate: forward premium, or forward discount?
> 1 S: a timeline "today | future date" · 2 C: pause card · H: 4 s

N99b · Forward discount. The higher-interest currency trades at a forward discount. So if the rupee is at a discount, the dollar is at a premium.
> 1 C: rule card "higher interest → forward discount" [F20] · 2 S: flip arrow "₹ discount ⇄ $ premium"

N99c · Don't mix the two. Higher rates pull money in and lift a currency today. Parity is only about the forward price. The sums come in A-zero-five.
> 1 S: see-saw thumbnail "today: money in → stronger" · 2 S: timeline thumbnail "future price: discount" [F21] · 3 N: "sums: A05"

N99d · Pause check for chapter four. Which of these strengthens the rupee? A bigger current account deficit. Higher inflation in India. More foreign investment coming in.
> 1 C: check card "Chapter 4" with three options · H: 5 s

N99e · More foreign investment coming in. The other two weaken it. If you picked inflation, go back to the baskets: each rupee buys less.
> 1 S: option 3 ticked; options 1 and 2 crossed · 2 N: "inflation → weaker"

N99f · If you remember one thing from chapter four: more rupees per dollar means a weaker rupee, and the see-saw shows which way.

## Chapter 5 · Who sets the rate in India?

N85a · Chapter five. The big question. First, from chapter four. Higher inflation in India makes the rupee stronger, or weaker?
> 1 S: chapter title "5 · Who sets the rate in India?" · H: 3 s

N85b · Weaker. Good. Now, three regimes. A fixed rate is set officially, and the central bank holds it there. A floating rate is set by the market alone.
> 1 S: card "fixed · set officially, held" [F22] · 2 S: card "floating · market only"

N85c · And a managed float is in between. The market sets the rate. The central bank smooths it, with no fixed target.
> 1 S: card "managed float · market sets, central bank smooths, no target" · 2 B: box around "no target"

N85d · Two words to keep apart. An official cut in a fixed rate is a devaluation. A market fall in a floating rate is a depreciation, and a rise is an appreciation.
> 1 S: compare card "devaluation = official cut" · 2 S: "depreciation = market fall · appreciation = market rise" [F21a]

N85e · Now India's road, in five posts. Post one. India started with a par value system, a fixed official rate, under the Bretton Woods system.
> 1 S: the road draws left to right · 2 S: post 1 "par value · Bretton Woods" [F23]

N85f · Post two. When that system broke down in nineteen seventy-one, the rupee was linked to the pound sterling.
> 1 S: post 2 "1971 · linked to £" [F24]

N85g · Post three. From September nineteen seventy-five, it was pegged to a basket of currencies, so no single currency could pull it around.
> 1 S: post 3 "Sept 1975 · basket peg" [F25]

N85h · Then the storm of nineteen ninety-one. In July, the rupee was devalued in two steps, about eighteen percent in all.
> 1 S: storm cloud "1991" · 2 S: "Jul 1991 · 2 steps · ≈ 18%" [F26]

N85i · Post four. March nineteen ninety-two. LERMS, the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System. A dual rate: forty percent of exchange earnings at the official rate, sixty percent at the market rate.
> 1 S: post 4 "Mar 1992 · LERMS · dual" [F27] · 2 S: bar "40% official · 60% market"

N85j · Post five. March nineteen ninety-three. The two rates were unified. From then, the rupee's rate is market determined.
> 1 S: post 5 "Mar 1993 · unified" [F28] · 2 B: box "market-determined"

N85k · One more flag. In August nineteen ninety-four, the rupee became convertible on the current account, as India accepted Article Eight of the I-M-F.
> 1 S: flag past the road "Aug 1994 · current account convertible · IMF Art. VIII" [F29]

N85l · That means you can change rupees into dollars for trade, services or travel without special permission. A short list is banned or capped by the rules. Capital moves still have limits.
> 1 S: "₹ → $ for trade · services · travel ✓" · 2 S: "a short list banned or capped" [F29a] · 3 S: "capital: limits" greyed

N85m · Quick one. Since when has the rupee's rate been market determined?
> 1 S: the road; posts dim · H: 3 s
