## Chapter 1 · What foreign exchange is

N06 · Okay. Here's the honest answer. Neither. And by the end of today you'll know exactly why. But first, we need
one word clear. What is foreign exchange?
> 1 S: chapter title slides in "1 · What foreign exchange is" · 2 N: "honest answer"
> · 3 S: both clouds get a small cross, "neither" · 4 N: "why: by the end"
> · 5 S: the counter shrinks to the left; a coat with three pockets is drawn on the right
> · 6 N: "foreign exchange = ?"

N07 · The law for this is FEMA, the Foreign Exchange Management Act of nineteen ninety-nine. It came into force in
June two thousand, replacing the old FERA.
> 1 S: a law-book card "FEMA 1999" · 2 N: "in force 1 June 2000 · replaced FERA 1973"

N08 · FEMA first defines foreign currency simply. Any currency other than Indian currency. Dollar, euro, yen,
dirham, all foreign currency.
> 1 S: card line "s. 2(m) foreign currency" · 2 N: "= any currency other than Indian"
> · 3 S: four small notes drawn: USD, EUR, JPY, AED

N09 · Then it defines foreign exchange, and this is wider. Foreign exchange means foreign currency, and it includes
three more things. Think of them as three pockets.
> 1 S: card line "s. 2(n) foreign exchange = foreign currency + …" · 2 G: "includes"
> · 3 S: the three pockets on the coat light up, empty

N10 · Pocket one: deposits, credits and balances payable in any foreign currency. Pocket two: drafts, travellers
cheques, letters of credit or bills drawn in rupees but payable in a foreign currency. Pocket three is the surprise:
the same instruments drawn by someone outside India, but payable in rupees.
> 1 S: pocket 1 fills: "deposits · credits · balances in FCY"
> · 2 S: pocket 2 fills: "drafts · TCs · LCs · bills: drawn in ₹, payable in FCY"
> · 3 S: pocket 3 fills: "same instruments, drawn abroad, payable in ₹"; G: pocket 3

N11 · Read that third one again. Payable in rupees, and still foreign exchange, because it's drawn from outside India.
Examiners love that line.
> 1 N: "payable in ₹ … still forex!" · 2 S: a small globe stamp on pocket 3 · 3 N: "exam favourite"

N12 · So the hook is simple. Foreign currency is the note. Foreign exchange is the note plus every claim and paper
that stands for it. And the exchange rate is just the price of one currency in another. In India we quote it
directly, so many rupees for one dollar.
> 1 N: "note vs note + claims"
> · 2 S: one note on the left, the full coat on the right, a bracket "foreign exchange" over both
> · 3 S: a price tag "₹ per 1 USD" · 4 N: "direct quote" · 5 M: branch node 1 "what forex is"

N12a · Why does that price matter so much? An importer paying dollars wants a strong rupee. An exporter receiving
dollars is happier with a weaker one. Same rate, opposite feelings. Okay, first chapter done. Stretch for a second.
> 1 N: "why it matters" · 2 S: importer figure, tag "pays USD · wants strong ₹"
> · 3 S: exporter figure, tag "gets USD · likes weak ₹" · 4 N: "same rate, opposite feelings"

## Chapter 2 · The market and its players

N13 · Now, where is this market? Here's the first surprise. There's no building. No trading floor where everyone
meets.
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · The market and its players" · 2 N: "surprise" · 3 S: an empty building outline, crossed out
> · 4 N: "no trading floor"

N14 · The forex market is over the counter. Banks and dealers trade with each other directly, mostly on screens. And
it follows the sun.
> 1 N: "OTC" · 2 S: dealer screens linked by thin lines
> · 3 S: the world strip drawn; a small sun appears at the far east

N15 · Sydney and Tokyo open first, then Singapore, then Mumbai and Dubai, then London, then New York. So somewhere in

> 1 S: the centres light up west-ward as the sun moves: Sydney, Tokyo, Singapore, Mumbai, Dubai, London, New York
> · 2 N: "round the clock, weekdays"

N16 · In India, the onshore market for rupee deals runs from nine in the morning to five in the evening, Indian time.
And Saturday is not a working day for forex.
> 1 G: Mumbai; S: a clock "9.00 a.m. to 5.00 p.m. IST · FEDAI Rule 1.1" · 2 N: "Saturday ✗"

N17 · Now, who's trading? The central bank, for its reserves and to steady the market. Banks, for their customers
and for their own trading. Companies, for trade and to hedge. Investors and funds. Brokers who bring buyers and
sellers together. And people like you and me.
> 1 S: a ring of players around the world strip · 2 S: "RBI · reserves, steadying" · 3 S: "banks · customers, trading"
> · 4 S: "companies · trade, hedging" · 5 S: "investors · funds" · 6 S: "brokers" · 7 S: "individuals"

N18 · And their reasons boil down to five. Trade, investment, hedging, speculation, and arbitrage.
> 1 N: "5 motives" · 2 S: five tags drop under the ring: trade · investment · hedging · speculation · arbitrage

N19 · Arbitrage needs one card. Buy where it's cheap, sell where it's dear, at the same moment. Between two centres,

> 1 C: rule card "Arbitrage" · 2 S: two centres, an arrow cheap → dear · 3 S: label "simple: 2 centres"
> · 4 S: a triangle of three centres, label "compound: 3 or more"

N20 · And because arbitrageurs jump on every gap, prices in different centres stay close. Remember that line.
> 1 S: the gap between the two centre prices closes · 2 N: "keeps prices together"

N21 · Next, the two tiers. Banks dealing with each other is the interbank market, the wholesale side. A bank dealing

> 1 N: "two tiers" · 2 S: upper tier drawn "interbank · bank ↔ bank · wholesale"
> · 3 S: lower tier "merchant · bank ↔ customer"

## Chapter 3 · Why rates move

N25 · Okay, back to our customer. Why was the dollar dearer today? Here's the picture I want you to keep.
> 1 S: chapter title "3 · Why rates move" · 2 S: the customer and receipt return, small, top left · 3 N: "keep this"

N26 · A see-saw. On one side, people who want dollars. Importers paying bills, students paying fees, investors
pulling money out. On the other side, people who have dollars to sell. Exporters, remittances from abroad,
investors bringing money in.
> 1 S: the see-saw drawn; a dial above the pivot reads "₹ per $" · 2 S: left end labelled "want $"
> · 3 S: importer, student, outflow icons on the left · 4 S: right end labelled "offer $"
> · 5 S: exporter, remittance, inflow icons on the right

N27 · When more people want dollars than offer them, the dollar side goes heavy. The rupee weakens. When dollars pour
in, the rupee strengthens. That's the whole engine. Everything else just pushes on this see-saw.
> 1 S: left end sinks · 2 S: the dial needle moves up "₹ weaker"
> · 3 S: right end fills; needle moves down "₹ stronger" · 4 N: "the whole engine" · 5 G: the see-saw

N28 · What pushes it? Teachers group the pushes into three. Fundamental, technical, and speculative.
> 1 N: "what pushes it?" · 2 S: three empty boxes under the see-saw
> · 3 S: box labels: fundamental · technical · speculative

N29 · Fundamental factors are the economy itself. The balance of payments: a big current account deficit weakens the
rupee. Inflation: higher inflation at home weakens it. Interest rates: higher rates pull money in, and strengthen it.
Growth, the government's finances, foreign investment, and political stability.
> 1 S: box 1 fills: "economy" · 2 S: "BoP deficit → ₹ weaker" · 3 S: "inflation ↑ → ₹ weaker"
> · 4 S: "interest rates ↑ → money in → ₹ stronger" · 5 S: "growth · fiscal · FDI/FPI · stability"

N30 · Technical factors are about how the market is set up. Government controls, limits on capital moving in and
out, and the demand and supply sitting in the market that day. And speculative factors are expectations. If traders
expect the dollar to rise, they buy it, and it rises.
> 1 S: box 2 fills: "controls · capital limits · market position" · 2 S: box 3 fills: "expectations"
> · 3 S: a small loop arrow "expect up → buy → up" · 4 M: branch node 4 "three groups of factors"

N31 · Quick check. Some of you are thinking, high inflation in India makes the rupee stronger, because prices are
higher. Others are thinking it makes the rupee weaker. Which is it?
> 1 N: "quick check" · 2 S: cloud "Some of you": "inflation ↑ → ₹ stronger"
> · 3 S: cloud "Others": "inflation ↑ → ₹ weaker" · 4 C: pause card, 5 s ring (hold)

N32 · Weaker. If the same basket of goods costs more in rupees here, each rupee buys less, so over time it's worth
less against the dollar. That idea has a name, purchasing power parity. You only need the idea today.
> 1 S: "Others" cloud ticked, "Some of you" crossed
> · 2 S: two baskets, India and USA, same goods; India's price tag grows · 3 N: "PPP: same basket, same price"
> · 4 N: "idea only"

## Chapter 4 · Who sets the rate in India?

N35 · Now the big question from the start. Does R-B-I fix the rate? Let's walk India's road, quickly.
> 1 S: chapter title "4 · Who sets the rate in India?" · 2 N: "does RBI fix it?"
> · 3 S: the road drawn left to right, five milestone posts

N36 · First, the regimes. A fixed rate is pegged, and the central bank holds it there. A floating rate is set purely
by the market. And a managed float is in between: the market sets it, the central bank smooths it, with no fixed
target.
> 1 C: three small cards: "fixed · pegged, held" · 2 S: "floating · market only"
> · 3 S: "managed float · market sets, central bank smooths, no target" · 4 G: managed float

N37 · India started with a par value system. When the Bretton Woods system broke down in nineteen seventy-one, the
rupee was linked to the pound sterling.
> 1 S: post 1 "par value" · 2 S: post 2 "1971 · linked to £ sterling"

N38 · From September nineteen seventy-five, it was pegged to a basket of currencies, so no single currency could
pull it around.
> 1 S: post 3 "Sept 1975 · basket peg", a small basket of coins on the post

N39 · Then came the crisis of nineteen ninety-one, and reform. In March nineteen ninety-two, the Liberalised Exchange
Rate Management System, LERMS. A dual rate: forty percent of receipts at the official rate, sixty percent at the
market rate.
> 1 S: a storm cloud "1991 crisis" · 2 S: post 4 "Mar 1992 · LERMS · dual rate"
> · 3 S: a bar split "40% official · 60% market"

N40 · And in March nineteen ninety-three, the two rates were unified. From then, the rupee's rate is market
determined. Then in August nineteen ninety-four, the rupee became convertible on the current account, when India
accepted Article Eight of the I-M-F.
> 1 S: post 5 "Mar 1993 · unified · market-determined" · 2 G: post 5
> · 3 S: a small flag past the road "Aug 1994 · current account convertible · IMF Art. VIII"

N41 · So what does R-B-I do today? It doesn't fix the rate. In its own words, it acts to keep the market orderly and
to contain excessive volatility. It buys or sells dollars from its reserves when the swings get jerky.
> 1 S: RBI's hand reaches toward the see-saw · 2 N: "fix ✗"
> · 3 S: quote card "orderly · contain excessive volatility · RBI Annual Report 2025-26"
> · 4 S: the hand dampens a wild swing; the see-saw still moves gently

N42 · And the reference rate you see every afternoon? That's published by F-B-I-L, Financial Benchmarks India, since
July twenty eighteen. It's built from actual spot trades in a random fifteen-minute slot between eleven-thirty and
twelve-thirty, and it comes out around one-thirty.
> 1 S: a notice board "FBIL reference rate" · 2 N: "FBIL · since 10 July 2018"
> · 3 S: a clock band 11.30 to 12.30 with a random 15-minute slice lit, then "≈ 1.30 p.m."

N43 · It's a benchmark, used for things like converting figures in reports. It isn't a rate that R-B-I forces on
your deal. Your counter rate comes from the interbank market, plus your bank's margin.
> 1 N: "benchmark"; S: a report sheet with "₹ conversion" · 2 N: "not imposed"
> · 3 S: arrow interbank → board, "+ margin"; M: branch node 5 "regimes and RBI"

N44 · So, back to our customer. What's the one-line answer? The rate moves with the market every day. R-B-I only
steps in to stop wild swings, and our board follows the interbank rate. Neither of our two clouds had it fully right.
> 1 S: the customer and officer return · 2 N: "one-line answer" · 3 S: officer's bubble: "moves with the market"
> · 4 S: bubble adds "RBI stops wild swings · board follows interbank"
> · 5 S: both opening clouds return, each with a half tick
