Chapter three. First, from chapter two. The fee charged from the forfaiter's promise till the purchase? The commitment fee. Now, what does a bank give its best exporters? R-B-I's Gold Card scheme. Who gets one? Creditworthy exporters with a good track record, small ones included, on the bank's own criteria. Two are kept out. First, exporters on E-C-G-C's Specific Approval List. E-C-G-C insures export credit; this list holds exporters it covers only case by case. Second, those with overdue bills above ten per cent of last year's turnover. The limits: in principle for three years, renewed automatically. Plus a standby. How much? Not less than twenty per cent of the assessed limit, for sudden orders. Speed: twenty-five days for a fresh application, fifteen for renewal, seven for an ad hoc limit. And: preference for P-C-F-C, foreign currency packing credit. Collateral, and E-C-G-C's cover for the bank, may be waived. And the rate is no higher than the bank's normal export-credit rate. Now, status holders. The government, through D-G-F-T, recognises top exporters by their export performance. Five ranks, in million dollars: three, fifteen, fifty, two hundred, eight hundred. One Star Export House to Five Star. Their privileges include clearances on self-declaration, and no bank guarantees under trade-policy schemes. They may send documents straight to the buyer, but the money still comes through a bank. And they may export goods free, to promote sales: up to one crore rupees or two per cent of average exports, whichever is lower. Micro and small firms' exports count double, but only towards the One Star rank. A paper asked: may a status holder write off ten per cent of its bills itself? Today? That was the old rule; five per cent for others. Today, the bank may allow a reduction for good reasons. And where the shipping bill is up to ten lakh rupees, the exporter's own declaration is enough, even if nothing came. Now, the Hamburg buyer pays. Must our exporter convert every dollar to rupees at once? No. She may keep it in an E-E-F-C account, the Exchange Earners' Foreign Currency account. Some of you think it earns interest, like a savings account. Does it? No. It pays no interest. And no loans against the balance. Rupees taken out can't be put back. She may also keep an account abroad for export money. Use it for imports, or bring it home by the end of next month. Three months, if that bank is in an I-F-S-C, like GIFT City. Diamond traders have their own: the Diamond Dollar Account. Three years' record, three crore turnover, up to five accounts, dollars only. Older books list more: commission to a foreign agent who found the buyer, a cap on reducing invoices, and conditions for refunds. Since October twenty twenty-six those fixed conditions are gone. The bank's own policy decides, within the new regulations. In the exam, answer by today's rule. If the options offer only the old figure, the question follows the old rule: pick it. Pause check for chapter three. One. The Gold Card standby limit? Not less than twenty per cent. Two, the twist. A jeweller: three years' record, but two and a half crore turnover.
