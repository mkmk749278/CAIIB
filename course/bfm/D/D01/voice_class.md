```
[warmly] Hello everyone, welcome to Module D. Open a fresh notebook page, because today we read the bank's whole balance sheet. Put your phone on silent. Let us start with two quick recall questions from earlier classes. From risk management: what is the current Statutory Liquidity Ratio commercial banks must maintain against NDTL? Eighteen per cent of NDTL, held in cash, bullion, excess CRR, or unencumbered approved Government Securities. From treasury: what are the three books in an integrated treasury, and which holds sovereign bonds? Money market, securities, and forex, with Government Securities held inside the investment portfolio. Balance sheet components and profit account heads appear regularly in recent exam cycles as classification questions. Stay with me, and you will place every balance-sheet and profit-account item into its exact schedule. Picture Branch Manager Meera at Deccan Commercial Bank, an illustrative branch, opening the annual report on her phone. She asks a new officer two plain questions: where does the bank's money come from, and where does it go? Some of you think customer deposits are assets, because the branch celebrates every new fixed deposit it mobilises. Others know deposits are liabilities, yet hesitate on NABARD refinance, corporate debentures, or deposit insurance premium. Hold that thought. Let us build the legal frame first, and every item will fit its shelf. Every bank's financial statements follow a fixed statutory format, not a format chosen by management. Under Section twenty-nine of the Banking Regulation Act, nineteen forty-nine, every banking company prepares a Balance Sheet and P&L. Indian banks cover all their business, while foreign banks cover all business transacted through their Indian branches. Both statements are drawn as on thirty-first March, in the forms set out in the Third Schedule. Quick JAIIB refresher: Form A is the Balance Sheet, and Form B is the Profit and Loss Account. Unlike a company's balance sheet, Form A has no split between current and non-current assets or liabilities. Who holds the statutory power to amend the Third Schedule forms? Only the Central Government, by Official Gazette notification after at least three months' notice. Who must sign Form A and Form B before audit and publication? For an Indian bank, the principal officer and at least three directors, or all directors if three or fewer. For a foreign bank, the manager or agent of its principal office in India signs them. Under Section thirty, a qualified auditor audits them, and appointing or removing that auditor needs prior RBI approval. Under Section thirty-one, three copies go to RBI within three months of year-end, extendable up to three more months. Banks follow the Companies Accounting Standards Rules, because Ind AS for banks remains deferred till further notice. Look at the complete sixteen-schedule map on one screen. Write this down, because exam questions test these numbers directly. Form A has twelve schedules: Schedules one to five are Sources of Funds, Schedules six to eleven are Uses. Below the total sits Schedule twelve Contingent Liabilities, followed by Bills for Collection with no schedule number. Form B uses Schedules thirteen to sixteen: Interest Earned, Other Income, Interest Expended, and Operating Expenses. Pause now for Chapter one checks. First: which schedule holds Contingent Liabilities, and what number carries Bills for Collection? Contingent Liabilities is Schedule twelve, while Bills for Collection sits in summary below it with no schedule number. Second: within how many months must three copies of audited accounts reach RBI, and what is the maximum extension? Within three months from year-end, extendable by RBI by up to three further months under Section thirty-one. One thing to remember: Section twenty-nine fixes Form A, Form B, and all sixteen schedules by law. Quick recall: does Form A separate current assets from non-current assets? No, the Third Schedule Form A has no current versus non-current split. Now let us answer Meera's question by loading our two shelves: Sources on the left, Uses on the right. Both shelves always balance: every rupee raised on the left sits in an asset on the right. Left shelf, Schedule one is Capital: paid-up equity and Tier one perpetual non-cumulative preference shares. Capital is permanent, absorbs losses first, and stands last in line for repayment if a bank winds up. Schedule two is Reserves and Surplus: Statutory Reserve, Capital Reserve, Share Premium, Revenue Reserves, and P&L balance. Watch two Reserve rules. Section seventeen sets a twenty per cent floor, but RBI mandates twenty-five per cent for commercial banks. Also, RBI discontinued the Investment Fluctuation Reserve on eighteen May twenty twenty-six, removing it from Schedule two Revenue Reserves. Schedule three is Deposits: Demand, Savings Bank, and Term Deposits, split by India and outside India. Deposits are liabilities because the bank owes them back, and demand deposits carry the highest withdrawal risk. Schedule four is Borrowings: from RBI, other banks, institutions like NABARD and EXIM Bank, outside India, plus Tier two debt. Borrowings cost more and roll over faster than retail deposits, so heavy reliance raises funding risk. Schedule five is Other Liabilities and Provisions: bills payable, net credit inter-office adjustments, accrued interest, tax, and standard asset provisions. Now the right shelf, Uses of Funds. Schedule six is Cash in hand and Balances with RBI. Cash and CRR balances earn zero interest, while RBI balances above CRR act as liquid settlement buffers. Schedule seven is Balances with Banks and Money at Call and Short Notice, for tenors up to fourteen days. From our money market class: Call Money is overnight, Notice Money is two to fourteen days, Term Money is fifteen days to one year. Schedule eight is Investments: Government Securities, other approved securities, shares, debentures and bonds, subsidiaries, and others. Government Securities dominate Schedule eight because of the eighteen per cent SLR mandate and ready repo liquidity. Schedule nine is Advances, net of NPA provisions: the highest-yielding asset and the biggest source of credit risk. Schedule ten is Fixed Assets, and Schedule eleven is Other Assets like advance tax and non-banking assets. Fixed assets are non-earning, and non-banking assets acquired against bad loans must be disposed within seven years under Section nine. Below sits the dotted shelf: Schedule twelve Contingent Liabilities — guarantees, letters of credit, forward forex, and DEA Fund claims. They sit off the balance sheet pending a future event, yet as we saw in credit risk, they still require capital. Pause now for Chapter two checks. First: which asset schedule earns zero interest on mandatory balances, and which yields highest? Schedule six Cash and CRR balances earn zero interest, while Schedule nine Advances yield highest with highest credit risk. Second: what separates Call Money, Notice Money, and Term Money? Tenor: Call is overnight, Notice is two to fourteen days, and Term is over fourteen days up to one year. One thing to remember: every schedule trades off cost, stability, liquidity, and earning power. Quick recall: what percentage of net profit must a commercial bank transfer to Statutory Reserve? At least twenty-five per cent under RBI directions, above the twenty per cent floor in Section seventeen. Now let us test six classic placement traps. First: a customer's fixed deposit matured last month and remains unpaid. Many of you think it leaves Deposits and moves to Schedule five Other Liabilities. That is wrong. Matured or overdue term deposits stay in Schedule three Deposits, reclassified under Demand Deposits because they are payable on demand. Second trap: interest accrued on term deposits that is not yet due. Does it sit in Schedule three? No. Accrued interest not yet due sits under Schedule five Other Liabilities, excluded from Deposits. Third trap: refinance from NABARD or EXIM Bank, or RBI repo borrowing. Where does it go? Always under Schedule four Borrowings, never Deposits, while the underlying loans stay gross in Schedule nine. Fourth trap: bills payable like demand drafts, net credit inter-office adjustments, and Standard Asset provisions. All three sit in Schedule five Other Liabilities; Standard Asset provisions are never netted from Schedule nine Advances. One coming change: from one April twenty twenty-seven under Expected Credit Loss rules, that line becomes Stage one and two provisions. Fifth trap: the bank buys corporate debentures. Many officers put them in Advances because they fund a company. Wrong. Corporate debentures and bonds always sit in Schedule eight Investments, never Schedule nine Advances. Sixth trap: Schedule nine splits advances by security — tangible assets, bank or government guarantee, or unsecured. If an infrastructure borrower pledges project rights or licences, is that loan secured or unsecured? Unsecured. Rights, licences, and authorisations are not tangible security, so they sit under Unsecured in Schedule nine. Also watch staff loans: interest-bearing staff loans sit in Schedule nine, while non-interest-bearing staff loans sit in Schedule eleven. Pause now for Chapter three checks. First: where go an overdue fixed deposit and NABARD refinance? Overdue fixed deposit goes to Schedule three Demand Deposits, and NABARD refinance goes to Schedule four Borrowings. Second: where go corporate debentures and a net debit inter-office adjustment balance? Corporate debentures go to Schedule eight Investments, and net debit inter-office adjustments go to Schedule eleven Other Assets. One thing to remember: check the item's legal nature, not its branch nickname.
```

```
Quick recall: where do Standard Asset provisions sit on Form A? Under Schedule five Other Liabilities and Provisions, never netted against Schedule nine Advances. Open Form B, the Profit and Loss Account: two income schedules, two expense schedules, and one separate provisions line. Schedule thirteen is Interest Earned: interest and discount on advances, investment income, and interest on RBI or interbank funds. Schedule fourteen is Other Income: commission and brokerage, net profit on investment sale or revaluation, forex profit, and miscellaneous income. Schedule fifteen is Interest Expended: interest on deposits, borrowings, refinance, plus broken period interest expensed on G-Secs. Schedule sixteen is Operating Expenses: staff costs, rent and municipal taxes, printing, advertisement, depreciation, audit fees, and insurance. Watch the biggest Form B trap: many officers place Provisions and Contingencies inside Schedule sixteen Operating Expenses. Wrong. Provisions and Contingencies — for NPA, NPI, and income tax — sits outside Schedule sixteen, booked above the line. Now a five-item P&L drill. Say the schedule aloud during each pause. Item one: DICGC deposit insurance premium. Schedule sixteen Operating Expenses under Insurance, with a mandatory timely-payment note from April twenty twenty-six. Item two: depreciation on bank buildings, including leasehold properties and non-banking assets. Schedule sixteen Operating Expenses under Depreciation on bank's property. Item three: net profit on sale of investments and net profit on revaluation of investments. Schedule fourteen Other Income, whereas provision for non-performing investments goes to Provisions and Contingencies. Item four: printing publicity brochures, versus printing regular branch forms. Both sit in Schedule sixteen, but publicity printing goes under Advertisement, while regular forms go under Printing and Stationery. Item five: provision for income tax, versus municipal property tax on a branch building. Municipal tax goes under Schedule sixteen Rent, Taxes and Lighting, while income tax goes to Provisions and Contingencies. One thing to remember: Provisions and Contingencies stays outside Schedule sixteen, deducted above the line to reach Net Profit. Mixed check across the lesson: name the exact schedule for overdue fixed deposit, corporate debenture, and DICGC premium. Overdue fixed deposit is Schedule three Demand Deposits, corporate debenture is Schedule eight Investments, and DICGC premium is Schedule sixteen. Look at our blank Module D mind map. Fill in today's three branches from memory before I reveal them. First, Form A and Form B under Section twenty-nine; second, the twelve Balance Sheet components; third, the four P&L heads. You can now read Third Schedule statements, spot all six placement traps, and classify every P&L head cleanly. Homework tonight: draw the sixteen-schedule map from memory and mark NABARD refinance, standard asset provisions, and income tax. Think about this for next class: if deposits are short-term and loans are long-term, how does a bank manage that mismatch? [warmly] That is the balance sheet and profit account mapped cleanly. Rest well tonight, and I will meet you in the next class.
```
