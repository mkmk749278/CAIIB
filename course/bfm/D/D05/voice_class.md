Stay with me to see why. Before the 1990s, RBI administered rates, shielding banks from rate swings. Deregulation began in the early 1990s, largely finished by 1997, and ended on savings deposits on 25 October 2011. Interest rate risk is the risk that market rate changes adversely affect a bank's financial condition. Trading book faces price risk; banking book tracks earnings and economic value. Meet six IRR sources, each with one branch situation. First is gap or mismatch risk: assets and liabilities mature or reprice on different dates. Example: 5-year fixed loans funded by 1-year FDs squeeze margins when FDs renew higher. Second is basis risk: similar-tenor assets and liabilities priced on different benchmarks move by unequal amounts. Example: T-Bill loans funded by repo deposits lose spread when indices diverge. Third is embedded option risk: borrowers prepay loans when rates fall, or depositors break FDs early when rates rise. Many think customer options work both ways. In practice, customers exercise them only against the bank. Basel standards split option risk into automatic options like caps and floors, and behavioural options like prepayments. Fourth is yield curve risk: non-parallel curve twists, steepening, or flattening hurt books priced across different maturities. Example: 3-month funding into 5-year loans hurts when short rates jump and long rates stay flat. Fifth is price risk: selling a fixed-rate bond before maturity after yields rise locks in a capital loss. Sixth is reinvestment risk: uncertainty over the lower rate earned when maturing loans or bond coupons are reinvested. RBI adds net interest position risk: when earning assets exceed paying liabilities, falling rates cut NII. Chapter One check. Name the risk in three cases. First: borrowers prepay fixed loans after a 1% rate cut. Which risk? Embedded option risk — specifically behavioural option risk, exercised against the bank. Second: 3-month repo deposits fund 3-month T-Bill loans, moving unequally. Which risk? Basis risk, because similar-tenor instruments priced on different indices shift by different magnitudes. Third: a high-coupon bond matures in a rate cut, reinvested lower. Which risk? Reinvestment risk, because future cash flows face a lower reinvestment rate. Remember: same tenor, different benchmarks is basis risk; different tenors on a twisting curve is yield curve risk. Chapter One recall: which risk hits bonds sold early after a hike? Price risk, because bond prices move opposite to market yields. Now measure mismatch risk via Traditional Gap Analysis, RBI's operative earnings tool. An item is rate sensitive if it matures, pays principal, resets contractually, or can be withdrawn within the bucket. RBI's bucketing rule: slot every item by residual maturity or next repricing date, whichever is earlier. Under RBI's ALM Directions, banks use 11 IRS buckets: 1–28 days up to over 15 years, plus Non-sensitive. Cash, fixed assets, current deposits, capital, and reserves do not reprice, so they sit in Non-sensitive. Savings, current deposits, and mortgages need behavioural studies on ≥ 3 years data, reviewed yearly. Now break a classic myth: many of you think a positive gap always helps a bank. False. A positive gap helps only when rates rise; when rates fall, more assets reprice down, cutting NII. Second: if RSL exceeds RSA, is the bank asset-sensitive or liability-sensitive? Liability-sensitive — a negative gap whose NII falls when rates rise and rises when rates fall. Third: at what global share must a bank compute gap separately per currency? 5% or more of global assets or global liabilities; smaller currencies are aggregated as residual. One thing to remember: Gap is RSA minus RSL excluding non-sensitive items, and a positive gap gains only when rates rise. Chapter Two recall: negative gap plus falling rates — does NII rise or fall? NII rises, because more liabilities reprice down to cheaper rates than assets do. Turn the gap into rupees with our ruler: change in NII equals Gap times change in rate. Both Gap and rate change are positive, so NII rises by ₹4.50 crore. Your turn, Godavari Bank: RSA ₹4,100 crore, RSL ₹4,900 crore, rates rise 50 bps. Pause and find Gap and ΔNII. Fifteen seconds. Gap is −₹800 crore; −800 × 0.50% gives −₹4.00 crore, meaning NII drops ₹4 crore. Watch two classic slips: dropping the minus sign, or multiplying by 50 instead of 0.50%. Next is Net Interest Margin, or NIM, expressing NII as a percentage of the bank's asset base. RBI's Statistical Tables and IIBF's standard formula define NIM as NII divided by Average Total Assets, times 100. Denominator trap: international texts divide by Average Earning Assets, excluding cash and premises, giving a higher %. Use Average Total Assets unless the question specifies or gives only earning assets. Now add the favourite exam twist: reversing unrealised interest on an account turning NPA. Under IRACP Directions, accrual applies only to Standard loans; on NPA, reverse unrealised accrued interest. From 1 October 2026, accrued unrealised interest on acquired SNFAs also cannot be booked as income. Sum Three for Kaveri Bank: advances ₹6,000 crore yield 9.50%; investments ₹2,500 crore yield 7.20%. Second: when a Government-guaranteed loan turns NPA, may the bank continue booking accrued interest? No. NPA income recognition is strictly on cash basis even for Government-guaranteed accounts, and unrealised interest must be reversed. One thing to remember: reverse unrealised NPA interest before computing NII, and confirm whether NIM uses Average Total Assets.

```
Chapter Three recall: which denominator makes NIM higher — Total or Earning Assets? Average Earning Assets, because excluding non-earning assets shrinks the denominator and lifts the ratio. Traditional Gap Analysis is fast, yet it has six major blind spots that exams regularly test. First, it assumes every item inside a bucket reprices on the exact same day, ignoring intra-bucket timing. Second and third, it ignores basis risk and embedded options like loan prepayments or early deposit withdrawals. Fourth, it assumes a parallel shift across the yield curve, missing steepening or flattening twists. Fifth and sixth, it ignores time value of money on long-term equity value, and ignores non-interest income and expense shifts. That leads to the two complementary views of IRR required under RBI's ALM Directions. The earnings perspective tracks short-term profit — NII and NIM over one year — via Traditional Gap Analysis and Earnings at Risk. Earnings at Risk, or EaR, is the potential 1-year NII drop under adverse rate scenarios, capped by Board-approved limits. The economic value perspective tracks long-term Market Value of Equity across all cash flows via Duration Gap Analysis. Operative ALM Directions require TGA and DGA; a 200 bps shock cutting banking-book MVE over 20% flags an outlier. RBI's six-shock IRRBB (15% Tier 1 outlier) sits in Forthcoming Instructions — issued, coming. Three-rung ladder: Gap for short-term earnings, Duration for economic value, Simulation at the top. Static simulation tests today's existing balance sheet; dynamic simulation adds future rate paths, curve twists, and projected new business. Pause check for Chapter Four. Two quick questions. First: does Traditional Gap Analysis capture embedded loan prepayment risk and non-parallel yield curve shifts? No. Gap analysis ignores options, basis risk, intra-bucket timing, and non-parallel shifts; simulation models capture them. Second: which view focuses on long-term Market Value of Equity — earnings perspective or economic value perspective? The economic value perspective, measured under RBI's operative framework by Duration Gap Analysis. One thing to remember: Gap handles short-term NII and EaR, Duration handles long-term MVE, and Simulation handles twists and options. Mixed check: a bank runs a negative gap, and short deposit rates jump more than loan benchmarks. Which two risks hit? Gap risk from the negative gap cuts NII, and basis risk squeezes the spread even further. Look at the blank mind map for Interest Rate Risk. Name all five nodes we added today from memory. Sources, Gap, NII, NIM, and EaR — sitting cleanly on branch eight of our balance sheet map. You can now spot six IRR sources, compute gap, ΔNII, NIM with NPA reversals, and gap limits. Homework tonight: RSA is ₹3,200 crore, RSL is ₹4,000 crore, and rates fall 100 bps. Find Gap and ΔNII. Next class, we climb rung two: Duration Gap, change in equity value, and why EBLR resets faster than MCLR. [warmly] That is interest rate risk mapped from sources to margin. Rest well tonight, and I will meet you in the next class.
```
