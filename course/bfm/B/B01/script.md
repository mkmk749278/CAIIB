## Chapter 1 · What risk is, and why capital

N017 · Chapter one. What risk is. Let me ask you first. Is risk the same as loss?
> 1 S: chapter title "1 · What risk is" · 2 S: two tiles "risk" "loss" with a "?" between · H: 3 s

N018 · No. Risk is the uncertainty. The chance that what actually happens is different from what you expected. Mostly, the chance of a loss.
> 1 B: "≠" draws between the tiles · 2 N: "risk = uncertainty of outcome" [F1] · 3 B: underline "different from what you expected"

N019 · And a bank can't avoid it. Every loan is a bet that the borrower will pay. Banks earn by taking risk. So the job is to manage it, not remove it.
> 1 S: a loan card "a bet that he pays" · 2 N: "managed, not removed" [F1, F7]

N020 · R-B-I says banks face financial and non-financial risks. Roughly, the financial ones are credit, interest rate, foreign exchange, liquidity, equity and commodity prices.
> 1 S: two boxes draw: "financial" · "non-financial" [F2] · 2 S: the financial box fills as each is named

N021 · And non-financial risks, like legal, regulatory, reputational and operational. And R-B-I adds: they're linked. One event can set off others.
> 1 S: the non-financial box fills · 2 S: arrows draw between boxes, "linked" [F2]

N022 · Now, losses come in two kinds. Every year a bank expects some loans to go bad. On average, about the same each year. That's the expected loss.
> 1 S: a bar chart of yearly losses draws; a dashed average line across it · 2 N: "expected loss (EL) = the average" [F3]

N023 · The expected loss is a cost of doing business. The bank covers it in two ways. It prices it into the loan rate, and it makes provisions.
> 1 S: two arrows from the dashed line: "pricing" · "provisions" [F3] · 2 B: box around "cost of doing business"

N024 · Then some years there's a spike. A loss far above the average, nobody knows when or how big. That's the unexpected loss.
> 1 S: one bar shoots up past the line · 2 N: "unexpected loss (UL) = the spike" [F3]

N025 · What covers the spike? Have a guess. Provisions, or capital?
> 1 C: pause card "the spike: provisions or capital?" · H: 3 s

N026 · Capital. Provisions are for the average. Capital is the cushion that absorbs the surprise.
> 1 S: a cushion labelled "capital" slides under the spike [F3] · 2 B: circle "capital" on the word

N027 · Once more, slower. Expected loss… pricing and provisions. Unexpected loss… capital.
> 1 G: the dashed line, then the spike, on their words

N028 · And as a question back to you. A sudden, huge fraud loss, far above normal. Which does it eat into?
> 1 S: a small "fraud ₹ crores" card hits the spike · H: 3 s

N029 · Capital. It's unexpected. So now you see the triangle. Risk, capital, return.
> 1 S: the triangle draws live on the front wall of the house: corners "risk" "capital" "return"

N030 · More risk needs more capital to absorb the surprises. And that capital must be paid for, so the deal must earn more return. All three move together.
> 1 S: arrow risk → capital, label "needs" · 2 S: arrow capital → return, label "must be paid for" [F4] · 3 B: underline "move together"

N031 · Banks even measure this. It's called RAROC, risk-adjusted return on capital. It compares businesses with different risk on one scale. We'll do it properly in Module D.
> 1 S: a small tag on the triangle "RAROC · return per unit of capital at risk" [F5] · 2 N: "sums: D07"

N032 · So why manage risk at all? Write these four in your notebook. Protect depositors and capital. Keep earnings steady. Price each deal right. Meet R-B-I's rules.
> 1 S: the margin notebook gets four lines as each is spoken [F7] · H: 5 s

N033 · And R-B-I's four verbs for it: identify, measure, monitor and control. We'll build that framework in the next class.
> 1 S: four verbs write on the house's door frame [F6] · 2 N: "framework: B02"

N034 · Pause check for chapter one. Two questions. First. The average yearly loss on a loan book. Expected or unexpected loss? And who covers it?

## Chapter 2 · The credit door

N038 · Chapter two. The first door of the house, credit. Before we start. What does capital cover?
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · The credit door"; the house with five doors drawn, one lit · H: 3 s

N039 · The unexpected loss. Good. Now, here's a question for the credit door. Can a bank lose money on a loan or bond when nobody defaults?
> 1 S: the bond from the morning story, small · H: 3 s

N040 · Most of you will say no. No default, no credit loss. Keep that answer. We'll test it in a minute.
> 1 N: "no default → no credit loss?" · 2 S: the bond moves to the margin with a "?" tag

N041 · Credit risk, in R-B-I's words, is the inability or unwillingness of a customer or counterparty to meet commitments. Counterparty just means the other side of a deal.
> 1 C: rule card "credit risk = inability or unwillingness to pay" [F9] · 2 B: underline "or unwillingness" · 3 N: "counterparty = the other side of a deal"

N042 · Notice the two words. Inability, he can't pay. Unwillingness, he won't. Both are credit risk.
> 1 S: two faces "can't" "won't" under the card

N043 · And risk is spotted at three levels. The single deal, the whole portfolio, and the bank as a whole. On one loan, R-B-I calls it transaction risk, or default risk.
> 1 S: three rings "deal · portfolio · whole bank" [F8] · 2 S: one loan card "transaction / default risk" [F9]

N044 · Across the loan book, it's portfolio risk, with two parts. Intrinsic risk, built into the borrowers and their industries. And concentration risk. Too many loans to one group or one sector. If that sector falls, they fall together.
> 1 S: a stack of loans "portfolio risk: intrinsic · concentration" [F9] · 2 S: the stack tilts; one colour of cards dominates, "one sector"

N045 · Now, remember my question? Losing money without any default. R-B-I's two thousand two note defines credit risk as losses from a fall in credit quality. Actual, or even perceived.
> 1 S: the bond slides back from the margin [F10] · 2 C: card "RBI 2002: losses from diminution in credit quality, no default needed"

N046 · So yes. A borrower gets downgraded, its bond's price falls, and the bank loses, though every rupee of interest was paid. That's downgrade, or migration, risk.
> 1 S: a rating ladder; the borrower steps down a rung; the bond price falls · 2 N: "downgrade (migration) risk" · 3 B: circle "no default"

N047 · Next, counterparty risk. Basel defines it as the risk that the other party to a deal defaults before the deal is finally settled.
> 1 C: rule card "counterparty credit risk = defaults before final settlement" [F12]

N048 · Here's what makes it different. On a loan, only the bank can lose. On a deal like a forward contract, the value can swing either way. So either side could be the one that loses. Basel calls it bilateral.
> 1 S: a loan arrow, one way · 2 S: a forward contract with a two-way arrow, "bilateral" [F12] · 3 B: box around "either way"

N049 · And a close cousin, settlement risk. You pay away the currency you sold, but the currency you bought never arrives. You can lose the full value of the deal.
> 1 S: two currency arrows: "USD out ✓" and "EUR in ✗" [F13] · 2 N: "full value at risk"

N049a · And it's two risks at once. A credit risk, because the full value can be lost. And a liquidity risk, because cash you counted on today isn't there.
> 1 S: two tags on the arrows "credit: full value" · "liquidity: today's cash missing" [F13a]

N050 · It's also called Herstatt risk, after a German bank, Bankhaus Herstatt. It was closed in nineteen seventy-four, before it had paid its side of the day's deals.
> 1 S: a tag "Herstatt risk · 1974" [F13] · 2 B: underline "1974"

N051 · So how do you tell the two apart? Counterparty risk lives while the deal is still open, on its changing value. Settlement risk strikes on the payment day, on the full amount, after you have paid.
> 1 S: a timeline "deal open → payment day"; "counterparty" over the open part, "settlement" on the payment day [F12, F13]

N052 · Quick one. You've paid your dollars, and the other bank collapses before your euros arrive. Counterparty risk in general, or settlement risk?
> 1 S: the two arrows, small · H: 3 s

N052a · Settlement risk. The loss comes on the payment day, on the full amount. It's the Herstatt case.
> 1 S: answer "✓ settlement (Herstatt)"

N053 · Last on this door, country risk. Lending abroad, and the country can't repay on time. Inside it sits the core one, transfer risk.
> 1 C: rule card "country risk · core = transfer risk" [F14] · 2 S: a small globe on the credit door

N054 · Picture a borrower abroad. He has the money, in his own currency. But his government blocks payments out of the country. He's willing, but he can't send it.
> 1 S: a borrower with local notes; a wall at the border · 2 B: underline "willing, but can't send"

N055 · That's transfer risk. And lending to a foreign government itself is sovereign risk. A government may claim immunity from the courts.
> 1 N: "transfer = can pay locally, can't pay in FCY" [F14] · 2 S: "sovereign = lending to a government"

N055a · And if a government takes over the borrower's assets, nationalisation, that's political risk. R-B-I also names cross-border risk, a borrower living in a different country from where the loan is booked. And currency risk, exchange rates changing what you get back.
> 1 S: the country card adds "political (nationalisation) · cross-border · currency" [F14]

N056 · Pause check for chapter two. Four questions. First. A borrower's bond is downgraded and its price falls. No default. Is it still credit risk?
> 1 C: check card "Chapter 2 · 4 questions" · 2 S: Q1 · H: 4 s

N057 · Yes. Downgrade, or migration, risk. A loss from worse credit quality, without default.
> 1 S: answer "✓ credit · downgrade risk"

N058 · Second. A borrower in another country has the money, but can't remit it because of his government's rules. Which risk?
> 1 S: Q2 · H: 3 s

N059 · Transfer risk. The core of country risk.
> 1 S: answer "✓ transfer risk"

N060 · Third. In a forward deal, why is counterparty risk called bilateral?
> 1 S: Q3 · H: 4 s

N061 · Because the deal's value can move either way, so either party could end up the loser.
> 1 S: answer "✓ value can swing either way"

N061a · Fourth, a case. A loan in US dollars to a company abroad, at a floating rate. Name four risks in it.
> 1 S: Q4 card "USD loan · borrower abroad · floating rate" · H: 6 s

N061b · Credit risk, he may not pay. Interest rate risk, the rate floats. Foreign exchange risk, it's in dollars. And country risk, his country may block payments. One loan, many risks. That's R-B-I's point.
> 1 S: four tags land on the loan card "credit · interest rate · FX · country" [F8]

N062 · If you remember one thing from chapter two: credit risk is more than default. Downgrade, counterparty, settlement and country risk all live behind this door.

## Chapter 4 · The interest-rate door

N073f · Chapter four. The most asked definitions of the lesson are here, so stay sharp. Quick one first. A guarantee is invoked. Which liquidity risk?
> 1 S: chapter title "4 · The interest-rate door" · 2 N: "most asked: basis risk" · H: 3 s

N073g · Call risk. Now the interest-rate door. Interest rate risk is the effect of unexpected rate changes on the bank's net interest income, N-I-I, or on the market value of its equity.
> 1 S: the interest door lights · 2 C: rule card "IRR: unexpected rate changes → NII or market value of equity" [F18]

N073h · The market value of equity is what the bank is worth, assets minus liabilities, at today's rates.
> 1 N: "MVE = assets − liabilities, at today's rates"

N074 · R-B-I names seven types. You only need to tell them apart today. The measuring comes in Module D. Write each name as I say it.
> 1 S: seven empty rungs draw on the door · 2 N: "measure: D05"

N075 · First, one word. Reprice means the rate is reset, like a floating loan on its reset date, or a deposit renewed at a new rate.
> 1 N: "reprice = the rate is reset"

N076 · One. Gap, or mismatch, risk. Assets and liabilities of different amounts, or that mature or reprice on different dates. When the level of rates moves, the mismatch hurts.
> 1 S: rung 1 "gap / mismatch: different amounts or dates" [F19] · 2 B: underline "amounts or dates"

N077 · Two. Basis risk. Now, some of you think basis risk is just interest rate risk in general. Others think it has its own meaning. Decide.
> 1 S: cloud "Some of you": "basis = IRR in general" · 2 S: cloud "Others": "its own meaning" · H: 3 s

N078 · Its own meaning. Basis risk is when the rates of different instruments change by different amounts over the same time, even when they reset on the same day.
> 1 S: "Some of you" cloud crossed · 2 S: rung 2 "basis: different instruments' rates, different amounts" [F20] · 3 B: circle "different amounts"

N079 · Picture it. Your loans are linked to one benchmark, your deposits to another. Rates rise. The loan rate goes up half a percent, the deposit rate a full percent. Same day, different size. Your margin shrinks.
> 1 S: two arrows up, one short "loans +0.5%", one long "deposits +1%", "illustrative" · 2 N: "same day, different size"

N080 · R-B-I notes Indian loan books carry a lot of it, because a mixed pool of liabilities funds a mixed pool of loans. And it can work for you, or against you.
> 1 S: two pools "mixed liabilities → mixed loans" [F20] · 2 N: "can be favourable too"

N081 · Three. Embedded option risk. Customers have choices. A borrower prepays his loan when rates fall. A depositor breaks his fixed deposit when rates rise.
> 1 S: rung 3 "embedded option: prepay · break FD early" [F21]

N082 · Four. Yield curve risk. This one is about maturities. The bank prices loans and deposits off instruments of different maturities, and the curve doesn't move in parallel. Short rates and long rates drift apart.
> 1 S: rung 4 "yield curve: different maturities, non-parallel moves" [F22] · 2 S: a curve that twists · 3 B: underline "different maturities"

N083 · So four questions sort the first four. A mismatch of amounts or dates? Gap. Two different rates for the same time moving differently? Basis. Rates for different maturities moving differently? Yield curve. A customer's choice? Embedded option.
> 1 C: sorting card "mismatch → gap · two rates, same maturity → basis · different maturities → yield curve · customer's choice → option" · 2 G: rungs 1–4 in turn

N084 · Five. Price risk. You sell a bond before maturity, after rates have risen. Bond prices and yields move opposite, so you sell at a loss. R-B-I links it to the trading book, which we meet in chapter six.
> 1 S: rung 5 "price: sell before maturity" [F23] · 2 S: a see-saw "yield ↑ price ↓"

N085 · Careful, this price risk is narrow. It's only about selling a bond early. Market risk as a whole covers every market price: rates, currencies, shares and commodities.
> 1 B: box "price risk (IRR) = sell a bond early ≠ market risk = all market prices" [F16, F23]

N085a · Six. Reinvestment risk. Money comes back to you, and you don't know what rate you'll reinvest it at.
> 1 S: rung 6 "reinvestment: rate for money coming back" [F24]

N085b · Seven. Net interest position risk. Some money costs the bank nothing, like current-account balances. R-B-I calls it float. If a bank has more earning assets than paying liabilities, a fall in rates cuts its income, and a rise lifts it.
> 1 S: rung 7 "net interest position: earning assets > paying liabilities" [F25] · 2 N: "float = money that costs nothing"

N085c · R-B-I adds that a large float is a natural hedge against changes in interest rates. Now, without looking. Say the seven out loud.
> 1 N: "large float = natural hedge (RBI)" [F25] · 2 S: the seven rungs blur · H: 8 s

N085d · Gap. Basis. Embedded option. Yield curve. Price. Reinvestment. Net interest position. Write them in your notebook now.
> 1 S: rungs sharpen one by one as named; the margin notebook gets the seven · H: 10 s

N086 · Pause check for chapter four. Three questions. First. Loans and deposits of equal amounts both reprice on the first of April. But the deposit rate, on a different benchmark of the same maturity, rises more than the loan rate. Which risk?
> 1 C: check card "Chapter 4 · 3 questions" · 2 S: Q1 · H: 5 s

N087 · Basis risk. The amounts match and the dates match, so there's no gap. The two benchmark rates moved by different amounts. If you said gap, remember gap is a mismatch of amounts or dates.
> 1 S: answer "✓ basis" · 2 N: "same amounts, same date → not gap"

N088 · Second. Loans and deposits of equal amounts reset on the same day. Loans are priced off a one-year rate, deposits off a three-month rate. The three-month rate jumps, the one-year rate doesn't. Basis or yield curve?
