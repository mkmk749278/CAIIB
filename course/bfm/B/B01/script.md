## Chapter 1 · What risk is, and why capital

N017 · Chapter one. What risk is. Let me ask you first. Is risk the same as loss?
> 1 S: chapter title "1 · What risk is" · 2 S: two tiles "risk" "loss" with a "?" between · H: 3 s

N018 · No. Risk is the uncertainty. The chance that what actually happens is different from what you expected. Mostly, the chance of a loss.
> 1 B: "≠" draws between the tiles · 2 N: "risk = uncertainty of outcome" [F1] · 3 B: underline "different from what you expected"

N019 · And a bank can't avoid it. Every loan is a bet that the borrower will pay. Banks earn by taking risk. So the job is to manage it, not remove it.
> 1 S: a loan card "a bet that he pays" · 2 N: "managed, not removed" [F1, F7]

N020 · R-B-I puts banks' risks in two groups. Financial risks, like credit, interest rate, foreign exchange, liquidity, equity and commodity prices.
> 1 S: two boxes draw: "financial" · "non-financial" [F2] · 2 S: the financial box fills as each is named

N021 · And non-financial risks, like legal, regulatory, reputational and operational. And R-B-I adds: they're linked. One event can set off others.
> 1 S: the non-financial box fills · 2 S: arrows draw between boxes, "linked" [F2]

N022 · Now, losses come in two kinds. Every year a bank expects some loans to go bad. On average, about the same each year. That's the expected loss.
> 1 S: a bar chart of yearly losses draws; a dashed average line across it · 2 N: "expected loss (EL) = the average" [F3]

N023 · The expected loss is a cost of doing business. The bank covers it in two ways. It prices it into the loan rate, and it makes provisions.
> 1 S: two arrows from the dashed line: "pricing" · "provisions" [F3] · 2 B: box around "cost of doing business"

N024 · Then some years there's a spike. A loss far above the average, nobody knows when or how big. That's the unexpected loss.
> 1 S: one bar shoots up past the line · 2 N: "unexpected loss (UL) = the spike" [F3]

N025 · What covers the spike? Have a guess. Provisions, or capital?
> 1 C: pause card "the spike: provisions or capital?" · H: 3 s

N026 · Capital. Provisions are for the average. Capital is the cushion that absorbs the surprise.
> 1 S: a cushion labelled "capital" slides under the spike [F3] · 2 B: circle "capital" on the word

N027 · Once more, slower. Expected loss… pricing and provisions. Unexpected loss… capital.
> 1 G: the dashed line, then the spike, on their words

N028 · And as a question back to you. A sudden, huge fraud loss, far above normal. Which does it eat into?
> 1 S: a small "fraud ₹ crores" card hits the spike · H: 3 s

N029 · Capital. It's unexpected. So now you see the triangle. Risk, capital, return.
> 1 S: the triangle draws live on the front wall of the house: corners "risk" "capital" "return"

N030 · More risk needs more capital to absorb the surprises. And that capital must be paid for, so the deal must earn more return. All three move together.
> 1 S: arrow risk → capital, label "needs" · 2 S: arrow capital → return, label "must be paid for" [F4] · 3 B: underline "move together"

N031 · Banks even measure this. It's called RAROC, risk-adjusted return on capital. It compares businesses with different risk on one scale. We'll do it properly in Module D.
> 1 S: a small tag on the triangle "RAROC · return per unit of capital at risk" [F5] · 2 N: "sums: D07"

N032 · So why manage risk at all? Write these four in your notebook. Protect depositors and capital. Keep earnings steady. Price each deal right. Meet R-B-I's rules.
> 1 S: the margin notebook gets four lines as each is spoken [F7] · H: 5 s

N033 · And R-B-I's four verbs for it: identify, measure, monitor and control. We'll build that framework in the next class.
> 1 S: four verbs write on the house's door frame [F6] · 2 N: "framework: B02"

N034 · Pause check for chapter one. Two questions. First. The average yearly loss on a loan book. Expected or unexpected loss? And who covers it?
> 1 C: check card "Chapter 1 · 2 questions" · 2 S: Q1 · H: 4 s

N035 · Expected loss. Covered by pricing and provisions. Second. Is risk the same as loss?
> 1 S: answer "✓ EL · pricing + provisions" · 2 S: Q2 · H: 3 s

N036 · No. Risk is the uncertainty, the chance the outcome differs from what you expected. A loss is one result of it.
> 1 S: answer "✓ risk = uncertainty"

N037 · If you remember one thing from chapter one: provisions cover the expected loss, capital covers the unexpected one. Stretch for a second.
> 1 B: the cushion under the spike pulses; everything else dims · 2 M: lesson-map node 1 "risk and capital" · H: 1 s

## Chapter 2 · The credit door

N038 · Chapter two. The first door of the house, credit. Before we start. What does capital cover?
> 1 S: chapter title "2 · The credit door"; the house with four doors drawn, one lit · H: 3 s

N039 · The unexpected loss. Good. Now, here's a question for the credit door. Can a bank lose money on a loan or bond when nobody defaults?
> 1 S: the bond from the morning story, small · H: 3 s

N040 · Most of you will say no. No default, no credit loss. Keep that answer. We'll test it in a minute.
> 1 N: "no default → no credit loss?" · 2 S: the bond moves to the margin with a "?" tag

N041 · Credit risk, in R-B-I's words, is the inability or unwillingness of a customer or counterparty to meet commitments. In lending, trading, hedging, settlement and other transactions.
> 1 C: rule card "credit risk = inability or unwillingness to pay" [F9] · 2 B: underline "or unwillingness"

N042 · Notice the two words. Inability, he can't pay. Unwillingness, he won't. Both are credit risk.
> 1 S: two faces "can't" "won't" under the card

N043 · On one loan, it's called transaction risk, or default risk. Across the whole loan book, it's portfolio risk. And that has two parts, intrinsic risk and concentration risk.
> 1 S: one loan card "transaction / default risk" · 2 S: a stack of loans "portfolio risk: intrinsic · concentration" [F9]

N044 · Concentration is the easy one to picture. Too many loans to one group or one sector. If that sector falls, they fall together.
> 1 S: the stack tilts; one colour of cards dominates, "one sector"

N045 · Now, remember my question? Losing money without any default. R-B-I says credit losses can also come from a fall in value when credit quality worsens, actual or even perceived.
> 1 S: the bond slides back from the margin [F10] · 2 C: card "loss from worse credit quality, no default"

N046 · So yes. A borrower gets downgraded, its bond's price falls, and the bank loses, though every rupee of interest was paid. That's downgrade, or migration, risk.
> 1 S: a rating ladder; the borrower steps down a rung; the bond price falls · 2 N: "downgrade (migration) risk" · 3 B: circle "no default"

N047 · Next, counterparty risk. Basel defines it as the risk that the other party to a deal defaults before the deal is finally settled.
> 1 C: rule card "counterparty credit risk = defaults before final settlement" [F12]

N048 · Here's what makes it different. On a loan, only the bank can lose. On a deal like a forward contract, the value can swing either way. So either side could be the one that loses. Basel calls it bilateral.
> 1 S: a loan arrow, one way · 2 S: a forward contract with a two-way arrow, "bilateral" [F12] · 3 B: box around "either way"

N049 · And a close cousin, settlement risk. You pay away the currency you sold, but the currency you bought never arrives. You can lose the full value of the deal.
> 1 S: two currency arrows: "USD out ✓" and "EUR in ✗" [F13] · 2 N: "full value at risk"

N050 · It's also called Herstatt risk, after a German bank, Bankhaus Herstatt, that failed in nineteen seventy-four, in the middle of the day's payments.
> 1 S: a tag "Herstatt risk · 1974" [F13] · 2 B: underline "1974"

N051 · Quick one. You've paid your dollars, and the other bank collapses before your euros arrive. Counterparty risk in general, or settlement risk?
> 1 S: the two arrows, small · H: 3 s

N052 · Settlement risk. The loss comes at the moment of payment. It's the Herstatt case.
> 1 S: answer "✓ settlement (Herstatt)"

N053 · Last on this door, country risk. Lending abroad, and the country can't repay on time. Inside it sits the core one, transfer risk.
> 1 C: rule card "country risk · core = transfer risk" [F14] · 2 S: a small globe on the credit door

N054 · Picture a borrower abroad. He has the money, in his own currency. But his government blocks payments out of the country. He's willing, but he can't send it.
> 1 S: a borrower with local notes; a wall at the border · 2 B: underline "willing, but can't send"

N055 · That's transfer risk. And lending to a foreign government itself is sovereign risk. A government may claim immunity from the courts.
> 1 N: "transfer = can pay locally, can't pay in FCY" [F14] · 2 S: "sovereign = lending to a government"

N056 · Pause check for chapter two. Three questions. First. A borrower's bond is downgraded and its price falls. No default. Is it still credit risk?
> 1 C: check card "Chapter 2 · 3 questions" · 2 S: Q1 · H: 4 s

N057 · Yes. Downgrade, or migration, risk. A loss from worse credit quality, without default.
> 1 S: answer "✓ credit · downgrade risk"

N058 · Second. A borrower in another country has the money, but can't remit it because of his government's rules. Which risk?
> 1 S: Q2 · H: 3 s

N059 · Transfer risk. The core of country risk.
> 1 S: answer "✓ transfer risk"

N060 · Third. In a forward deal, why is counterparty risk called bilateral?
> 1 S: Q3 · H: 4 s

N061 · Because the deal's value can move either way, so either party could end up the loser.
> 1 S: answer "✓ value can swing either way"

N062 · If you remember one thing from chapter two: credit risk is more than default. Downgrade, counterparty, settlement and country risk all live behind this door.
> 1 B: the credit door pulses with its four tags · 2 M: lesson-map node 2 "credit door" · H: 1 s

## Chapter 3 · The market, liquidity and interest-rate doors

N063 · Chapter three. Three more doors. We're nearly halfway, and this chapter carries the most asked definition of the lesson, so stay sharp. First, from chapter two. What's Herstatt risk?
> 1 S: chapter title "3 · Market, liquidity, interest rate" · 2 N: "most asked: basis risk" · H: 3 s

N064 · Settlement risk. You pay, and the other side's money never arrives. Now, the market door. Basel defines market risk as losses from movements in market prices.
> 1 S: the market door lights · 2 C: rule card "market risk = losses from market price moves" [F16]

N065 · And it covers positions on and off the balance sheet. Which prices? Interest rates, exchange rates, equity prices and commodity prices.
> 1 S: four price tags hang on the door: "interest · FX · equity · commodity" [F16] · 2 B: underline "on and off"

N066 · Now the liquidity door. Quick check first. A bank that's profitable, with good loans. Can it still fail?
> 1 S: the liquidity door lights; a healthy profit chart · H: 3 s

N067 · Yes, if it can't pay out cash when it's due. R-B-I says liquidity risk comes from funding long-term assets with short-term liabilities. The deposits have to be rolled over.
> 1 C: rule card "liquidity risk: long assets, short liabilities → rollover" [F17] · 2 S: a long loan bar funded by short deposit bars

N068 · And R-B-I names three forms. These three get mixed up, so watch. Funding risk. Depositors withdraw, or don't renew. The bank must replace that money.
> 1 S: three slots draw: "funding · time · call" · 2 S: slot 1 fills "deposits leave → replace" [F17] · 3 B: box around "deposits"

N069 · Time risk. Money you expected to come in doesn't come. A good loan turns non-performing, and its repayments stop.
> 1 S: slot 2 fills "expected inflows don't come · NPA" · 2 B: box around "inflows"

N070 · Call risk. A contingent liability crystallises. A guarantee is invoked, and suddenly you must pay. It also means missing a good business chance because the cash isn't there.
> 1 S: slot 3 fills "guarantee invoked → must pay · missed chances" · 2 B: box around "contingent"

N071 · So the hook. Funding risk, the liability side. Time risk, the asset side. Call risk, the off-balance-sheet side. Say it with me. Funding, deposits. Time, loans. Call, guarantees.
> 1 G: slots 1, 2, 3 in turn, each with its side label "liabilities · assets · off-balance sheet"

N072 · As a question. A big corporate deposit is not renewed at maturity. Funding, time or call?
> 1 S: a deposit receipt "not renewed" · H: 3 s

N073 · Funding risk. Deposits leaving. Now the interest-rate door. Interest rate risk is the effect of unexpected rate changes on the bank's net interest income, N-I-I, or on the value of its equity.
> 1 G: slot 1 · 2 S: the interest door lights · 3 C: rule card "IRR: unexpected rate changes → NII or value of equity" [F18]

N074 · R-B-I names seven types. You only need to tell them apart today. The measuring comes in Module D. Write the seven as I go.
> 1 S: seven empty rungs draw on the door · 2 N: "measure: D05"

N075 · One. Gap, or mismatch, risk. Assets and liabilities reprice or mature on different dates. When the level of rates moves, the gap hurts.
> 1 S: rung 1 "gap / mismatch: different dates" [F19] · 2 B: underline "different dates"

N076 · Two. Basis risk. Now be careful here, this is where people slip. Basis risk is when different rates move by different amounts, even if they reprice on the same day.
> 1 S: rung 2 "basis: different rates, different amounts" [F20] · 2 B: circle "different amounts"

N077 · Picture it. Your loans are linked to one benchmark, your deposits to another. Rates rise. The loan rate goes up half a percent, the deposit rate goes up a full percent. Same day, different size. Your margin shrinks.
> 1 S: two arrows up, one short "loans +0.5%", one long "deposits +1%", "illustrative" · 2 N: "same day, different size"

N078 · R-B-I notes Indian loan books carry a lot of it, because a mixed pool of liabilities funds a mixed pool of loans. And it can work for you, or against you.
> 1 S: two pools "mixed liabilities → mixed loans" [F20] · 2 N: "can be favourable too"

N079 · So the key line. Gap risk is about when. Basis risk is about how much. Once more. Gap, different dates. Basis, different amounts.
> 1 B: box "gap = when · basis = how much" · 2 G: rungs 1 and 2 on their words

N080 · Three. Embedded option risk. Customers have choices. A borrower prepays his loan when rates fall. A depositor breaks his fixed deposit when rates rise.
> 1 S: rung 3 "embedded option: prepay · break FD early" [F21]

N081 · Four. Yield curve risk. Rates for short and long periods don't move in parallel. The curve twists, and income changes.
> 1 S: rung 4 "yield curve: non-parallel moves" [F22] · 2 S: a curve that twists

N082 · Five. Price risk. You sell a bond before maturity, after rates have risen. Bond prices and yields move opposite, so you sell at a loss. R-B-I links it to the trading book.
> 1 S: rung 5 "price: sell before maturity" [F23] · 2 S: a see-saw "yield ↑ price ↓"

N083 · Six. Reinvestment risk. Money comes back to you, and you don't know what rate you'll reinvest it at.
> 1 S: rung 6 "reinvestment: rate for money coming back" [F24]

N084 · Seven. Net interest position risk. If a bank has more earning assets than paying liabilities, a fall in rates cuts its income. A large free float, like current accounts, is a natural hedge.
> 1 S: rung 7 "net interest position: earning assets > paying liabilities" [F25] · 2 N: "float = natural hedge"

N085 · Write the seven now. Gap. Basis. Embedded option. Yield curve. Price. Reinvestment. Net interest position.
> 1 S: the margin notebook gets the seven as each is spoken · H: 6 s

N086 · Pause check for chapter three. Three questions. First. A bank's loans and deposits both reprice on the first of April. But the deposit rate rises more than the loan rate. Which risk?
> 1 C: check card "Chapter 3 · 3 questions" · 2 S: Q1 · H: 4 s

N087 · Basis risk. Same date, so it isn't gap risk. Different amounts, so it's basis. If you said gap, look at the dates again.
> 1 S: answer "✓ basis" · 2 N: "same date → not gap"

N088 · Second. A good loan turns non-performing and its instalments stop. Funding, time or call risk?
> 1 S: Q2 · H: 3 s

N089 · Time risk. Expected inflows that don't come.
> 1 S: answer "✓ time risk"

N090 · Third. Rates fall, and many home-loan borrowers prepay. Which interest-rate risk?
> 1 S: Q3 · H: 3 s

N091 · Embedded option risk. The customer used his option to prepay.
> 1 S: answer "✓ embedded option"

N092 · If you remember one thing from chapter three: gap risk is about when, basis risk is about how much.
> 1 B: the "gap = when · basis = how much" box pulses · 2 M: lesson-map node 3 "market · liquidity · interest" · H: 1 s

> 1 S: a faded card "RBI 1999: whatever isn't market or credit" [F28] · 2 B: strike on the faded card

N101 · Now, the weather around the house. These risks don't come through one door. First, compliance risk. The risk of penalties from the regulator, a big financial loss or a bad name, because the bank broke laws, rules or codes.
> 1 S: weather icons begin round the house · 2 C: card "compliance risk = sanctions · loss · bad name, from not complying" [F29]

N102 · Reputational risk. Customers, investors, markets or the regulator start to see the bank badly. Then it loses business, and even its access to funding.
> 1 S: a cloud "reputational: negative perception → business and funding" [F30] · 2 B: underline "access to funding"

N103 · And reputation can pull in other risks. A run of withdrawals becomes liquidity risk. That's why it's watched under Pillar two, which you'll meet in B-zero-four b.
> 1 S: arrow from the cloud to the liquidity door [F30] · 2 N: "Pillar 2: B04b"

N104 · Strategic risk. Loss from wrong business decisions, or from carrying them out badly. Entering the wrong market. A product nobody wants.
> 1 S: a signpost pointing the wrong way, "strategic: wrong decisions" [F31]

N105 · Systemic risk. One bank's failure spreads to others, and the whole system shakes. Think of dominoes.
> 1 S: a row of small houses falling like dominoes, "systemic: contagion" [F32]

N106 · Model risk. Banks run on models, for ratings, prices and limits. A model can be wrong, used for the wrong job, or out of date. R-B-I has issued draft guidance on it.
> 1 S: a gear icon "model risk: wrong · misused · out of date" [F33] · 2 N: "RBI draft guidance, 2026"

N107 · And climate risk, in two kinds. Physical risk, from floods, heatwaves and storms, or slow changes like rising seas. Transition risk, from the move to a low-carbon economy.
> 1 S: rain and sun over the house: "physical: acute · chronic" [F34] · 2 S: a factory chimney with a "policy change" tag: "transition"

N108 · Transition means new climate rules, new technology and customers changing what they buy. These are from R-B-I's draft disclosure framework. And climate risk hits through the old doors. A flooded borrower is a credit loss.
> 1 S: three tags under "transition": "rules · technology · customer choices" · 2 S: an arrow from the rain to the credit door [F34]

N111 · The cash shortage: operational risk, a failed process or a person. The story online: reputational risk. And if depositors start withdrawing, liquidity risk, funding risk, follows.
